Every German noun comes with a little label: {der}, {die} or {das}. English lost its genders a thousand years ago; German kept all three. That is the bad news. The good news is that the labels follow patterns more often than textbooks admit, that the colours in this course will help you see them, and that this lesson is about a topic people love to talk about: family.

If you learnt German before, you probably remember the advice "learn every noun with its article". It was good advice, and this lesson shows you how to make it stick.

## Die Familie

```words
die Familie, -n | family
der Vater, ¨ | father
die Mutter, ¨ | mother
die Eltern (Pl.) | parents
der Sohn, ¨-e | son
die Tochter, ¨ | daughter
das Kind, -er | child
der Bruder, ¨ | brother
die Schwester, -n | sister
die Geschwister (Pl.) | brothers and sisters, siblings
der Großvater, ¨ | grandfather
die Großmutter, ¨ | grandmother
die Großeltern (Pl.) | grandparents
der Opa, -s | grandpa
die Oma, -s | grandma
der Onkel, - | uncle
die Tante, -n | aunt
der Mann, ¨-er | man; husband
die Frau, -en | woman; wife; Mrs
der Partner, - | partner (male)
die Partnerin, -nen | partner (female)
```

A few things worth noticing straight away:

- **{Mann}** and **{Frau}** do double duty. {mein Mann} is "my husband", {meine Frau} "my wife". {Frau Becker} is "Mrs Becker" (or "Ms", German makes no difference).
- **{Geschwister}** has no English equivalent: "brothers and sisters" in one word. {Hast du Geschwister?} is the standard question.
- Many words for women are made from the word for men with **-in**: {der Partner} → {die Partnerin}, {der Lehrer} → {die Lehrerin}. They are always {die}.

## Three genders

Here is the whole idea in one table:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "the" | {der Vater} | {die Mutter} | {das Kind} | {die Eltern} |
| "a" | {ein Vater} | {eine Mutter} | {ein Kind} | (no word) |

German gender is **grammatical**, not biological. It is a property of the word, not of the thing. {Der Tisch} (the table) is not male, {die Lampe} is not female, and {das Mädchen} (the girl) is neuter because every word ending in {-chen} is neuter, whatever it describes. Mark Twain complained about this in 1880 and nobody listened.

:::key
Learn every noun with its article, as one unit: not "Tisch, table" but "{der Tisch}, table". Say it aloud with the article. The word lists in this course always give the article, and in the text nouns are coloured: **blue for der**, **red for die**, **green for das**, purple for plural-only words. You can switch the colours off with the **Gender** button at the top.
:::

The good news is that the endings of many nouns tell you the gender. Two you can trust completely: **-ung** is always {die} ({die Zeitung}, {die Wohnung}) and **-chen** is always {das} ({das Mädchen}, {das Brötchen}). And about nine nouns in ten that end in **-e** are {die} ({die Tante}, {die Lampe}, {die Straße}).

::gender-rules

```words
das Mädchen, - | girl
der Junge, -n | boy
das Baby, -s | baby
der Hund, -e | dog
die Katze, -n | cat
das Haus, ¨-er | house
die Wohnung, -en | flat, apartment
der Tisch, -e | table
die Lampe, -n | lamp
das Foto, -s | photo
```


:::mistake
{Der Junge} ends in -e but is masculine: he is one of a small, famous group of male nouns ending in -e ({der Junge}, {der Name}, {der Kollege}). They behave unusually in other ways too, which you will meet at B1.
:::

## A, an, no: ein, eine, kein

"A" is {ein} for masculine and neuter nouns and {eine} for feminine nouns. There is no plural "a", just as in English ("I have brothers").

"No", "not a", "not any" is {kein}, and it works exactly like {ein}: {kein Hund}, {keine Katze}, {kein Kind}, and in the plural {keine Kinder}.

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a | {ein Bruder} | {eine Schwester} | {ein Kind} | {Kinder} |
| no | {kein Bruder} | {keine Schwester} | {kein Kind} | {keine Kinder} |

```fill
title: ein or eine?
items:
  - de: Das ist ___ Foto von meiner Familie.
    options: [ein, eine]
    answer: 0
    en: This is a photo of my family.
  - de: Das ist ___ Katze.
    options: [eine, ein]
    answer: 0
    en: That's a cat.
  - de: Lena ist ___ Mädchen.
    options: [ein, eine]
    answer: 0
    explain: "{das Mädchen} is neuter, so {ein}."
  - de: Ist das ___ Hund?
    options: [ein, eine]
    answer: 0
  - de: Nein, das ist ___ Hund. Das ist eine Katze!
    options: [kein, keine, nicht]
    answer: 0
    en: No, that's not a dog. That's a cat!
    explain: '"Not a" with a noun is {kein}, not {nicht ein}.'
```

## Plurals: the one thing to memorise

English has one plural ending, -s, and a few oddities (children, mice). German has five common patterns, and the plural article is always {die}, whatever the gender in the singular:

| Pattern | Example | Typical for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **-e** (often with ¨) | {der Sohn} → {die Söhne} | many masculine nouns |
| **-er** (with ¨ if possible) | {das Kind} → {die Kinder}, {der Mann} → {die Männer} | many short neuter nouns |
| **-(e)n** | {die Schwester} → {die Schwestern}, {die Frau} → {die Frauen} | almost all feminine nouns |
| **–** (no ending, sometimes ¨) | {der Onkel} → {die Onkel}, {der Vater} → {die Väter} | nouns ending in -er, -el, -en |
| **-s** | {das Foto} → {die Fotos}, {die Oma} → {die Omas} | words ending in a vowel, foreign words |

The notation in the word lists shows this compactly: **{die Stadt}, ¨-e** means "umlaut plus -e": {die Städte}. **{der Onkel}, -** means the plural looks just like the singular.

:::tip
Feminine nouns are the easy ones: nearly all of them add **-n** or **-en**. If you are unsure about a feminine plural, -(e)n is a very safe bet.
:::


## haben: to have

{haben} is irregular in two places: **du hast** and **er/sie/es hat** (the b disappears). Everything else is regular.

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich habe} | I have | {wir haben} | we have |
| {du hast} | you have | {ihr habt} | you (all) have |
| {er/sie/es hat} | he/she/it has | {sie/Sie haben} | they have / you have (formal) |

```conjugate
title: haben
items:
  - verb: haben
    tense: present
```

Now you can talk about your family. With feminine, neuter and plural nouns, nothing changes after {haben}:

- {Ich habe eine Schwester.} I have a sister.
- {Wir haben ein Kind.} We have a child.
- {Hast du Geschwister?} Do you have brothers and sisters?
- {Ich habe keine Geschwister.} I don't have any brothers or sisters.

With a **masculine** noun, {ein} becomes {einen}: {Ich habe einen Bruder.} That is the accusative case, the subject of the next lesson. For now, take {einen Bruder}, {einen Sohn} and {keinen Hund} as fixed phrases.

```words
haben (hat, hatte, hat gehabt) | to have
verheiratet | married
ledig | single, unmarried
geschieden | divorced
zusammen | together
nur | only
viele | many
```

```cloze
title: Talking about family
prompt: Type the missing form of haben, or the missing article.
items:
  - de: '[Hast](haben) du Geschwister?'
    en: Do you have brothers and sisters?
  - de: Ja, ich [habe](haben) [eine] Schwester und zwei Brüder.
    en: Yes, I have a sister and two brothers.
  - de: Paul und Mia [haben](haben) [ein] Kind, Emil.
    en: Paul and Mia have a child, Emil.
  - de: Meine Tante [hat](haben) [keine] Kinder.
    en: My aunt has no children.
  - de: '[Habt](haben) ihr einen Hund?'
    en: Do you (all) have a dog?
```

```dialogue
title: Hast du Geschwister?
Jonas: Hast du Geschwister, Aylin? | Do you have any brothers or sisters, Aylin?
Aylin: Ja, ich habe zwei Brüder und eine Schwester. | Yes, I have two brothers and a sister.
Jonas: Wow, vier Kinder! | Wow, four children!
Aylin: Ja, wir sind eine große Familie. Und du? | Yes, we're a big family. And you?
Jonas: Ich habe keine Geschwister. Aber ich habe einen Hund! | I don't have any brothers or sisters. But I have a dog!
Aylin: Und wie heißt der Hund? | And what's the dog called?
Jonas: Er heißt Bruno. | He's called Bruno.
```

Notice {er heißt Bruno}: the dog is {der Hund}, so German calls him **er**. A table ({der Tisch}) is also {er}, a lamp is {sie}, a photo is {es}. The pronoun follows the grammatical gender.

## Mine and yours: mein, dein, sein, ihr

The possessives work like {ein}: no ending before masculine and neuter nouns, **-e** before feminine and plural nouns.

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| my | {mein Vater} | {meine Mutter} | {mein Kind} | {meine Eltern} |
| your (du) | {dein Vater} | {deine Mutter} | {dein Kind} | {deine Eltern} |
| his | {sein Vater} | {seine Mutter} | {sein Kind} | {seine Eltern} |
| her | {ihr Vater} | {ihre Mutter} | {ihr Kind} | {ihre Eltern} |
| our | {unser Vater} | {unsere Mutter} | {unser Kind} | {unsere Eltern} |
| your (Sie) | {Ihr Vater} | {Ihre Mutter} | {Ihr Kind} | {Ihre Eltern} |

```words
mein | my
dein | your (to someone you call du)
sein | his; its
ihr | her; their
unser | our
euer | your (to several people you call ihr)
Ihr | your (formal, to someone you call Sie)
```

:::grammar[Whose gender?]
The ending agrees with the **thing owned**, not with the owner. "Her father" is {ihr Vater} (no -e: {Vater} is masculine), and "his mother" is {seine Mutter} (with -e: {Mutter} is feminine). Many learners get this backwards for years.
:::

```fill
title: Whose?
items:
  - de: Das ist Anna und ___ Bruder.
    options: [ihr, ihre, sein]
    answer: 0
    en: That's Anna and her brother.
    explain: "Anna's → {ihr}; {Bruder} is masculine → no ending."
  - de: Das ist Tom und ___ Schwester.
    options: [seine, sein, ihre]
    answer: 0
    en: That's Tom and his sister.
  - de: Wie heißt ___ Mutter, Jonas?
    options: [deine, dein, meine]
    answer: 0
    en: What's your mother called, Jonas?
  - de: Frau Becker, ist das ___ Sohn?
    options: [Ihr, dein, ihre]
    answer: 0
    en: Mrs Becker, is that your son?
    explain: "Formal {Sie} → {Ihr}, with a capital I."
  - de: Das sind ___ Großeltern, Oma Ruth und Opa Klaus.
    options: [meine, mein]
    answer: 0
    en: These are my grandparents, Grandma Ruth and Grandpa Klaus.
```

```read

  Hello! My name is Lena and I come from Graz.
  This here is my family. My father is called Thomas; he's a teacher.
  My mother is called Sabine. She's a doctor.
  I have a brother, Felix. He's 19.
  Felix has a girlfriend; she's called Mira.

questions:
  - claim: Lena hat zwei Brüder.

  - claim: Lenas Mutter ist Ärztin.
    answer: true
  - prompt: Who is Mira?
    options: [Felix's girlfriend, Lena's sister, the cat]

  - prompt: Where do Lena's grandparents live?
    options: [in Graz, in Wien, with Lena]
    answer: 0
  - claim: Lena hat eine Tante.

```

:::culture
Graz is Austria's second city. In Austria and southern Germany you will often hear {Oma} and {Opa}; in the north, too. The formal {Großmutter} and {Großvater} are for forms and speeches. Children in Austria may also say {Omi}.
:::

```scene
turns:

      - de: Wie heißen die Kinder?
```
