In lesson 1 you learnt {Ich heiße …} and {Wie heißt du?} as phrases. Now we take them apart. {heiße} and {heißt} are two forms of one verb, {heißen}, and German verbs change their ending for every person: I, you, he, we, they. English has almost forgotten how to do this (only "he lives" keeps an -s); German does it all the time.

That sounds like a lot to learn, but there is one pattern, and it covers hundreds of verbs. Learn it here and you can talk about yourself and ask about anybody. You will also meet German's three words for "you", and find out which one to use with whom.

## ich, du, Sie: who is who

```words
ich | I
du | you (to one person you know well)
er | he
sie | she; they
es | it
wir | we
ihr | you (to several people you know well); her; their
Sie | you (formal, to one person or several)
```

| | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st person | {ich}, I | {wir}, we |
| 2nd person, informal | {du}, you | {ihr}, you (all) |
| 3rd person | {er}, {sie}, {es}: he, she, it | {sie}, they |
| formal "you" | {Sie}, you | {Sie}, you |

German has three ways to say "you":

- **{du}** to one person you are on first-name terms with: family, friends, children, fellow students, and among young people generally.
- **{ihr}** to two or more of those people at once. It is the plural of {du}: {Kommt ihr?} "Are you (both, all) coming?"
- **{Sie}**, always with a capital S, to adults you do not know or address as {Herr} or {Frau}: in shops, offices, at the doctor's, with your landlord. It is the same for one person or several.

A good rule of thumb: if you would use someone's first name, it is {du}; if you would say {Herr Weber} or {Frau Weber}, it is {Sie}. When in doubt, use {Sie} and let the other person offer {du}, which they often do with {Wir können uns gern duzen.} ({duzen} is "to say du", {siezen} "to say Sie".)

:::culture
The line keeps moving. Many workplaces, especially young companies, use {du} from the first day, and so do some shops and brands in their adverts. In Austria and Switzerland the rules are much the same, and a saying among Alpine hikers holds that above a certain height, everyone is {du}.
:::

The small {sie} does double duty: "she" and "they". The verb ending tells you which: {sie wohnt} is "she lives", {sie wohnen} "they live". The formal {Sie} takes the same form as "they": {Sie wohnen} is "you live". Out loud, the context makes it clear.

## sein: to be

```words
sein (ist, war, ist gewesen) | to be
Das ist … | This is …, That is …
hier | here
```

The most important verb in German is also the most irregular, just like "to be" in English:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich bin} | I am | {wir sind} | we are |
| {du bist} | you are | {ihr seid} | you (all) are |
| {er/sie/es ist} | he/she/it is | {sie/Sie sind} | they are / you are |

You use it, as in English, to say who and what people are: {Ich bin Lena.} {Das ist Jonas.} {Wir sind aus Wien.} {Herr Yilmaz ist hier.} Note the useful {Das ist …}, "this is" or "that is", for introducing people and pointing at things.

:::mistake
{ihr seid} (you are) is spelt with a d. {seit} with a t is a different word, "since". Native speakers mix them up too, so this is one spelling where you can do better than many Germans.
:::

## Regular verbs: one pattern for hundreds

```words
heißen (heißt, hieß, hat geheißen) | to be called
kommen (kommt, kam, ist gekommen) | to come
wohnen | to live (in a place)
lernen | to learn; to study
arbeiten | to work
spielen | to play
machen | to do; to make
```

Take the infinitive (the dictionary form), remove -en, and you have the **stem**: {wohn-}, {komm-}, {lern-}. Then add the ending for each person:

| | ending | {wohnen} | {arbeiten} | {heißen} |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich} | -e | {wohne} | {arbeite} | {heiße} |
| {du} | -st | {wohnst} | {arbeitest} | {heißt} |
| {er/sie/es} | -t | {wohnt} | {arbeitet} | {heißt} |
| {wir} | -en | {wohnen} | {arbeiten} | {heißen} |
| {ihr} | -t | {wohnt} | {arbeitet} | {heißt} |
| {sie/Sie} | -en | {wohnen} | {arbeiten} | {heißen} |

Three things to notice:

- **{wir}, {sie} and {Sie} always look like the infinitive.** The only exception in the whole language is {sein}: {wir sind}.
- **Stems ending in -t or -d add an e** before -st and -t, or you could not pronounce them: {du arbeitest}, {er arbeitet}, {ihr arbeitet}.
- **Stems ending in s, ß or z add only -t for du**: {du heißt}, not "heißst".

The word list gives extra forms for {heißen} and {kommen} because they are irregular in the past tense. In the present, which is all you need for now, they follow the pattern exactly.

:::grammar[One present tense, three English ones]
German has no "do" and no "-ing" form. {Ich wohne in Wien} is "I live in Vienna" and also "I am living in Vienna"; with a time word it can even mean the future: {Ich komme morgen} is "I'm coming tomorrow". And the questions need no "do": {Wohnst du hier?} is simply "Do you live here?"
:::

:::tip
In everyday speech you will hear {ich komm}, {ich wohn}, {ich lern}, with the -e swallowed. That is normal spoken German, but in writing keep the -e: {ich komme}.
:::

{wohnen} is where you live: your town, your street, your flat. {leben}, which you will also hear, is "to live" in the wider sense: to be alive, or to live a certain way. For "I live in Bern", say {Ich wohne in Bern.}

## Woher kommst du? Countries and languages

```words
aus | from; out of
in | in
Deutschland | Germany
Österreich | Austria
aus der Schweiz | from Switzerland
sprechen (spricht, sprach, hat gesprochen) | to speak
Deutsch | German (the language)
Englisch | English (the language)
die Sprache, -n | language
ein bisschen | a little, a bit
```

You come **{aus}** a country or town and live **{in}** one: {Ich komme aus Irland, aber ich wohne in Wien.} In speech, {Ich bin aus Irland} is just as common.

Most countries are used without an article, as in English. A few always have one: {die Schweiz}, {die Türkei}, {die USA}. With them you say {aus der Schweiz}, {in der Schweiz}, {aus der Türkei}, {aus den USA}. Learn these as fixed phrases for now; lessons 8 and 14 explain the {der} and {den}.

| Country | He is … | She is … | Language |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {Deutschland} | {Deutscher} | {Deutsche} | {Deutsch} |
| {Österreich} | {Österreicher} | {Österreicherin} | {Deutsch} |
| {die Schweiz} | {Schweizer} | {Schweizerin} | {Deutsch}, {Französisch}, {Italienisch}, {Rätoromanisch} |
| {Großbritannien} | {Brite} | {Britin} | {Englisch} |
| {Irland} | {Ire} | {Irin} | {Englisch}, {Irisch} |
| {die USA} | {Amerikaner} | {Amerikanerin} | {Englisch} |
| {Frankreich} | {Franzose} | {Französin} | {Französisch} |
| {Italien} | {Italiener} | {Italienerin} | {Italienisch} |
| {die Türkei} | {Türke} | {Türkin} | {Türkisch} |
| {Polen} | {Pole} | {Polin} | {Polnisch} |

Languages are nouns, so they take a capital: {Ich spreche Deutsch und Englisch.} Nationalities for women add **-in**: {Österreicherin}, {Britin}. The odd one out is German itself: a German man is {Deutscher} and a German woman {Deutsche}, because the word is really an adjective and changes its ending like one. You will see the pattern when you meet adjective endings at A2; for now, just learn the two.

:::culture
"High German" ({Hochdeutsch}) means standard German, the language of this course, not a posh variety: the "high" is geographical, from the southern highlands. Most German-speaking Swiss speak Swiss German dialect at home and write standard German, and Switzerland has four national languages. Austria has its own standard words, such as {Jänner} for January.
:::

**{sprechen}** is your first verb that changes its vowel: e becomes i, but **only** with {du} and {er/sie/es}. Everything else follows the regular pattern:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich spreche} | I speak | {wir sprechen} | we speak |
| {du sprichst} | you speak | {ihr sprecht} | you (all) speak |
| {er/sie/es spricht} | he/she/it speaks | {sie/Sie sprechen} | they speak / you speak |

You will meet a few dozen verbs like this. The change never touches {ich}, {wir}, {ihr} or {sie}: "ich spriche" and "ihr spricht" are classic slips.

## Was sind Sie von Beruf?

```words
der Beruf, -e | job, profession
Was sind Sie von Beruf? | What do you do (for a living)?
der Lehrer, - | teacher (man)
die Lehrerin, -nen | teacher (woman)
der Student, -en | student (man)
die Studentin, -nen | student (woman)
der Arzt, ¨-e | doctor (man)
die Ärztin, -nen | doctor (woman)
der Ingenieur, -e | engineer (man)
die Ingenieurin, -nen | engineer (woman)
studieren | to study (at university)
```

To ask what someone does, say {Was sind Sie von Beruf?} or {Was machen Sie beruflich?}; to a friend, simply {Was machst du?} The answer needs no "a":

- {Ich bin Lehrerin.} I'm a teacher.
- {Mehmet ist Arzt.} Mehmet is a doctor.
- {Ich arbeite als Ingenieurin.} I work as an engineer.

:::mistake
Not {Ich bin eine Lehrerin}. With jobs, nationalities and religions, German leaves the article out: {Ich bin Student}, {Sie ist Österreicherin}. You add it only when there is an adjective too ({Sie ist eine gute Lehrerin}), which comes later.
:::

As with nationalities, the word for a woman adds **-in**, sometimes with an umlaut: {Arzt} → {Ärztin}. Other jobs work the same way: {Kellner} / {Kellnerin} (waiter, waitress), {Verkäufer} / {Verkäuferin} (shop assistant), {Informatiker} / {Informatikerin} (IT specialist). In writing you will also see forms like {Studierende} (students) and Lehrer*innen, used to include everyone.

**{studieren}** is what you do at university: {Ich studiere Medizin.} **{lernen}** is learning anything else, including a language, and revising for a test: {Ich lerne Deutsch.} {Ich lerne für den Test.} "I study German at school" is {Ich lerne Deutsch}; "I study German at university" is {Ich studiere Germanistik}.

## Asking questions

```words
wer | who
was | what
wie | how
wo | where
woher | where from
```

German has two kinds of question, and the verb shows you which:

| | First | Second | The rest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| statement | {Ich} | {wohne} | {in Wien.} |
| W-question | {Wo} | {wohnst} | {du?} |
| yes/no question | {Wohnst} | {du} | {in Wien?} |

- In a **W-question**, the question word comes first and the verb second: {Woher kommen Sie?} {Was machst du?} {Wer ist das?}
- In a **yes/no question**, the verb comes first: {Sprechen Sie Englisch?} {Bist du Lehrerin?} You answer {ja} or {nein}.
- In a **statement**, the verb is second too: {Ich wohne in Wien.} This "verb second" rule is the backbone of German word order; lesson 6 shows how far it goes.

Your voice usually rises at the end of a yes/no question and falls at the end of a W-question, as in English.

:::mistake
**{wer}** means "who", not "where"; "where" is **{wo}**. This pair catches out learners for years, so say it to yourself: {wer} who, {wo} where. And "Where are you from?" stays together at the front: {Woher kommst du?} In speech you will also hear it split: {Wo kommst du her?}
:::
