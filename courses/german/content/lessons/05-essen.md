Food is the friendliest way into a language. You need it every day, the words are concrete, and a waiter in Vienna or Zurich will wait patiently while you find them. In this lesson you order in a café, read a menu, talk about what you like, and pay the bill, including the slightly odd German way of tipping.

On the grammar side, this is the lesson of the **accusative**: the case of the thing you order, eat, drink or have. If the word "case" brings back memories of four-by-four tables, relax. In the accusative, only one thing changes, and you will see it within five minutes.

## Essen und Trinken

```words
das Essen, - | food; meal
das Frühstück, -e | breakfast
das Mittagessen, - | lunch

das Brot, -e | bread

der Käse, - | cheese
die Wurst, ¨-e | sausage; cold meats
das Ei, -er | egg
das Fleisch (Sg.) | meat

das Gemüse, - | vegetables
das Obst (Sg.) | fruit
der Apfel, ¨ | apple
die Kartoffel, -n | potato
der Salat, -e | salad; lettuce
die Suppe, -n | soup
der Kuchen, - | cake
der Kaffee, -s | coffee
der Tee, -s | tea
das Wasser, - | water
die Milch (Sg.) | milk
der Saft, ¨-e | juice
das Bier, -e | beer
der Wein, -e | wine
```

A few things worth noticing:

- **{Gemüse}** and **{Obst}** are singular in German, though English says "vegetables": {Das Gemüse ist frisch.} Like English "fruit", each is one word for a whole category.
- German builds food words like Lego, and the **last** part decides the gender: {die Kartoffel} + {der Salat} = {der Kartoffelsalat}; {der Käse} + {das Brot} = {das Käsebrot}; {die Tomate} + {die Suppe} = {die Tomatensuppe}.
- Drinks: wine and spirits are masculine ({der Wein}, {der Sekt}, {der Schnaps}), but beer is {das Bier}. Germans will tell you this proves something about beer.

:::culture
A bread roll has a different name almost everywhere: {das Brötchen} in most of Germany, {die Semmel} in Bavaria and Austria, {die Schrippe} in Berlin, {das Weckle} in Swabia, {das Brötli} in Switzerland. Austria also has its own words for some vegetables: {die Erdäpfel} for potatoes and {die Paradeiser} for tomatoes. The standard words are understood everywhere; recognising the local ones is half the fun.
:::

## Im Café: Was möchten Sie?

In a café or restaurant you will be asked {Was möchten Sie?} (What would you like?) or {Was darf es sein?} (literally "What may it be?"). Here are your answers, all perfectly polite, from the most formal to the shortest.

| German | English | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {Ich hätte gern einen Kaffee.} | I'd like a coffee. | The classic polite phrase. Take it as a fixed phrase for now. |
| {Ich möchte einen Kaffee.} | I'd like a coffee. | Polite and neutral. |
| {Ich nehme einen Kaffee.} | I'll have a coffee. | When choosing from the menu. |
| {Für mich einen Kaffee, bitte.} | A coffee for me, please. | When a group is ordering. |
| {Einen Kaffee, bitte.} | A coffee, please. | Short and fine. |

```words
Ich hätte gern … | I'd like …
möchten | would like
bestellen | to order
die Speisekarte, -n | menu
der Kellner, - | waiter
```

```dialogue
title: Im Kaffeehaus in Wien
Kellner: Grüß Gott! Was möchten Sie? | Hello! What would you like?
Sophie: Ich hätte gern eine Melange, bitte. | I'd like a melange, please.
Kellner: Sehr gern. Möchten Sie auch etwas essen? | Certainly. Would you like something to eat as well?
Sophie: Ja, einen Apfelstrudel, bitte. | Yes, an apple strudel, please.
Kellner: Und für Sie? | And for you?
Luca: Für mich einen Tee mit Milch, bitte. | A tea with milk for me, please.
Kellner: Möchten Sie auch einen Strudel? | Would you like a strudel too?
Luca: Nein, danke. Ich habe keinen Hunger. | No, thank you. I'm not hungry.
```

:::culture
A {Melange} is the Viennese version of a cappuccino, and a Viennese coffee house serves every coffee with a small glass of water. Nobody will hurry you out: you can sit for hours over one cup and read the newspapers on their wooden holders. The waiter is traditionally addressed as {Herr Ober}.
:::

:::mistake
Two traps for English speakers. {Ich will einen Kaffee} is grammatical but sounds like a demand: "I want a coffee." And {bekommen} means "to get", not "to become". {Ich bekomme einen Kaffee} ("I'll get a coffee") is how many people in Germany order. You are not about to turn into a drink.
:::

{möchten} is the verb of polite wishes. It looks like an ordinary verb, but notice the **er/sie/es** form: it has no -t.

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich möchte} | I'd like | {wir möchten} | we'd like |
| {du möchtest} | you'd like | {ihr möchtet} | you'd like |
| {er/sie/es möchte} | he/she/it would like | {sie/Sie möchten} | they'd like / you'd like |

:::grammar[Why no -t?]
{möchte} is really a form of {mögen} (to like) meaning "would like". Like the English modal verbs ("she can", not "she cans"), it adds nothing in the he/she/it form: {sie möchte}. You can use it with a noun ({Ich möchte einen Tee}) or with a second verb, which goes to the very end: {Ich möchte bezahlen.} (I'd like to pay.) You will meet its whole family, the modal verbs, in lesson 9.
:::

## The accusative: den Kaffee, einen Tee

- {Der Kaffee ist heiß.} The coffee is hot.
- {Ich trinke den Kaffee.} I drink the coffee.

In the first, the coffee is the **subject**: the sentence is about it. In the second, *I* am the subject, and the coffee is the **direct object**, the thing the action is done to. German marks the difference on the article. The subject is in the **nominative** (the dictionary form); the direct object is in the **accusative**.

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative: the | {der Saft} | {die Suppe} | {das Bier} | {die Äpfel} |
| accusative: the | **{den Saft}** | {die Suppe} | {das Bier} | {die Äpfel} |
| nominative: a | {ein Saft} | {eine Suppe} | {ein Bier} | {Äpfel} |
| accusative: a | **{einen Saft}** | {eine Suppe} | {ein Bier} | {Äpfel} |
| accusative: no | **{keinen Saft}** | {keine Suppe} | {kein Bier} | {keine Äpfel} |

Only the masculine column changes. Feminine, neuter and plural look exactly as they did in the nominative.

:::key
In the accusative, a masculine article ends in **-n**: {den}, {einen}, {keinen}, and the possessives too: {meinen}, {deinen}, {Ihren}. Nothing else changes.
:::

Which nouns are accusative? The direct object of most verbs: {haben}, {nehmen}, {essen}, {trinken}, {möchten}, {mögen}, {bestellen}… That is why lesson 4 had {Ich habe einen Bruder}: the brother is what I have. And it is why you order {einen Kaffee} but {eine Melange}.

:::mistake
{sein} never takes an accusative. In {Das ist ein Apfel} or {Das ist der Kellner}, both sides are the same thing, so both are nominative. {Das ist einen Kaffee} is a very common slip.
:::

Because the article shows who does what, German can move things around without changing the meaning. {Den Fisch nimmt Ben, den Salat nehme ich.} means "Ben's having the fish, I'm having the salad": the {den} tells you the fish is being taken, not taking. Try it in the case machine.

## essen, nehmen and trinken

```words
essen (isst, aß, hat gegessen) | to eat
trinken (trinkt, trank, hat getrunken) | to drink
nehmen (nimmt, nahm, hat genommen) | to take; to have (when ordering)
```

{trinken} is completely regular in the present. {essen} and {nehmen} are vowel-change verbs, like {sprechen} in lesson 2: the **e** of the stem becomes **i** in the du and er/sie/es forms.

| | {essen} | {nehmen} | {trinken} |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ich | {esse} | {nehme} | {trinke} |
| du | {isst} | {nimmst} | {trinkst} |
| er/sie/es | {isst} | {nimmt} | {trinkt} |
| wir | {essen} | {nehmen} | {trinken} |
| ihr | {esst} | {nehmt} | {trinkt} |
| sie/Sie | {essen} | {nehmen} | {trinken} |

Two spelling details: the stem of {essen} already ends in s, so du adds only -t, and {du isst} looks just like {er isst}. And {nehmen} loses its h and doubles the m: {du nimmst}, {er nimmt}.

:::tip
The vowel change only ever affects **du** and **er/sie/es**. The plural forms keep the vowel of the infinitive: {wir nehmen}, {ihr esst}, {sie essen}.
:::

## I like it: mögen and gern

```words
mögen (mag, mochte, hat gemocht) | to like
gern | gladly; verb + gern = to like doing
kochen | to cook
lecker | tasty, delicious
```

{mögen} is irregular in the singular: {ich mag}, {du magst}, {er/sie/es mag}, then {wir mögen}, {ihr mögt}, {sie mögen}. Like {möchte}, the ich and er/sie/es forms are the same.

Use {mögen} with a **noun**: {Ich mag Käse.} {Magst du Fisch?}

To say you like **doing** something, German doesn't use a verb for "like" at all. It takes the verb and adds {gern} (gladly):

- {Ich koche gern.} I like cooking.
- {Ich trinke gern Tee.} I like (drinking) tea.
- {Ich esse nicht gern Fisch.} I don't like (eating) fish.

{gerne} is the same word with an extra -e; both are correct, and {gerne} is very common in speech.

:::mistake
{möchte} and {mag} come from the same verb but mean different things. {Ich möchte einen Kaffee} is a wish for now: "I'd like a coffee", bring me one. {Ich mag Kaffee} is a general taste: "I like coffee". Ordering with {Ich mag einen Kaffee} sounds to a German ear like a declaration of affection for one particular coffee.
:::

## nicht or kein?

```words
der Hunger (Sg.) | hunger
der Durst (Sg.) | thirst
```

German says "I have hunger": {Ich habe Hunger.} {Hast du Durst?} No article, just like {Ich habe Zeit} (I have time). Now make them negative, and you meet the question every learner of German asks: **nicht** or **kein**?

**{kein}** negates a noun that would otherwise have {ein} or no article at all. It takes the same endings as {ein}:

- {Das ist ein Apfel.} → {Das ist kein Apfel.}
- {Ich habe Hunger.} → {Ich habe keinen Hunger.}
- {Wir essen Fleisch.} → {Wir essen kein Fleisch.}

**{nicht}** negates everything else: a verb, an adjective, {gern}, or a noun with {der}, {die}, {das} or a possessive.

- {Ich koche nicht.} I don't cook.
- {Die Suppe ist nicht warm.} The soup isn't warm.
- {Ich nehme den Fisch nicht.} I'm not having the fish.
- {Das ist nicht mein Kaffee.} That's not my coffee.

:::mistake
English says "I'm not hungry" and "I don't have time", and returners often produce {Ich habe nicht Hunger}. German needs {kein}: {Ich habe keinen Hunger}, {Ich habe keine Zeit}. The test: could the noun stand with {ein} or with no article? Then the negative is {kein}. (Where {nicht} goes in longer sentences is a topic of its own, in lesson 7.)
:::

## Die Rechnung, bitte!

```words
die Rechnung, -en | bill
bezahlen | to pay
getrennt | separately
Stimmt so. | Keep the change.
Guten Appetit! | Enjoy your meal!
```

When the food arrives, people say {Guten Appetit!} (or {Mahlzeit!} at lunchtime at work), and with drinks {Prost!} or, more elegantly, {Zum Wohl!}. When you want to leave, call {Die Rechnung, bitte!}, {Zahlen, bitte!} or {Wir möchten bezahlen.}

:::culture
In German-speaking countries the waiter will not bring the bill until you ask: lingering is your right. You pay at the table, and you will be asked {Zusammen oder getrennt?} (together or separately?): splitting the bill item by item is completely normal. A tip of around 5–10 % is usual in Germany and Austria; in Switzerland service is included, and people simply round up. You don't leave the tip on the table. You say the total you want to pay as you hand over the money: if the bill is 17,60 € and you hand over a 50-euro note, say {Zwanzig, bitte} (or {Machen Sie zwanzig}), and you get 30 € back. If you hand over exactly what you want to pay, say {Stimmt so}.
:::
