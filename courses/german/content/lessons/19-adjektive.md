---
title: Describing people and things
goals:
  - describe what people look like and what they are like
  - understand the one principle behind adjective endings, so you can work them out instead of memorising tables
  - use the endings after der, after ein and mein, and with no article, in the nominative, accusative and dative
  - describe things you have lost, want to buy or want to sell

Adjective endings are where many people give up on German. You probably met them at school as three big tables ("weak", "strong", "mixed"), learnt them for a test, and lost them within a month. If that is you, good news: you do not need the tables. Behind them is **one idea**, and once you see it you can work out any ending in a second or two, and soon without thinking.

The idea is this: **the words in front of a noun must show its gender and case, once.** If the article already does it, the adjective can relax. If the article does not, the adjective has to do the job itself.

This lesson builds that up step by step, with people to describe (a mysterious new neighbour among them), things to find and buy, and a great deal of practice.

## Wie sieht er aus?

```dialogue
title: Am Bahnhof
Jonas: Kannst du morgen meinen Cousin vom Bahnhof abholen? Ich muss leider arbeiten. | Can you pick my cousin up from the station tomorrow? I have to work, unfortunately.
Lena: Klar. Aber ich kenne ihn gar nicht. Wie sieht er denn aus? | Sure. But I don't know him at all. What does he look like?
Jonas: Er ist ziemlich groß und schlank, und er hat kurze, blonde Haare. | He's quite tall and slim, and he's got short blond hair.
Lena: Hat er eine Brille? | Does he wear glasses?
Jonas: Ja, eine runde Brille. Und er trägt bestimmt seine grüne Jacke. Die trägt er immer. | Yes, round glasses. And he'll definitely be wearing his green jacket. He always wears it.
Lena: Und wie ist er so? Ist er nett? | And what's he like? Is he nice?
Jonas: Sehr nett! Am Anfang ein bisschen schüchtern, aber sehr lustig. | Very nice! A bit shy at first, but very funny.
Lena: Also: ein großer, blonder Mann mit einer runden Brille und einer grünen Jacke. Den finde ich! | So: a tall, blond man with round glasses and a green jacket. I'll find him!
```

Two questions to keep apart: **{Wie sieht er aus?}** asks about appearance ("What does he look like?"), **{Wie ist er?}** about character ("What is he like?").

```words
aus|sehen (sieht aus, sah aus, hat ausgesehen) | to look (appearance)
das Haar, -e | hair
blond | blond, fair-haired
dunkel | dark
hell | light, bright
lockig | curly
glatt | straight (hair); smooth; slippery
schlank | slim
dick | fat; thick
hübsch | pretty
die Brille, -n | (pair of) glasses
der Bart, ¨-e | beard
tragen (trägt, trug, hat getragen) | to wear; to carry
```

:::note
German usually talks about hair in the plural: {Sie hat lange Haare} (she has long hair). {Das Haar} in the singular is a single hair, or slightly literary: {Sie hat langes Haar} is also correct. And glasses are singular: {eine Brille}, one pair of glasses.
:::

After {sein}, an adjective has **no ending**, as you know from A1: {Er ist groß.} {Die Brille ist rund.} {Seine Haare sind kurz.} An ending appears only when the adjective stands **in front of a noun**: {ein großer Mann}, {eine runde Brille}, {kurze Haare}.

## The one principle

Look at these pairs and see where the -er and -es come from:

| with der, die, das | with ein, eine |
| --- | --- |
| {der alte Mann} | {ein alter Mann} |
| {das kleine Kind} | {ein kleines Kind} |
| {die nette Frau} | {eine nette Frau} |

In {der alte Mann}, the article {der} already says "masculine, nominative", so the adjective can take a plain **-e**. But {ein} could be masculine or neuter: it gives nothing away. So the adjective takes over the ending that {der} would have shown, **-er**: {ein alter Mann}. In the same way {das} → **-es**: {ein kleines Kind}. With {eine} the article already shows the feminine (that -e), so the adjective relaxes: {eine nette Frau}.

:::key
**If the article shows gender and case, the adjective ends in -e or -en. If the article does not show them, or there is no article, the adjective shows them itself, with the ending of {der}, {die} or {das}.**
:::

So the endings an adjective "takes over" are just the endings of the definite article, which you already know:

::article-table{kind="definite" cases="nom,acc,dat"}

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {der} → -er | {die} → -e | {das} → -es | {die} → -e |
| accusative | {den} → -en | {die} → -e | {das} → -es | {die} → -e |
| dative | {dem} → -em | {der} → -er | {dem} → -em | {den} → -en |

```sort

```

## Nach der, die, das: -e or -en

After {der}, {die}, {das} (and {dieser}, {welcher}, {jeder}, which end the same way), the article does the work in every case. The adjective only needs one of two endings, and there is a simple picture for which:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {der alte Mann} -e | {die nette Frau} -e | {das kleine Kind} -e | {die neuen Schuhe} -en |
| accusative | {den alten Mann} -en | {die nette Frau} -e | {das kleine Kind} -e | {die neuen Schuhe} -en |
| dative | {dem alten Mann} -en | {der netten Frau} -en | {dem kleinen Kind} -en | {den neuen Schuhen} -en |

**-e in five places, -en everywhere else.** The five -e are the places where the article is the "dictionary" form {der}, {die} or {das} (nominative and accusative singular). Every {den}, every {dem}, the dative {der} and the whole plural take -en.

::adjective-endings

```cloze
    en: I can't walk with this heavy suitcase.
```

## Nach ein, mein, kein: three gaps to fill

{Ein}, {kein} and the possessives ({mein}, {dein}, {sein}, {ihr}, {unser}…) have endings almost everywhere: {eine}, {einen}, {einem}, {einer}, {meine}, {meinen}… In those places they do the job, and the adjective takes -e or -en exactly as after {der}.

But in **three places** they have no ending at all: masculine nominative ({ein Mann}), neuter nominative and accusative ({ein Kind}). There the adjective fills the gap with the ending of {der} or {das}:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {ein alter Mann} **-er** | {eine nette Frau} -e | {ein kleines Kind} **-es** | {meine neuen Schuhe} -en |
| accusative | {einen alten Mann} -en | {eine nette Frau} -e | {ein kleines Kind} **-es** | {meine neuen Schuhe} -en |
| dative | {einem alten Mann} -en | {einer netten Frau} -en | {einem kleinen Kind} -en | {meinen neuen Schuhen} -en |

Compare it with the table before: it is the same table with three cells changed. That is all the "mixed declension" of the grammar books is.

:::mistake
The classic slip is {ein großes Mann}: -es is the neuter signal, and {Mann} is masculine. Ask yourself which article the noun has: {der Mann} → {ein großer Mann}; {das Haus} → {ein großes Haus}.

And after {sein}, there is never an ending: {Der Mann ist groß}, not "großer".
:::

```cloze

prompt: Type the adjective with its ending.
items:
  - de: Das ist ein [netter](nett) Kollege.
    en: That's a nice colleague.
    explain: "{ein} + masculine nominative: the adjective shows it, -er."
  - de: Sie hat eine [kleine](klein) Wohnung in Graz.
    en: She has a small flat in Graz.
  - de: Wir haben ein [großes](groß) Problem.
    en: We have a big problem.
  - de: Ich suche einen [warmen](warm) Mantel.
    en: I'm looking for a warm coat.
  - de: Er wohnt mit einem [lustigen](lustig) Hund zusammen.
    en: He lives with a funny dog.
  - de: Ich wohne in einer [ruhigen](ruhig) Straße.
    en: I live in a quiet street.
  - de: Mein [alter](alt) Lehrer wohnt noch in Linz.
    en: My old teacher still lives in Linz.
  - de: Das sind meine [besten](gut) Freunde.
    en: These are my best friends.
    explain: "Superlatives take the same endings: {meine} + plural → -en."
```

:::tip
Adjectives ending in **-el** and **-er** drop their e when an ending is added, just as in the comparative: {dunkel} → {eine dunkle Jacke}, {teuer} → {ein teures Auto}. And {hoch} loses its c: {der Berg ist hoch}, but {der hohe Berg}.
:::

## Ohne Artikel: the adjective does it alone

On menus, signs, in adverts and with plurals, there is often no article at all. Then nobody else can show gender and case, and the adjective takes the endings of {der}, {die}, {das} in every cell:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {frischer Fisch} | {frische Milch} | {kaltes Wasser} | {frische Brötchen} |
| accusative | {frischen Fisch} | {frische Milch} | {kaltes Wasser} | {frische Brötchen} |
| dative | {mit frischem Fisch} | {mit frischer Milch} | {mit kaltem Wasser} | {mit frischen Brötchen} |

You have known one of these for years without noticing: the end of every formal letter, **{Mit freundlichen Grüßen}**, is a dative plural with no article. And a postcard ends with **{Liebe Grüße}**, an accusative plural.

:::tip
After numbers and after {viele}, {einige} and {wenige}, the adjective behaves as if there were no article: {zwei große Zimmer}, {viele nette Leute}. After {alle} it behaves as after {die}: {alle netten Leute}.
:::
