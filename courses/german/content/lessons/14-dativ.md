Your mother has a birthday, your neighbour watered your plants, a colleague is leaving: what do you give them? Presents are the perfect way into the third German case, the **dative**, because every present has someone who receives it.

You have already met the dative in passing: {mit dem Bus} and {zum Bahnhof} in lesson 8, {Das gefällt mir} in lesson 10. Now it gets the full treatment. If you were taught that the dative is "the case of *to*", hold that thought loosely. It works for presents, but it will lead you astray elsewhere, and this lesson gives you a better way to think about it.

## Wem schenkst du das?

```dialogue
title: Mamas Geburtstag
Aylin: Mama hat am Samstag Geburtstag. Was schenken wir ihr? | Mum's birthday is on Saturday. What shall we give her?
Mehmet: Wir können ihr Blumen schenken. | We could give her flowers.
Aylin: Blumen? Die bekommt sie schon von Papa. | Flowers? She's already getting those from Dad.
Mehmet: Hm. Und ein Buch? Sie liest doch so gern. | Hm. What about a book? She does love reading.
Aylin: Ein Buch haben wir ihr letztes Jahr geschenkt. | We gave her a book last year.
Mehmet: Dann einen Gutschein für ein Konzert? | Then a voucher for a concert?
Aylin: Gute Idee! Den geben wir ihr mit einem Kuchen. | Good idea! We'll give it to her with a cake.
Mehmet: Und den Kuchen backst du! | And you're baking the cake!
```

In {Wir schenken ihr einen Gutschein} there are two objects. The thing that changes hands, {einen Gutschein}, is in the accusative, as you know from lesson 5. The person who receives it, {ihr} (her), is in the **dative**. The question word for the dative is {wem?}: {Wem schenkst du das?} (Who are you giving that to?)

Verbs of giving, showing and telling usually have both: {geben}, {schenken}, {zeigen}, {schicken}, {leihen}, {bringen}, {erklären}, {erzählen}, {empfehlen}, and {kaufen} when you buy something *for* someone.

:::grammar[Not "the case of to"]
Many learners were taught that the dative means "to". That works for {Ich gebe dem Kind ein Buch} (I give a book to the child), but it fails in both directions:

- Many datives have no "to" in English: {Ich helfe dir} (I help you), {Ich kaufe meiner Mutter Blumen} (I'm buying my mother flowers), {Mir ist kalt} (I'm cold).
- Many English "to"s are not dative: {Ich fahre in die Stadt} (I'm going to town) is accusative.

A better way to think of it: the dative marks the **person affected** by what happens, the one who receives, benefits, loses out or is spoken to. Besides that, it has two more jobs: it follows certain verbs, and certain prepositions. That is the whole case, and this lesson covers all three.
:::

```words
schenken | to give (as a present)
geben (gibt, gab, hat gegeben) | to give
zeigen | to show
schicken | to send
leihen (leiht, lieh, hat geliehen) | to lend
das Geschenk, -e | present, gift
der Gutschein, -e | voucher
die Blume, -n | flower
wem? | to whom? for whom?
```

## dem, der, dem, den: the dative articles

Here are the three cases you know side by side. The dative row is the new one:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {der/ein/mein Vater} | {die/eine/meine Mutter} | {das/ein/mein Kind} | {die/meine Kinder} |
| accusative | {den/einen/meinen Vater} | {die/eine/meine Mutter} | {das/ein/mein Kind} | {die/meine Kinder} |
| **dative** | {dem/einem/meinem Vater} | {der/einer/meiner Mutter} | {dem/einem/meinem Kind} | {den/meinen Kindern} |

Four things to notice:

- **Masculine and neuter are the same** in the dative: **-m** ({dem}, {einem}, {meinem}, {keinem}).
- **Feminine is -r**: {der}, {einer}, {meiner}. Yes, {der} again. In {Ich helfe der Frau}, {der} cannot be the masculine nominative, because {Frau} is feminine: it is the dative.
- **Plural is {den}**, and the noun itself adds **-n**: {den Kindern}, {meinen Freunden}. Plurals that already end in -n or -s add nothing: {meinen Eltern}, {den Omas}.
- The endings read **-m, -r, -m, -n**, and the masculine -m is everywhere: {dem}, {wem}, and as you will see, {ihm}.

::article-table{kind=definite cases=nom,acc,dat}

:::mistake
A few masculine nouns add -n in every case but the nominative, so they add it in the dative too: {dem Jungen}, {dem Kollegen}, {Herrn Becker}. You will meet this group properly at B1; for now, just don't be surprised by the -n.
:::

## mir, dir, ihm: the dative pronouns

| nominative | accusative | dative | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich} | {mich} | {mir} | (to/for) me |
| {du} | {dich} | {dir} | (to/for) you |
| {er} | {ihn} | {ihm} | (to/for) him, it |
| {sie} | {sie} | {ihr} | (to/for) her, it |
| {es} | {es} | {ihm} | (to/for) it |
| {wir} | {uns} | {uns} | (to/for) us |
| {ihr} | {euch} | {euch} | (to/for) you |
| {sie} | {sie} | {ihnen} | (to/for) them |
| {Sie} | {Sie} | {Ihnen} | (to/for) you, formal |

You know {mir}, {dir} and {Ihnen} from lesson 10. The rest fall into line: {ihm} has the masculine -m, {ihr} the feminine -r, {ihnen} the plural -n. {uns} and {euch} do not change at all.

{ihr} now has two jobs: "you (all)" as a subject ({Habt ihr Zeit?}) and "(to) her" in the dative ({Ich schenke ihr Blumen}). The verb tells you which: {ihr habt}, but {ich schenke ihr}.

```words
ihm | (to, for) him, it
ihnen | (to, for) them
```

## Two objects: which comes first?

When a sentence has a dative and an accusative, their order follows three rules:

| | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| two nouns | dative before accusative | {Ich gebe dem Kind den Ball.} |
| a pronoun and a noun | the pronoun first | {Ich gebe ihm den Ball.} {Ich gebe ihn dem Kind.} |
| two pronouns | accusative before dative | {Ich gebe ihn ihm.} |

In short: **pronouns before nouns**; between two nouns the dative goes first, between two pronouns the accusative goes first. The logic is that short, known information comes early and the new, important bit comes late. Aylin's {Den geben wir ihr} shows the other option: either object can also take position 1 for emphasis, and then the verb follows it.

## helfen, danken, gefallen: dative verbs

A handful of very common verbs take a dative where English has a plain object. Learn them as a group:

| Verb | Example | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {helfen} | {Kann ich Ihnen helfen?} | Can I help you? |
| {danken} | {Ich danke dir!} | Thank you! |
| {gratulieren} | {Wir gratulieren dir zum Geburtstag!} | Happy birthday from us! |
| {antworten} | {Warum antwortest du mir nicht?} | Why aren't you answering me? |
| {gehören} | {Gehört das Handy dir?} | Is this phone yours? |
| {gefallen} | {Das Geschenk gefällt ihr.} | She likes the present. |
| {schmecken} | {Schmeckt dir der Kuchen?} | Do you like the cake? |
| {passen} | {Die Jacke passt mir nicht.} | The jacket doesn't fit me. |

Why the dative? In each case the person is not acted upon but is the one who benefits or is affected: help and thanks go *to* someone, a present is pleasing *to* someone, a cake tastes good *to* someone. Older English worked the same way ("it pleases me", "methinks").

With {gefallen}, {schmecken}, {passen} and {gehören}, the thing is the subject and the verb agrees with it: {Die Schuhe gefallen mir} (the shoes please me), with a plural verb because of {Schuhe}.

:::mistake
{Ich helfe dich} is one of the commonest mistakes there is: {helfen} always takes the dative, {Ich helfe dir}. The reverse trap: {besuchen}, {fragen} and {anrufen} take the accusative: {Ich besuche dich}, {Ich frage dich}, {Ich rufe dich an}. (In Switzerland you will also hear {Ich rufe dir an}.)
:::

```words
gratulieren (+ D) | to congratulate
antworten (+ D) | to answer, to reply to
gehören (+ D) | to belong to
schmecken (+ D) | to taste (good) to
```

## aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu

Eight prepositions **always** take the dative, whatever the sentence is doing:

| Preposition | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {aus} | out of; from (origin) | {Sie kommt aus der Schweiz.} {Er nimmt das Handy aus der Tasche.} |
| {bei} | at someone's home or work; near | {Ich wohne bei meinen Eltern.} {Sie arbeitet bei einer Bank.} {beim Arzt} |
| {mit} | with; by (transport) | {mit meiner Schwester}, {mit dem Zug} |
| {nach} | to (towns, most countries); after | {nach Wien}, {nach Hause}, {nach dem Essen} |
| {seit} | since; for (until now) | {seit Montag}, {seit einem Jahr} |
| {von} | from; of; by | {ein Geschenk von meiner Oma}, {von der Arbeit} |
| {zu} | to (people, places) | {zu meiner Freundin}, {zum Bahnhof} |
| {gegenüber} | opposite | {gegenüber dem Bahnhof}, {mir gegenüber} |

Four of them usually merge with {dem} or {der}: **{zum}** (zu dem), **{zur}** (zu der), **{beim}** (bei dem), **{vom}** (von dem). Only {zur} is feminine: {zur Party}, {zur Arbeit}, but {zum Arzt}, {zum Bahnhof}.

:::fun
Generations of English-speaking pupils have learnt these prepositions by singing **aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu** to the opening of Strauss's *Blue Danube* waltz. Try it, and you may never forget them.
:::

:::mistake
"I have been learning German for three years" is {Ich lerne seit drei Jahren Deutsch}: **{seit}** with the **present tense**, because you are still learning. Not {für drei Jahre}, and not the perfect. Notice the dative plural: {seit drei Jahren}.
:::

{gegenüber} can stand after a pronoun ({Sie wohnt mir gegenüber}: she lives opposite me). With places, people often say {gegenüber vom Bahnhof} in conversation.

```words
aus (+ D) | out of; from
bei (+ D) | at (someone's); near
nach (+ D) | to (a town or country); after
seit (+ D) | since; for
von (+ D) | from; of; by
gegenüber (+ D) | opposite
```
