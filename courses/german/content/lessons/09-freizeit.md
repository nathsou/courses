What do you do when you are not working? German has a word for that time, {die Freizeit}, and German speakers take it seriously. Germany alone has more than half a million registered clubs ({Vereine}), for everything from football and choir singing to beekeeping.

To talk about free time you need a handful of small, hard-working verbs: *can*, *want*, *must*, *may*, *should*. These are the **modal verbs**, and they bring with them one of the big ideas of German word order: the verb that carries the meaning goes to the **end** of the sentence. Once you have seen it here, you will recognise it everywhere: in the perfect tense (lesson 11), in clauses with {weil} and {dass} (A2), and in every German newspaper.

If you are coming back to German, one warning before we start: {Ich will} does not mean "I will". It means "I want". More on that below.

## Was machst du in deiner Freizeit?

{Was machst du in deiner Freizeit?} (What do you do in your free time?) is the standard question. Take it as a fixed phrase for now.


Four verbs do most of the work when you talk about hobbies:

- **{spielen}** for games, sports and instruments: {Ich spiele Fußball.} {Ich spiele Klavier.} Notice: no article. English plays *the* piano; German just plays {Klavier}.
- **{machen}** for activity in general: {Ich mache Sport.} {Ich mache Yoga.}
- **{gehen}** plus a second verb, for things you go out to do: {Ich gehe schwimmen.} {Wir gehen tanzen.} {Gehst du heute wandern?} And with a place: {Ich gehe ins Kino.} {Ich gehe ins Fitnessstudio.}
- **{fahren}** for anything with wheels: {Ich fahre Fahrrad.} In Switzerland the bike is {das Velo}: {Ich fahre Velo.}

## Gern, lieber, am liebsten

To say you like *doing* something, German does not use a verb meaning "like". It adds a little word to the verb that is already there: **{gern}**, "gladly".

- {Ich schwimme gern.} I like swimming. (literally: I swim gladly)
- {Ich koche nicht gern.} I don't like cooking.
- {Ich spiele gern Tennis, aber ich spiele lieber Fußball.} I like playing tennis, but I prefer playing football.
- {Am liebsten lese ich.} Most of all I like reading. / Reading is my favourite thing.

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {gern} | like doing | {Ich tanze gern.} |
| {lieber} | prefer doing | {Ich singe lieber.} |
| {am liebsten} | like doing best | {Am liebsten spiele ich Klavier.} |

:::mistake
In lesson 5 you met {mögen}: {Ich mag Musik.} That is for **things**. For **activities**, use the verb + {gern}: {Ich höre gern Musik}, not "Ich mag Musik hören". You will hear {Ich mag schwimmen} from some speakers, but {Ich schwimme gern} is what sounds natural.
:::

{lieber} and {am liebsten} also work with food and drink: {Ich trinke gern Kaffee, aber lieber Tee.} And on its own, {Gern!} is the friendliest way to say "I'd love to!"

```words
gern (lieber, am liebsten) | gladly; with a verb: like doing
```

## Ich kann schwimmen: the sentence bracket

Here are the modal verbs. Learn their principal parts later; for now, the present tense is what matters.

```words
können (kann, konnte, hat gekonnt) | can, to be able to
wollen (will, wollte, hat gewollt) | to want (to)
müssen (muss, musste, hat gemusst) | must, to have to
dürfen (darf, durfte, hat gedurft) | may, to be allowed to
sollen (soll, sollte, hat gesollt) | to be supposed to, should
```

Start with {können}:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich kann} | I can | {wir können} | we can |
| {du kannst} | you can | {ihr könnt} | you (all) can |
| {er/sie/es kann} | he/she/it can | {sie/Sie können} | they can / you can |

Two things to notice, and they hold for every modal verb: the **ich and er forms are identical** and have no ending ({ich kann}, {er kann}, never "er kannt"), and the vowel changes in the singular but not in the plural.

Now the important part, the word order. A modal verb needs a second verb, and that second verb goes to the **end**, in the infinitive:

| Position 1 | Verb | Middle | End |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {Ich} | {kann} | {sehr gut} | {schwimmen.} |
| {Am Samstag} | {kann} | {ich leider nicht} | {kommen.} |
| {Was} | {kannst} | {du} | {kochen?} |
| | {Kannst} | {du Gitarre} | {spielen?} |

The two verbs form a bracket around the rest of the sentence, the {Satzklammer}. The modal verb is conjugated and stands in the usual verb slot (second, or first in a yes/no question); the infinitive waits at the end. English keeps the two verbs together ("I can swim very well"); German puts everything else between them.

::word-order{mode=bracket}

Separable verbs from lesson 6 join up again at the end: {Ich stehe um sechs auf} becomes {Ich muss um sechs aufstehen.} And {Kommst du mit?} becomes {Kannst du mitkommen?}

:::mistake
Two classic slips. First, only the modal verb is conjugated: {Ich kann spielen}, not "Ich kann spiele". Second, there is **no {zu}** after a modal verb, even though English says "have *to*": {Ich muss arbeiten}, not "Ich muss zu arbeiten".
:::

```order
title: Put the second verb at the end
items:
  - en: I can play the guitar.
    de: Ich kann Gitarre spielen.
    extra: [spiele]
    also: [Gitarre spielen kann ich., Gitarre kann ich spielen.]
  - en: On Saturday I can't come, unfortunately.
    de: Am_Samstag kann ich leider nicht kommen.
    also: [Ich kann am_Samstag leider nicht kommen., Leider kann ich am_Samstag nicht kommen., Ich kann leider am_Samstag nicht kommen.]
  - en: Can you come along tomorrow?
    de: Kannst du morgen mitkommen?

```

## Wollen or möchten?

{wollen}: {ich will, du willst, er will, wir wollen, ihr wollt, sie wollen}. {möchten}: {ich möchte, du möchtest, er möchte, wir möchten, ihr möchtet, sie möchten}.

Both mean wanting. The difference is tone:

- **{wollen}** is direct: a firm wish, a plan, sometimes a demand. {Ich will Deutsch lernen.} I want to learn German. {Wir wollen im Sommer nach Italien fahren.} We want to go to Italy in the summer.
- **{möchten}** is polite and softer: "would like". It is what you say in a shop or café (lesson 5). {Ich möchte einen Tee, bitte.} An adult who says {Ich will einen Tee} to a waiter sounds like a small child.

{möchten} is really a form of {mögen} (it means "would like"), which is why its endings look a little different: {du möchtest}, {ihr möchtet}.

:::key
**{Ich will} = I want.** It never means "I will". German has a separate verb for the future ({werden}), which you will meet at A2. Very often German simply uses the present: {Ich komme morgen.} I'll come tomorrow.
:::

And one very common use: **{Wollen wir …?}** means "Shall we …?" It is a suggestion, not a question about what you want. {Wollen wir ins Kino gehen?} Shall we go to the cinema?

## Müssen, dürfen, sollen

Here are all six together. Look for the pattern rather than learning six tables: singular forms change their vowel (except {sollen} and {möchten}), {ich} and {er} are the same, and the plural is perfectly regular.

| | {können} | {wollen} | {müssen} | {dürfen} | {sollen} | {möchten} |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich} | {kann} | {will} | {muss} | {darf} | {soll} | {möchte} |
| {du} | {kannst} | {willst} | {musst} | {darfst} | {sollst} | {möchtest} |
| {er/sie/es} | {kann} | {will} | {muss} | {darf} | {soll} | {möchte} |
| {wir} | {können} | {wollen} | {müssen} | {dürfen} | {sollen} | {möchten} |
| {ihr} | {könnt} | {wollt} | {müsst} | {dürft} | {sollt} | {möchtet} |
| {sie/Sie} | {können} | {wollen} | {müssen} | {dürfen} | {sollen} | {möchten} |

::conjugator{verb=müssen}

- **{müssen}**: necessity. {Ich muss morgen früh aufstehen.} I have to get up early tomorrow.
- **{dürfen}**: permission. {Darf ich hier fotografieren?} May I take photos here? {Hier darf man nicht rauchen.} You're not allowed to smoke here.
- **{sollen}**: someone else's instruction or expectation. {Der Arzt sagt, ich soll mehr Sport machen.} The doctor says I should do more exercise. In questions it offers help: {Soll ich kochen?} Shall I cook?

That last example used **{man}**, the German "one" or general "you": {Hier darf man nicht rauchen.} It takes the er/sie/es form of the verb and is far more common than English "one": signs, rules and recipes are full of it.

```words
man | one, you (people in general)
rauchen | to smoke
allein | alone
morgen | tomorrow
```

:::mistake
**"Must not" is a trap.** English "you must not" forbids something; German {du musst nicht} only says it is not necessary.

- {Du musst nicht kommen.} You don't have to come. (but you can)
- {Du darfst nicht kommen.} You mustn't come. / You're not allowed to come.

To forbid, use {nicht dürfen}. Returners get this wrong more often than almost anything else in this lesson.
:::

## Hast du Lust? Inviting and saying no

```words
die Lust (Sg.) | desire; Lust haben: to feel like it
die Zeit, -en | time
die Idee, -n | idea
die Party, -s | party
leider | unfortunately
schade | a pity, what a shame
vielleicht | maybe, perhaps
das Mal, -e | time (occasion)
Tut mir leid. | I'm sorry.
der See, -n | lake
```

| Inviting | Saying yes | Saying no |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {Hast du am Samstag Zeit?} | {Ja, gern!} | {Leider kann ich nicht.} |
| {Hast du Lust auf Kino?} | {Gute Idee!} | {Tut mir leid, ich muss arbeiten.} |
| {Wollen wir schwimmen gehen?} | {Klar, warum nicht?} | {Ich habe leider keine Zeit.} |
| {Kommst du mit?} | {Ich komme gern mit.} | {Vielleicht ein anderes Mal?} |

{Lust haben} means "to feel like (doing) something": {Ich habe keine Lust} is "I don't feel like it". With a noun it takes {auf}: {Hast du Lust auf einen Kaffee?} You will also hear {Hast du Lust, ins Kino zu gehen?}: the {zu} construction comes at B1, but you can understand it already.

A polite "no" in German usually comes with a reason, and the reason very often contains {müssen}: {Leider kann ich nicht, ich muss arbeiten / lernen / meine Oma besuchen.}

```dialogue
title: Und am Sonntag?
Mia: Hallo Ben! Hast du am Samstag Lust auf Kino? | Hi Ben! Do you fancy the cinema on Saturday?
Ben: Am Samstag? Leider kann ich nicht. Ich muss arbeiten. | Saturday? Unfortunately I can't. I have to work.
Mia: Schade! Und am Sonntag? | What a shame! And on Sunday?
Ben: Am Sonntag habe ich Zeit. Aber ich möchte lieber etwas draußen machen. | On Sunday I'm free. But I'd rather do something outside.
Mia: Okay. Wollen wir zum Wannsee fahren? Da kann man schwimmen. | OK. Shall we go to the Wannsee? You can swim there.
Ben: Gute Idee! Kann Hannah auch mitkommen? | Good idea! Can Hannah come too?
Mia: Klar! Wann treffen wir uns? | Of course! When shall we meet?
Ben: Um zehn am Bahnhof? | At ten at the station?
Mia: Super. Bis Sonntag! | Great. See you on Sunday!
```

{Wann treffen wir uns?} (When shall we meet?) contains a reflexive {uns}, "each other". Take it as a phrase; reflexive verbs come at A2.
