English puts long descriptions *after* the noun: "the reform planned by the government", "rents that have been rising for years". Written German can put them *before* it, between the article and the noun: {die von der Regierung geplante Reform}, {die seit Jahren steigenden Mieten}. Open any German newspaper, contract or textbook and you will find these on every page; academic German stacks them three deep.

They are the main reason a returner who chats happily in German can still struggle with a newspaper. The good news: there is a simple way to read them, and once you see the pattern you will spot it everywhere. This lesson builds up to it in steps, starting with participles you already know.

## Der lachende Mann: Partizip I

**Partizip I** is the infinitive plus **-d**: {lachen} → {lachend}, {schlafen} → {schlafend}, {warten} → {wartend}. It means that something is happening *at the same time*, actively, like English "-ing": {der schlafende Hund} is the dog that is sleeping.

Before a noun it takes ordinary adjective endings:

- {Schlafende Hunde soll man nicht wecken.} Let sleeping dogs lie.
- {Die wartenden Fahrgäste wurden ungeduldig.} The waiting passengers grew impatient.
- {Gießen Sie das kochende Wasser über den Tee.} Pour the boiling water over the tea.

Without an ending it works as an adverb: {Sie kam lachend ins Zimmer.} {Er antwortete zögernd.}

:::mistake
German Partizip I is **never** a tense. English "I am reading" is {Ich lese}, not {Ich bin lesend}; "Reading is fun" is {Lesen macht Spaß}. The German -d form only describes: a reading person, a crying child, a convincing argument.
:::

Many Partizip I forms have become ordinary adjectives that you will use all the time: {dringend} (urgent), {spannend} (gripping), {überzeugend} (convincing), {entscheidend} (decisive), {zunehmend} (increasing, increasingly), {folgend} (following: {im Folgenden}, in what follows).

```words

überzeugend | convincing
entscheidend | decisive, crucial
zunehmend | increasing; increasingly
folgend | following
zögern | to hesitate
zittern | to tremble, to shake
nicken | to nod
wecken | to wake (someone)
```

## Das gekochte Ei: Partizip II

**Partizip II** before a noun describes something *completed*. What exactly it means depends on the verb:

- **Transitive verbs** (with an object) give a **passive** meaning: {das gekochte Ei} is the egg that has been boiled; {die gestohlenen Fahrräder}, {ein gut bezahlter Job}.
- **Verbs that take sein** (movement, change of state) give an **active** meaning: {der eingeschlafene Junge} is the boy who has fallen asleep; {die gestiegenen Preise} are prices that have risen; {der verspätet angekommene Zug}.
- **Other intransitive verbs** (with {haben}, no object) have no such participle: there is no {*der geschlafene Mann} or {*das gelachte Kind}.

Partizip I and II of the same verb make a neat pair:

| Partizip I: happening now | Partizip II: completed |
| --- | --- |
| {das kochende Wasser} boiling water | {das gekochte Wasser} boiled water (maybe cold by now) |
| {die steigenden Preise} rising prices | {die gestiegenen Preise} prices that have risen |
| {die fallenden Blätter} falling leaves | {die gefallenen Blätter} fallen leaves |
| {die abfahrenden Züge} departing trains | {die abgefahrenen Züge} trains that have left |

## Die von der Regierung geplante Reform

Now the big one. A participle before a noun can bring along everything a verb can have: objects, adverbs, an agent with {von}, a time, a place. All of it sits **between the article and the participle**:

| article | extension | participle + ending | noun |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {die} | {von der Regierung} | {geplante} | {Reform} |
| {die} | {seit Jahren} | {steigenden} | {Mieten} |
| {der} | {gestern Abend in Graz} | {angekommene} | {Brief} |
| {ein} | {von allen Kollegen} | {geschätzter} | {Chef} |
| {der} | {für seine Strenge} | {bekannte} | {Lehrer} |

The last row shows that ordinary adjectives can be extended in the same way ({bekannt für} → {der für seine Strenge bekannte Lehrer}).

:::key
**How to read an extended attribute.** When an article is followed by something that cannot belong to it (a preposition, an adverb, another article), hold the article in your head and **jump forward to the first noun that fits it**. Read that noun, then the participle, then the words in between, as if they were a relative clause: {die von der Regierung geplante Reform} → *die Reform*… *geplant*… *von der Regierung*: the reform that the government is planning.
:::

**Unfolding** an attribute into a relative clause is the best way to check that you have understood it:

- Partizip I → active relative clause, same time: {die seit Jahren steigenden Mieten} → {die Mieten, die seit Jahren steigen}
- Partizip II of a transitive verb → passive: {die von der Regierung geplante Reform} → {die Reform, die von der Regierung geplant wird} (or {wurde}, {worden ist}: the context decides)
- Partizip II of a sein-verb → active perfect: {der gestern angekommene Brief} → {der Brief, der gestern angekommen ist}

::attribute-unfolder

```type
title: Unfold it
items:
  - de: die von der Regierung geplante Reform → die Reform, die ___
    answer:
      - von der Regierung geplant wird
      - von der Regierung geplant wurde
      - von der Regierung geplant worden ist
      - von der Regierung geplant ist
  - de: der seit zwei Stunden wartende Zug → der Zug, der ___

  - de: der für seine Strenge bekannte Lehrer → der Lehrer, der ___

```

## Die zu lösende Aufgabe

In the last lesson you met {sein + zu}: {Die Aufgabe ist zu lösen} (must, or can, be solved). Put that before a noun and you get the **gerundive**: **zu + Partizip I** + ending.

- {Die Aufgabe ist bis Freitag zu lösen.} → {die bis Freitag zu lösende Aufgabe} (the task to be solved by Friday)
- {Die Kosten sind zu erwarten.} → {die zu erwartenden Kosten} (the costs to be expected)
- With a separable verb, {zu} goes inside: {die einzureichenden Unterlagen}, {der abzuholende Brief}.

It is formal written German through and through: contracts, official letters, academic texts. One phrase is common enough to learn as a whole: {ein nicht zu unterschätzendes Problem}, a problem not to be underestimated.

  - prompt: "{die Formulare, die ausgefüllt werden müssen}"
    answer: die auszufüllenden Formulare

## Im Zug sitzend: participle phrases

A participle and its companions can also stand on their own as a phrase, a little like a shortened clause. This is literary or formal written German:

- {Im Zug sitzend, las sie die Zeitung.} (= {Während sie im Zug saß, …})
- {In Wien angekommen, rief er sofort seine Mutter an.} (= {Nachdem er in Wien angekommen war, …})
- {Vom Lärm geweckt, stand sie auf.} (= {Weil der Lärm sie geweckt hatte, …})

As in English, the phrase belongs to the subject of the main clause, and the same jokes are possible when it does not ("Walking down the street, the church came into view"). A comma usually sets the phrase off; at the start of the sentence it is optional.

In speech you would use a clause instead, with one important exception: a handful of fixed participle phrases that everyone uses all the time.

| phrase | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| {ehrlich gesagt} | to be honest |
| {genau genommen} | strictly speaking |
| {im Grunde genommen} | basically |
| {kurz gesagt} | in short |
| {abgesehen davon} | apart from that |
| {verglichen mit} | compared with |

## Written and spoken

Extended attributes are a feature of **written** German. In conversation, people use relative clauses, or split the information into two sentences. Listen to a linguist explain it on a language podcast:

```dialogue
title: Podcast „Sprachlabor“
Hannah: Willkommen bei „Sprachlabor“, dem Podcast über die deutsche Sprache. Heute bei mir ist Luca Moser, Sprachwissenschaftler an der Universität Bern. Herr Moser, warum schreiben Zeitungen so lange Sätze? | Welcome to "Sprachlabor", the podcast about the German language. With me today is Luca Moser, a linguist at the University of Bern. Herr Moser, why do newspapers write such long sentences?
Luca: Die Sätze sind gar nicht unbedingt lang. Sie sind dicht. Nehmen Sie „die von der Regierung geplante Reform“. Das sind sechs Wörter statt acht. | The sentences aren't necessarily long. They're dense. Take "the reform planned by the government". That's six words instead of eight.
Hannah: Statt acht? | Instead of eight?
Luca: „Die Reform, die von der Regierung geplant wird.“ Zählen Sie nach. | "The reform that is being planned by the government." Count them.
Hannah: Und für die Leser ist das kein Problem? | And that's no problem for readers?
Luca: Für geübte Leser nicht. Aber man muss beim Lesen ein bisschen springen: vom Artikel zum Nomen und wieder zurück. | Not for practised readers. But you have to jump about a little as you read, from the article to the noun and back again.
Hannah: Und im Gespräch? Sagt man so etwas auch? | And in conversation? Do people say things like that too?
Luca: Kaum. Stellen Sie sich vor, ich sage beim Abendessen: „Reich mir bitte die von dir gestern gekaufte Butter.“ Meine Frau würde mich ziemlich komisch ansehen. | Hardly. Imagine I said at dinner, "Please pass me the butter bought by you yesterday." My wife would give me a very odd look.
Hannah: Verständlich. Was raten Sie unseren Lernenden? | Understandably. What do you advise our learners?
Luca: Lesen Sie solche Ausdrücke von hinten nach vorne. Erst das Nomen, dann das Partizip, dann den Rest. Dann sind sie gar nicht so schlimm. | Read expressions like that from the back to the front. First the noun, then the participle, then the rest. Then they're not so bad at all.
```

:::culture
Hannah says {unsere Lernenden}, not {unsere Lerner}. Participles used as nouns ({die Lernenden}, {die Studierenden}, {die Reisenden}, {die Anwesenden}) name people without choosing a gender in the plural, and have become standard in universities, public services and much of the media: {Studierende} has largely replaced {Studenten und Studentinnen} in official use. They decline like adjectives: {ein Reisender}, {eine Reisende}, {die Reisenden}.
:::
