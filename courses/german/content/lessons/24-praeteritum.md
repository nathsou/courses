  - read and tell stories in the Präteritum, the past tense of books, news and fairy tales
  - form the Präteritum of regular, strong and mixed verbs

Every German child knows how a story begins: {Es war einmal …}, "once upon a time there was …". And every child knows how it ends: {Und wenn sie nicht gestorben sind, dann leben sie noch heute.} "And if they haven't died, they are still alive today": the Brothers Grimm's version of "happily ever after".

The first sentence is in the **Präteritum**, the simple past. At A2 you told stories in the Perfekt ({ich bin gefahren}, {ich habe gesehen}), which is how Germans talk about the past. But open a novel, a newspaper or a book of fairy tales and the Präteritum is everywhere: {sie ging}, {er sah}, {es geschah}. You already know a good part of it: {war}, {hatte}, {wurde}, {konnte}, {gab}, {ging}, {kam}. This lesson gives you the rest, so that you can read real German and write your own stories, and it adds the tense for "had done": {hatte gemacht}, {war gegangen}.

:::note
English grammars often call the Präteritum the "imperfect". The name misleads: {ich ging} means "I went" at least as often as "I was going". German schools say {Präteritum} or {Imperfekt}; this course says Präteritum.
:::

## Regular verbs: the past in -te

{Lena wohnte damals in Kiel. Sie arbeitete in einem Café am Hafen, und abends spielte sie Gitarre in einer Band. Eines Abends hörte ein Musiker aus Hamburg sie spielen.}

Take the stem (**wohn-**, **spiel-**, **hör-**), add **-te**, then a person ending:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich sagte} | I said | {wir sagten} | we said |
| {du sagtest} | you said | {ihr sagtet} | you (all) said |
| {er/sie/es sagte} | he/she/it said | {sie/Sie sagten} | they/you said |

Three things to notice:

- **ich and er/sie/es are identical**: {ich sagte}, {sie sagte}. Unlike the present tense, the third person has no -t of its own.
- When the stem ends in **-t** or **-d** (and in a few verbs like {öffnen} and {regnen}), an extra **-e-** keeps the ending audible: {arbeitete}, {wartete}, {redete}, {öffnete}, {es regnete}.
- Separable verbs split exactly as in the present: {Der Zug kam pünktlich an.} {Sie kaufte im Supermarkt ein.} Inseparable verbs stay in one piece: {sie erzählte}, {er besuchte}, {wir verkauften}.

:::fun
The -te is a family heirloom. English "play-ed" and German "spiel-te" go back to the same Germanic past ending, a d or t sound added to the stem. Both languages also kept an older set of verbs that change their vowel instead: sing, sang; {singen}, {sang}. That is the next section.
:::

leben | to live, to be alive
heiraten | to marry, to get married
damals | back then, at that time

## Strong verbs: a new vowel and no ending

{Hans nahm das Gold, legte es auf die Schulter und ging fröhlich los.}

{legte} is regular. {nahm} and {ging} are **strong verbs**: instead of adding -te, they change their stem vowel. In the ich and er/sie/es forms they have **no ending at all**; the other persons add the familiar -st, -en, -t, -en:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich ging} | I went | {wir gingen} | we went |
| {du gingst} | you went | {ihr gingt} | you (all) went |
| {er/sie/es ging} | he/she/it went | {sie/Sie gingen} | they/you went |

As with regular verbs, a stem in -d or -t takes an extra -e- before -st and -t: {du fandest}, {ihr fandet}.

You cannot work out the new vowel from the infinitive, any more than you can work out "went" from "go". But most strong verbs belong to a few families:

| Vowel change | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| ei → ie or i | {schreiben} → {schrieb}, {bleiben} → {blieb}, {reiten} → {ritt}, {schneiden} → {schnitt} |
| ie → o | {fliegen} → {flog}, {verlieren} → {verlor}, {fliehen} → {floh} |
| i → a | {finden} → {fand}, {trinken} → {trank}, {singen} → {sang}, {springen} → {sprang}, {beginnen} → {begann} |
| e → a | {sprechen} → {sprach}, {helfen} → {half}, {nehmen} → {nahm}, {sehen} → {sah}, {geben} → {gab}, {essen} → {aß}, {sterben} → {starb} |
| a → u | {fahren} → {fuhr}, {tragen} → {trug}, {waschen} → {wusch} |
| a, au, u → ie | {fallen} → {fiel}, {schlafen} → {schlief}, {laufen} → {lief}, {rufen} → {rief}, {halten} → {hielt}, {lassen} → {ließ} |
| one of a kind | {gehen} → {ging}, {stehen} → {stand}, {sitzen} → {saß}, {liegen} → {lag}, {kommen} → {kam}, {tun} → {tat}, {sein} → {war}, {werden} → {wurde} |

:::key
Learn strong verbs as three forms, the way the word lists give them: {fahren, fuhr, gefahren}. Very often English changes the vowel in the same place: {singen, sang} and sing, sang; {trinken, trank} and drink, drank; {geben, gab} and give, gave; {fallen, fiel} and fall, fell. Tap any verb in this course to see its principal parts.
:::

## Mixed verbs: a new vowel and -te

{Ich dachte, du kanntest ihn!} {Niemand wusste, wo Hans war.}

A small group of verbs does both things at once: they change the vowel like strong verbs and add -te like regular ones. You know them already from their participles:

| Infinitive | Präteritum | Perfekt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {bringen} | {brachte} | {hat gebracht} |
| {denken} | {dachte} | {hat gedacht} |
| {kennen} | {kannte} | {hat gekannt} |
| {rennen} | {rannte} | {ist gerannt} |
| {wissen} | {wusste} | {hat gewusst} |

The modal verbs, which you met in the Präteritum in lesson 16, follow the same idea: they drop their umlaut and add -te: {können} → {konnte}, {müssen} → {musste}, {dürfen} → {durfte}, {mögen} → {mochte}, and {wollen}, {sollen} → {wollte}, {sollte}. {haben} → {hatte} also adds -te (and loses its b); {werden} → {wurde} is one of a kind.

:::mistake
{ich denkte}, {er wisste}, {sie bringte}: three of the commonest slips among returners. The forms are {ich dachte}, {er wusste}, {sie brachte}. A reliable test: if the participle is mixed ({gedacht}, {gewusst}, {gebracht}), so is the Präteritum.
:::

```words
bringen (bringt, brachte, hat gebracht) | to bring
denken (denkt, dachte, hat gedacht) | to think
kennen (kennt, kannte, hat gekannt) | to know (a person or place)
wissen (weiß, wusste, hat gewusst) | to know (a fact)
```

## Präteritum or Perfekt?

Many learners half remember a rule like "Perfekt for the recent past, Präteritum for long ago". Forget it. The choice has nothing to do with how long ago something happened. It depends on **how you are telling it**:

| Präteritum | Perfekt |
| --- | --- |
| written narration: novels, fairy tales, biographies | conversation: {Was hast du am Wochenende gemacht?} |
| news reports, police reports, history books | chats, text messages, informal emails |
| {war}, {hatte}, the modal verbs, {wurde}, {es gab}: in speech as well | almost every other verb when you speak |

So a German says {Ich bin gestern ins Kino gegangen, aber der Film war langweilig}: the Perfekt for {gehen}, the Präteritum for {sein}, in one breath. And the same person writing a short story writes {Sie ging ins Kino. Der Film war langweilig.}

:::culture
The further south you go, the rarer the spoken Präteritum becomes. In Hamburg or Berlin you will hear {Ich dachte …} or {Gestern kam er nicht} in conversation. In Bavaria and Austria many people say {ich bin gewesen} even where a northerner says {ich war}, and the Swiss German dialects have practically no Präteritum at all: "I was" is *i bi gsi*. In written Standard German, though, the Präteritum is the norm everywhere, from Kiel to Graz to Bern.
:::

```dialogue
title: Wie habt ihr euch kennengelernt?
Mia: Oma, wie hast du Opa eigentlich kennengelernt? | Grandma, how did you actually meet Grandpa?
Oma Helga: Ach, das war 1968. Ich arbeitete damals in einer Bäckerei am Marktplatz in Bremen. | Oh, that was in 1968. Back then I worked in a bakery on the market square in Bremen.
Mia: Und Opa kam jeden Tag und kaufte Brötchen? | And Grandpa came in every day and bought rolls?
Oma Helga: Nein, er kam nie. Er mochte keine Brötchen! Aber eines Tages regnete es furchtbar, und plötzlich stand er in der Tür, ganz nass. | No, he never came. He didn't like rolls! But one day it was pouring with rain, and suddenly there he was in the doorway, soaking wet.
Mia: Und dann? | And then?
Oma Helga: Er kaufte ein Stück Kuchen und blieb, bis der Regen aufhörte. Wir redeten fast zwei Stunden. | He bought a piece of cake and stayed until the rain stopped. We talked for almost two hours.
Mia: Wie romantisch! | How romantic!
Oma Helga: Am nächsten Tag kam er wieder, und am übernächsten auch. Ein Jahr später haben wir geheiratet. | The next day he came again, and the day after that too. A year later we got married.
Mia: Und die Brötchen? | And the rolls?

Oma Helga is from Bremen and is telling a story, so she uses plenty of Präteritum ({arbeitete}, {kam}, {regnete}, {redeten}). But listen to the questions, and to the moment she sums up: {Ein Jahr später haben wir geheiratet}, {hat er … gegessen}. Spoken German moves freely between the two.

## Before that: the Plusquamperfekt

{Als wir am Bahnhof ankamen, war der Zug schon abgefahren.}

Two things happened in the past: we arrived, and the train left. The train left **first**. To show that, German uses the **Plusquamperfekt**, exactly like the English "had left". It is built like the Perfekt, with the Präteritum of {haben} or {sein}:

| Perfekt | Plusquamperfekt |
| --- | --- |
| {ich habe gegessen} | {ich hatte gegessen} |
| {sie ist gegangen} | {sie war gegangen} |
| {wir haben angerufen} | {wir hatten angerufen} |
| {der Zug ist abgefahren} | {der Zug war abgefahren} |

The choice between {haben} and {sein} is the same as in the Perfekt: movement and change of state take {sein}.

:::mistake
Use the Plusquamperfekt only when one past event comes before another. It is not a "more past" past for things that happened long ago. In everyday speech, especially in the south, you will hear it used loosely ({Ich hatte dich doch gestern angerufen!}), but in careful German it always looks back from a point in the past.
:::

## nachdem, bevor, während, seitdem, sobald

These five link one past event to another. They are subordinating conjunctions, like {weil} and {dass}: the verb goes to the end of their clause, and if the clause comes first, the main clause starts with its verb.

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {nachdem} | after | {Nachdem sie gegessen hatte, rief sie ihre Mutter an.} |
| {bevor} | before | {Bevor er ging, schloss er alle Fenster.} |
| {während} | while | {Während Carl schlief, schob Bertha den Wagen aus der Werkstatt.} |
| {seitdem} / {seit} | (ever) since | {Seitdem sie in Wien wohnt, trinkt sie keinen Filterkaffee mehr.} |
| {sobald} | as soon as | {Sobald ich ankomme, schreibe ich dir.} |

{nachdem} has a tense rule of its own. Its clause is always **one step further back** than the main clause:

| nachdem clause | main clause |
| --- | --- |
| Plusquamperfekt: {Nachdem er gegessen hatte,} | Präteritum: {ging er spazieren.} |
| Perfekt: {Nachdem er gegessen hat,} | Präsens: {geht er spazieren.} |

The others usually take the same tense in both clauses: {Bevor er ging, schloss er die Fenster.} {Während sie kochte, hörte sie Radio.}

:::grammar[nach, nachdem, danach]
Three words, three jobs, endlessly confused:

- {nach} is a preposition with a noun in the dative: {nach dem Essen}.
- {nachdem} is a conjunction with a whole clause, verb at the end: {nachdem wir gegessen hatten}.
- {danach} is an adverb, "after that", and fills the first position of a main clause: {Danach gingen wir spazieren.}

The same trio exists for "before": {vor dem Essen}, {bevor wir aßen}, {vorher}. {seit} can be a preposition ({seit einem Jahr}) or a conjunction ({seit ich hier wohne}); {seitdem} is a conjunction or an adverb meaning "since then". And {während} is also a preposition, with the genitive, which you will meet in lesson 30.
:::

bevor | before (conjunction)

seitdem | since (conjunction); since then
sobald | as soon as
die Erfindung, -en | invention

```
