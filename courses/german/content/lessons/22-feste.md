The German-speaking year is full of festivals, and each comes with its own phrase you are expected to know: {Frohe Weihnachten}, {Frohe Ostern}, {Guten Rutsch}, and, in Cologne in February, {Alaaf!} This lesson takes you round the year from Silvester to Nikolaus, and on the way covers the grammar of giving presents (who gets what), of wishing for things ({Ich wünsche mir …}) and of whose party it is ({Fatmas Party}).

There is one cultural rule to learn before anything else: **never wish a German happy birthday before the day.** It is thought to bring bad luck. Wait until midnight, or until the next morning.

The year in festivals, roughly in order:

| when | what |
| --- | --- |
| {31. Dezember} | {Silvester}: fireworks at midnight, {Sekt}, and {Guten Rutsch!} beforehand |
| {Februar oder März} | {Karneval} (Rhineland), {Fasching} (Bavaria, Austria), {Fasnacht} (Switzerland, the south-west): costumes, parades, a week of chaos |
| {März oder April} | {Ostern}: painted eggs ({Ostereier}) hidden in the garden by the {Osterhase} |
| {1. August} | the Swiss national day, with bonfires and lanterns |
| {September} | the {Oktoberfest} in Munich: despite the name, it mostly happens in September |
| {3. Oktober} | {Tag der Deutschen Einheit}, Germany's national day |
| {26. Oktober} | {Nationalfeiertag}, Austria's national day |
| {11. November} | {Sankt Martin}: children walk through the streets with lanterns |
| {6. Dezember} | {Nikolaus}: children leave a boot outside the door and find it full of sweets |
| {24. Dezember} | {Heiligabend}: presents are opened in the evening, not on the 25th |

:::culture
Who brings the presents depends on where you live. In the north of Germany it is the {Weihnachtsmann}, a Father Christmas figure; in the south, in Austria and in much of Switzerland it is the {Christkind}, an angel-like child that nobody ever sees. Either way, the presents come on the evening of the 24th. In Austria, January is {Jänner}: {Neujahr} is {der 1. Jänner}.
:::

## Herzlichen Glückwunsch!

Every occasion has its formula:

| occasion | you say |
| --- | --- |
| a birthday | {Herzlichen Glückwunsch zum Geburtstag!} {Alles Gute zum Geburtstag!} |
| a wedding | {Herzlichen Glückwunsch zur Hochzeit!} |
| Christmas | {Frohe Weihnachten!} {Schöne Feiertage!} |
| Easter | {Frohe Ostern!} |
| before New Year | {Guten Rutsch!} {Einen guten Rutsch ins neue Jahr!} |
| at midnight on Silvester | {Frohes neues Jahr!} {Prost Neujahr!} (Austria: {Prosit Neujahr!}) |
| an exam, an interview | {Viel Glück!} {Viel Erfolg!} |
| someone who is ill | {Gute Besserung!} |
| a party, a trip | {Viel Spaß!} {Gute Reise!} |
| carnival | {Kölle Alaaf!} (Cologne), {Helau!} (Düsseldorf, Mainz) |

:::grammar[Why "Guten Rutsch" and not "Guter Rutsch"?]
All these wishes are short for a sentence with {wünschen}: {Ich wünsche dir einen guten Rutsch.} The wish is the **accusative object** of that hidden verb, and that is why the endings are accusative: {Guten Rutsch} (masculine, -en, as in {den}), {Frohe Weihnachten} (plural), {Herzlichen Glückwunsch}, {Schönen Abend noch!} Once you see the hidden {Ich wünsche dir …}, the endings follow from lesson 19.
:::

```words

Herzlichen Glückwunsch! | Congratulations! Happy birthday!
Alles Gute! | All the best!
Frohe Weihnachten! | Merry Christmas!
Guten Rutsch! | Happy New Year! (said before New Year)
Viel Glück! | Good luck!
das Glück (Sg.) | luck; happiness
```

## Was schenkst du deiner Mutter?

Mia and her brother Ben are planning their grandmother's birthday.

```dialogue
title: Was schenken wir Oma?
Mia: Oma wird nächsten Monat achtzig. Was schenken wir ihr? | Grandma's turning eighty next month. What shall we give her?
Ben: Keine Ahnung. Letztes Jahr haben wir ihr einen Schal geschenkt. | No idea. Last year we gave her a scarf.
Mia: Und den trägt sie nie. Ich glaube, sie wünscht sich etwas anderes. | And she never wears it. I think she'd like something else.
Ben: Was denn? Sie sagt immer: „Ich habe schon alles.“ | Like what? She always says, "I've got everything already."
Mia: Aber sie sieht sich so gern alte Fotos an. Wir machen ihr ein Fotobuch! | But she loves looking at old photos. We'll make her a photo book!
Ben: Super Idee! Mama gibt uns bestimmt die alten Fotos von Opa. | Great idea! Mum will definitely give us the old photos of Grandpa.
Mia: Und ich backe Omas Lieblingskuchen: Schwarzwälder Kirschtorte! | And I'll bake Grandma's favourite cake: Black Forest gateau!
```

With {schenken} (to give as a present), {geben}, {schicken}, {zeigen} and {bringen}, the person who receives is in the **dative**, the thing given in the **accusative**: {Wir schenken **ihr** (D) **ein Fotobuch** (A).} You met this in lesson 14. The order of the two objects follows a simple rule of thumb:

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| two nouns | dative before accusative | {Ich schenke meiner Mutter ein Buch.} |
| a pronoun and a noun | the pronoun first | {Ich schenke ihr ein Buch.} {Ich schenke es meiner Mutter.} |
| two pronouns | accusative before dative | {Ich schenke es ihr.} |

```words
schenken | to give (as a present)
das Geschenk, -e | present, gift
schicken | to send
```

## Ich wünsche mir …

In lesson 17 you met reflexive verbs with {mich} and {dich}: {Ich freue mich}, {Ich habe mich erkältet}. Some reflexive verbs take a **dative** pronoun instead, and the rule for when is simple: **if the sentence already has an accusative object, the reflexive pronoun is dative.**

- {Ich wünsche **mir** ein Fahrrad.} (the bike is the accusative; so {mir})
- {Wir sehen **uns** das Feuerwerk **an**.}
- {Ich kaufe **mir** neue Schuhe.}
- {Wasch **dir** die Hände!}

The dative reflexive pronouns differ from the accusative ones **only for ich and du**:

| | accusative | dative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ich | {mich} | {mir} |
| du | {dich} | {dir} |
| er/sie/es | {sich} | {sich} |
| wir | {uns} | {uns} |
| ihr | {euch} | {euch} |
| sie/Sie | {sich} | {sich} |

:::tip
{Was wünschst du dir zu Weihnachten?} is the question every child in Germany hears in December. In the south, in Austria and in Switzerland you will also hear {an Weihnachten}.
:::

```words
sich wünschen | to wish for, to want (as a present)
sich an|sehen (sieht an, sah an, hat angesehen) | to have a look at, to watch
sich überlegen | to think about, to consider
sich merken | to remember, to make a note of
```

## Fatmas Party: whose?

To say whose something is, German adds **-s** to a name, **without an apostrophe**: {Fatmas Party}, {Peters Geburtstag}, {Omas Kuchen}, {Zeyneps Mutter}. The name comes first, just as in English.

Names that already end in an s-sound (-s, -ß, -x, -z) take only an apostrophe: {Jonas' Party}, {Max' Auto}. Most people avoid that in speech and use **von** + dative instead, which works with any noun, not just names:

- {die Party von Jonas}
- {der Geburtstag von meiner Schwester}
- {das Auto von meinen Eltern}

{Von} is the everyday spoken form; the -s with names is common in both speech and writing.

:::mistake
English writes "Peter's". Standard German writes **{Peters}**, with no apostrophe. You will see {Peter's Imbiss} on shop signs, and a sign-writer may get away with it, but in a letter or an exam it counts as an error.
:::
