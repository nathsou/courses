Nobody plans to fall ill abroad, but it happens, usually in your first German winter, and usually when your German has gone to bed with a temperature too. This lesson gives you what you need when it counts: the body, the words for what hurts, a phone call to the doctor's, the appointment itself and the trip to the pharmacy.

On the grammar side, it brings the **reflexive verbs**, which German uses far more than English ({Ich fühle mich nicht gut}: literally "I feel myself not well"), and the most useful way to give advice: {Du solltest …}, you should.

A few things to notice:

- **{der Hals}** is both the neck and the throat, so {Halsschmerzen} is a sore throat.
- **{das Knie}** is spelt the same in the plural but usually said differently: one knee has one syllable, two knees usually have two (Kni-e).
- Several plurals take an umlaut: {Köpfe}, {Hände}, {Füße}, {Zähne}, {Bäuche}, and {Münder} with -er.

## Was tut weh?

There are two everyday ways to say what hurts.

**1. {Mir tut … weh.}** The verb is {wehtun} (separable: {tut … weh}), the body part is the **subject**, and the person who suffers is in the **dative**, just as with {gefallen} in lesson 14:

- {Mir tut der Kopf weh.} My head hurts.
- {Mir tun die Füße weh.} My feet hurt. (plural subject, plural verb)
- {Tut dir der Rücken weh?} Does your back hurt?
- {Was tut Ihnen weh?} Where does it hurt?

**2. {Ich habe …schmerzen.}** {der Schmerz} (pain) makes compounds with the body part, almost always in the plural: {Kopfschmerzen}, {Bauchschmerzen}, {Halsschmerzen}, {Rückenschmerzen}, {Zahnschmerzen}, {Ohrenschmerzen}.

Other symptoms work with {haben} or with {sein}:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| {Ich habe Fieber.} | I have a temperature. |
| {Ich habe Husten und Schnupfen.} | I have a cough and a runny nose. |
| {Ich habe (die) Grippe.} | I have flu. |
| {Ich bin erkältet.} | I have a cold. |
| {Mir ist schlecht.} | I feel sick. |
| {Mir ist kalt.} | I'm cold. |

:::mistake
Temperature and nausea also use the dative: **{Mir ist kalt}**, not {Ich bin kalt}, which means you are a cold person; and **{Mir ist schlecht}** means "I feel sick", while {Ich bin schlecht} means "I'm bad (at something)" or "I'm a bad person". The dative says "it is cold *to me*".
:::

```words
weh|tun (tut weh, tat weh, hat wehgetan) (+ D) | to hurt
der Schmerz, -en | pain
das Fieber (Sg.) | fever, temperature
der Husten (Sg.) | cough
der Schnupfen (Sg.) | runny nose, cold
die Grippe (Sg.) | flu
erkältet | having a cold
Mir ist schlecht. | I feel sick.
```

```cloze
    en: I feel sick. I think I've eaten too much cake.
```

## Beim Arzt

```dialogue
title: Ein Termin, bitte
Frau Krause: Praxis Dr. Weber, Krause, guten Morgen! | Dr Weber's surgery, Krause speaking, good morning!
Hannah: Guten Morgen, hier ist Hannah Schmidt. Ich hätte gern einen Termin. | Good morning, this is Hannah Schmidt. I'd like an appointment, please.
Frau Krause: Was haben Sie denn für Beschwerden? | What are your symptoms?
Hannah: Ich habe seit drei Tagen Fieber und Halsschmerzen. | I've had a temperature and a sore throat for three days.
Frau Krause: Waren Sie schon einmal bei uns? | Have you been here before?
Hannah: Nein, ich bin neu in Freiburg. | No, I'm new in Freiburg.
Frau Krause: Kein Problem. Können Sie heute um halb zwölf kommen? | No problem. Can you come today at half past eleven?
Hannah: Ja, das passt gut. | Yes, that suits me.
Frau Krause: Bringen Sie bitte Ihre Versichertenkarte mit. Bis später! | Please bring your health insurance card. See you later!
```

**{die Beschwerden}** (plural) are your symptoms; in lesson 16, {eine Beschwerde} was a complaint at a hotel. Both are about something that is bothering you. And notice {seit drei Tagen} with the present tense: the fever started three days ago and is still there.

:::culture
In Germany most people have a family doctor, the {Hausarzt} or {Hausärztin}, and bring their health insurance card ({die Versichertenkarte}) to every appointment; in Austria it is called {die e-card}. If you are ill for more than three days, your employer needs a sick note: the doctor signs you off sick ({Sie werden krankgeschrieben}), and these days the note goes to your health insurer electronically. And in Germany and Austria even a packet of painkillers comes from an {Apotheke}: a {Drogerie} sells shampoo and vitamins, not medicine. At night and on Sundays the pharmacies of each area take turns to stay open: look for the {Notdienst} sign.
:::

```words
die Ärztin, -nen | doctor (female)
die Praxis, Praxen | (doctor's) surgery, practice
der Termin, -e | appointment
das Rezept, -e | prescription; recipe
die Apotheke, -n | pharmacy, chemist's
die Tablette, -n | tablet, pill
das Medikament, -e | medicine
krank|schreiben (schreibt krank, schrieb krank, hat krankgeschrieben) | to sign off sick
die Krankenkasse, -n | health insurance (company)
das Krankenhaus, ¨-er | hospital
```

In Switzerland you go to {das Spital} rather than {das Krankenhaus}; Austrians use both words. A doctor in Austria is often addressed as {Frau Doktor} or {Herr Doktor}.
:::

## Ich fühle mich nicht gut: reflexive verbs

In {Ich wasche das Auto} the washing is done to the car. In **{Ich wasche mich}** it is done to the person doing it: subject and object are the same person. That is a **reflexive** verb. English mostly drops the "myself" ("I wash", "I feel", "I hurry"), but German keeps it, so many everyday verbs come with a reflexive pronoun:

| sich fühlen: singular | plural |
| --- | --- |
| {ich fühle mich} | {wir fühlen uns} |
| {du fühlst dich} | {ihr fühlt euch} |
| {er/sie/es fühlt sich} | {sie/Sie fühlen sich} |

Only one pronoun is new: **{sich}**, for er, sie, es, the plural sie, and the formal Sie (with a small s: {Setzen Sie sich!}). The others are the accusative pronouns you already know.

| Verb | Example | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {sich fühlen} | {Wie fühlen Sie sich heute?} | How are you feeling today? |
| {sich erkälten} | {Ich habe mich erkältet.} | I've caught a cold. |
| {sich ausruhen} | {Ruh dich am Wochenende aus!} | Get some rest at the weekend! |
| {sich beeilen} | {Wir müssen uns beeilen.} | We have to hurry. |
| {sich setzen} | {Setzen Sie sich bitte.} | Please sit down. |
| {sich hinlegen} | {Ich lege mich ein bisschen hin.} | I'm going to lie down for a bit. |

**Where does the pronoun go?** Straight after the conjugated verb, or after a pronoun subject that has moved behind the verb: {Heute fühle ich mich besser}. In the perfect, reflexive verbs always take **{haben}**: {Ich habe mich erkältet}, {Er hat sich ausgeruht}.

:::grammar[sich setzen or sitzen?]
In lesson 15, {setzen} was putting someone in a seat: {Ich setze das Kind auf den Stuhl}. Put *yourself* there and it becomes reflexive: {Ich setze mich auf den Stuhl} (I sit down). {sitzen} is the result: {Ich sitze auf dem Stuhl} (I'm sitting). The same goes for {sich hinlegen} (to lie down) and {liegen} (to be lying).
:::

```words
sich fühlen | to feel
sich erkälten | to catch a cold
sich aus|ruhen | to rest
sich beeilen | to hurry
sich setzen | to sit down
sich hin|legen | to lie down
```

## Du solltest zum Arzt gehen

The friendliest way to give advice is **{sollte}**: {Du solltest zum Arzt gehen} (you should go to the doctor). It is the Konjunktiv II of {sollen}, and it looks exactly like the Präteritum you met in lesson 16:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| {ich sollte} | {wir sollten} |
| {du solltest} | {ihr solltet} |
| {er/sie/es sollte} | {sie/Sie sollten} |

The context tells you which: {Du solltest im Bett bleiben} is advice; {Ich sollte Mia abholen, aber ich habe es vergessen} is the past. For polite advice to a stranger: {Sie sollten …}. To soften it further, add {vielleicht}: {Du solltest vielleicht zum Arzt gehen}. With {lieber} (rather) it suggests the better choice: {Du solltest lieber zu Hause bleiben} (you'd better stay at home).

Three neighbours of {solltest}, each with its own job:

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {Du solltest viel trinken.} | you should | advice |
| {Du sollst viel trinken.} | you're to, you're supposed to | someone else's instruction: {Der Arzt sagt, ich soll viel trinken.} |
| {Du musst viel trinken.} | you must, you have to | necessity |

:::mistake
**{Du musst nicht}** does not mean "you mustn't". It means "you don't have to": {Heute ist Sonntag, du musst nicht arbeiten}. "You mustn't" is **{Du darfst nicht}**: {Mit Fieber darfst du nicht zum Sport gehen}. English speakers trip over this one for years.
:::

```type
title: Give advice
items:
  - prompt: You should drink more water. (to a friend)
    answer: [Du solltest mehr Wasser trinken., Du solltest mehr trinken.]
  - prompt: You should go to the doctor. (formal)
    answer: [Sie sollten zum Arzt gehen., Sie sollten zur Ärztin gehen., Sie sollten zu einem Arzt gehen.]
  - prompt: We should stay at home.
    answer: [Wir sollten zu Hause bleiben., Wir sollten zuhause bleiben.]
  - prompt: You shouldn't work so much. (to a friend)
    answer: [Du solltest nicht so viel arbeiten.]
  - prompt: You don't have to come. (to a friend)
    answer: [Du musst nicht kommen.]
```

## Gute Besserung!

When you tell someone directly what to do, use the **imperative**, as in lesson 8. Here are the three forms again, now with reflexive and separable verbs. The reflexive pronoun follows the verb, and the separable prefix goes to the end:

| | du | ihr | Sie |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {trinken} | {Trink viel Tee!} | {Trinkt viel Tee!} | {Trinken Sie viel Tee!} |
| {nehmen} | {Nimm eine Tablette!} | {Nehmt eine Tablette!} | {Nehmen Sie eine Tablette!} |
| {sich ausruhen} | {Ruh dich aus!} | {Ruht euch aus!} | {Ruhen Sie sich aus!} |
| {sich beeilen} | {Beeil dich!} | {Beeilt euch!} | {Beeilen Sie sich!} |
| {sich hinlegen} | {Leg dich hin!} | {Legt euch hin!} | {Legen Sie sich hin!} |

Verbs that change e to i in the present keep the i in the du-imperative ({nimm}, {iss}, {gib}); verbs that change a to ä do not ({schlaf}, {fahr}). Add {bitte} to be polite, or {doch} to make it warmly encouraging: {Setz dich doch!} (do sit down!).

And to wish someone well: **{Gute Besserung!}** (get well soon). When someone sneezes, Germans say {Gesundheit!}

```words

```
