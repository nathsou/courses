So far your German sentences have mostly stood alone: one subject, one verb in second place, full stop. Real speech joins ideas together. *I'm late because the bus didn't come. I think that's a good idea. If it rains, we'll stay at home.*

German has a famous rule for this: in a clause that starts with **weil**, **dass**, **wenn** or **ob**, the verb goes to the **end**. Mark Twain joked that a German sentence makes you wait for the verb like a diver waiting to surface. In practice it is a rule you can feel very quickly, and it is the single biggest step from sounding like a phrasebook to sounding like a person.

If you learnt German before 1996, you will also remember {daß} with an ß. It is now spelt **{dass}**. More on that below.

## Warum? Weil …

Fatma has been waiting outside a café in Cologne for twenty minutes.

```dialogue
title: Warum kommst du so spät?
Fatma: Da bist du ja endlich! Warum kommst du so spät? | There you are at last! Why are you so late?
Mehmet: Tut mir leid! Ich habe den Bus verpasst. | Sorry! I missed the bus.
Fatma: Und warum hast du den Bus verpasst? | And why did you miss the bus?
Mehmet: Weil ich meinen Schlüssel nicht gefunden habe. | Because I couldn't find my key.
Fatma: Aha. Und warum hast du deinen Schlüssel nicht gefunden? | I see. And why couldn't you find your key?
Mehmet: Weil ich so spät aufgestanden bin. | Because I got up so late.
Fatma: Und warum bist du so spät aufgestanden? | And why did you get up so late?
Mehmet: Weil mein Wecker nicht geklingelt hat! Es ist also nicht meine Schuld. | Because my alarm clock didn't go off! So it's not my fault.
Fatma: Ich glaube, du brauchst einfach einen neuen Wecker. | I think you just need a new alarm clock.
```

Look at where the verb goes after {weil}:

| main clause: verb second | after weil: verb at the end |
| --- | --- |
| {Mein Wecker hat nicht geklingelt.} | {…, weil mein Wecker nicht geklingelt hat.} |
| {Der Bus hat Verspätung.} | {…, weil der Bus Verspätung hat.} |
| {Ich bin krank.} | {…, weil ich krank bin.} |

{Weil} starts a **subordinate clause** (a {Nebensatz}): a clause that cannot stand on its own as a statement. In every subordinate clause, the **conjugated verb goes to the very end**. The subject usually comes straight after {weil}. And German always puts a **comma** before {weil}, {dass}, {wenn} and {ob}.

:::tip
In conversation you can answer {Warum?} with a {weil}-clause on its own, exactly as Mehmet does: {Warum? – Weil ich krank bin.} In writing, attach it to a main clause.
:::

```words

weil | because
der Grund, ¨-e | reason
verpassen | to miss (a bus, a chance)
der Wecker, - | alarm clock
klingeln | to ring
verschlafen (verschläft, verschlief, hat verschlafen) | to oversleep
pünktlich | punctual, on time
die Ausrede, -n | excuse
die Schuld (Sg.) | fault, blame
```

## Weil oder denn?

{Denn} also means "because", but it is a different kind of word. It simply links two main clauses, like {und} and {aber}, so the word order after it does **not** change:

- {Ich bleibe zu Hause, **weil** ich krank **bin**.} (verb at the end)
- {Ich bleibe zu Hause, **denn** ich **bin** krank.} (verb second, as normal)

The meaning is the same. {Denn} sounds a little more written, and it cannot answer a question on its own: {Warum? – Weil ich krank bin} works, "Denn ich bin krank" does not.

:::note
In everyday speech you will hear Germans say {weil} with the verb second: {weil ich hab keine Zeit}. It is very common, but it counts as colloquial: in writing, and in exams, put the verb at the end after {weil}.
:::

::connector-lab

```words
denn | because, for (verb stays second)
```

## Separable verbs, modal verbs and the perfect

When the verb goes to the end of the clause, three things happen that are worth seeing side by side:

| main clause | subordinate clause | what happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {Ich stehe früh auf.} | {…, weil ich früh aufstehe.} | the separable verb joins up again |
| {Ich muss arbeiten.} | {…, weil ich arbeiten muss.} | the modal verb comes last, after the infinitive |
| {Ich habe zu viel gegessen.} | {…, weil ich zu viel gegessen habe.} | {haben} or {sein} comes last, after the participle |
| {Ich fühle mich nicht gut.} | {…, weil ich mich nicht gut fühle.} | the reflexive pronoun stays near the front |

The rule underneath is always the same: **only the conjugated verb moves**, and it goes to the very end. Everything else stays in its usual order.

```words

bestehen (besteht, bestand, hat bestanden) | to pass (an exam)
vergessen (vergisst, vergaß, hat vergessen) | to forget
```

## Ich finde, dass …

{Dass} ("that") introduces what you think, know, hope or say. It is also a subordinating word, so the verb goes to the end:

- {Ich finde, dass Deutsch eine schöne Sprache ist.} I think German is a beautiful language.
- {Ich glaube, dass Ben recht hat.} I think Ben is right.
- {Ich hoffe, dass du bald wieder gesund bist.} I hope you'll be well again soon.
- {Ich bin sicher, dass der Zug pünktlich kommt.} I'm sure the train will be on time.
- {Es ist wichtig, dass wir pünktlich sind.} It's important that we're on time.

With verbs of thinking and saying you can leave out {dass}, as English drops "that". Then the second clause is an ordinary main clause, verb second: {Ich glaube, Ben hat recht.} Both are correct; without {dass} is more common in speech.

:::grammar[das or dass?]
Since the spelling reform of 1996, the conjunction is **{dass}**; the old {daß} is gone. It is easy to confuse with **{das}** (the article, or "that" pointing at something). A quick test: if you could replace it with {dieses} or {welches}, write {das}; otherwise write {dass}. {Ich weiß, dass das Buch gut ist.} I know that the book is good.
:::

```words

meinen | to think, to be of the opinion; to mean
hoffen | to hope
sicher | sure, certain; safe
die Meinung, -en | opinion
recht haben | to be right
wahrscheinlich | probably
```

## Wenn ich Zeit habe, …

{Wenn} means "if" (a condition) or "whenever" (something that keeps happening). It works exactly like {weil}:

- {Ich komme mit, **wenn** ich Zeit **habe**.} I'll come along if I have time.
- {**Wenn** es regnet, **bleiben** wir zu Hause.} If it rains, we stay at home.

Now look at the second sentence. When the subordinate clause comes **first**, the whole clause counts as the first element of the sentence, so the main verb comes straight after the comma, in second place, and the subject follows it. The result is the typical German pattern **verb, comma, verb**:

| position 1 | verb | the rest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {Heute} | {bleiben} | {wir zu Hause.} |
| {Wenn es regnet,} | {bleiben} | {wir zu Hause.} |
| {Weil ich krank bin,} | {bleibe} | {ich zu Hause.} |

:::mistake
English speakers almost always write {Wenn es regnet, wir bleiben zu Hause}. The main clause still needs its verb in second place, and the {wenn}-clause has used up first place. **{…regnet, bleiben wir…}**: verb, comma, verb.
:::

::word-order{mode="subordinate"}

```words
wenn | if; whenever
```

## Ob: whether

English "if" has two jobs, and German has two words for them. **{wenn}** is "if" as a condition. **{ob}** is "if" in the sense of "whether", for a yes/no question tucked inside a sentence:

- {Ist der Zug pünktlich?} → {Weißt du, **ob** der Zug pünktlich ist?}
- {Kann ich kommen?} → {Ich weiß noch nicht, **ob** ich kommen kann.}
- {Hast du Hunger?} → {Mama fragt, **ob** du Hunger hast.}

And do not confuse either with **{wann}**, the question word "when?": {Wann kommst du?} → {Ich weiß nicht, wann er kommt.}

:::mistake
{Ich weiß nicht, wenn er kommt} does not mean "I don't know if he's coming". If you can say "whether" in English, German needs **{ob}**: {Ich weiß nicht, ob er kommt.}
:::

{Wissen} ("to know a fact") is irregular in the singular, like the modal verbs: {ich weiß}, {du weißt}, {er weiß}, but {wir wissen}, {ihr wisst}, {sie wissen}.

