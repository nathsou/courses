Ask a German speaker about their weekend and you will hear the perfect tense: {Ich habe lange geschlafen}, {Wir sind nach Salzburg gefahren}, {Was ist passiert?} In conversation it is *the* past tense, used where English says "I slept", "we drove", "what happened?". You met it in lesson 11 with the most common verbs. This lesson finishes the job: by the end you can build the participle of almost any verb, including verbs you have never seen, and you can tell a proper story.

If you learnt German at school, you may remember long lists of irregular verbs. You still need some of them, but fewer than you fear: most participles follow a handful of patterns, and one simple rule about stress tells you whether a participle starts with ge- at all.

## Was ist passiert?

```dialogue
title: Mitternacht in Leipzig
Sophie: Mehmet! Es ist Mitternacht! Was ist denn passiert? | Mehmet! It's midnight! What happened?
Mehmet: Frag nicht. Zuerst habe ich den Zug verpasst. | Don't ask. First I missed the train.
Sophie: Oh nein! Und dann? | Oh no! And then?
Mehmet: Dann habe ich eine Stunde am Bahnhof gewartet, und der nächste Zug ist auch zu spät gekommen. | Then I waited at the station for an hour, and the next train was late too.
Sophie: Warum hast du nicht angerufen? | Why didn't you ring?
Mehmet: Ich habe angerufen! Zweimal! | I did ring! Twice!
Sophie: Oh … stimmt, mein Handy war aus. Ich habe den ganzen Abend ferngesehen. | Oh… right, my phone was off. I watched TV all evening.
Mehmet: Und zum Schluss habe ich gemerkt: Mein Schlüssel ist im Büro. Zum Glück bist du noch wach! | And to top it all I realised my key is at the office. Luckily you're still up!
```

The perfect has two parts: a form of {haben} or {sein} in second position, and the **participle** at the very end. Together they make a bracket around the rest of the sentence, and everything else goes inside it:

| Position 1 | Position 2: haben/sein | middle | end: participle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {Ich} | {habe} | {den Zug} | {verpasst}. |
| {Dann} | {habe} | {ich eine Stunde am Bahnhof} | {gewartet}. |
| {Der Zug} | {ist} | {auch zu spät} | {gekommen}. |
| {Warum} | {hast} | {du nicht} | {angerufen}? |

:::mistake
English has two past tenses where spoken German has one. "I missed the train" and "I have missed the train" are both {Ich habe den Zug verpasst}. So the German perfect happily goes with a finished time: {Ich habe ihn gestern gesehen} is perfect German, though "I have seen him yesterday" is wrong in English. Don't let your English instincts talk you out of it.
:::

:::note
Three kinds of verb prefer the simple past (the Präteritum) even in conversation: {sein}, {haben} and the modal verbs. Sophie says {Mein Handy war aus}, not {Mein Handy ist aus gewesen}, and you will say {Ich hatte keine Zeit} rather than {Ich habe keine Zeit gehabt}. The longer versions are not wrong, just heavy. The past of the modal verbs ({konnte}, {musste}) comes in lesson 16.
:::

```words
passieren (passiert, passierte, ist passiert) | to happen
verpassen | to miss (a train, a bus)
das Handy, -s | mobile phone
der Schlüssel, - | key
zum Glück | luckily
endlich | at last, finally
```

## Gemacht or gefunden? Two families

Almost every participle belongs to one of two families.

**Weak (regular) verbs** take **ge-** + stem + **-t**: {machen} → {gemacht}, {kaufen} → {gekauft}, {merken} → {gemerkt}. If the stem ends in -t or -d, an extra e makes the ending audible: {arbeiten} → {gearbeitet}, {warten} → {gewartet}, {kosten} → {gekostet}.

**Strong verbs** take **ge-** + stem + **-en**, and often change their vowel. The vowel changes fall into a few families:

| Vowel | Infinitive → participle |
| --- | --- |
| no change | {fahren} → {gefahren}, {lesen} → {gelesen}, {schlafen} → {geschlafen}, {laufen} → {gelaufen} |
| ei → ie | {schreiben} → {geschrieben}, {bleiben} → {geblieben} |
| i → u | {trinken} → {getrunken}, {finden} → {gefunden}, {singen} → {gesungen} |
| e, i, ie → o | {sprechen} → {gesprochen}, {treffen} → {getroffen}, {nehmen} → {genommen}, {schwimmen} → {geschwommen}, {fliegen} → {geflogen} |
| something else | {gehen} → {gegangen}, {stehen} → {gestanden}, {sitzen} → {gesessen}, {sein} → {gewesen} |

:::tip
English helps more than you might think. Many verbs that are irregular in English are strong in German, often with a similar vowel: drink, drank, drunk and {trinken, trank, getrunken}; sing, sang, sung and {singen, sang, gesungen}; find, found and {finden, gefunden}; swim, swam, swum and {schwimmen, geschwommen}. It is not a rule ({kaufen} is regular although "buy, bought" is not), but it is right far more often than chance.
:::

There is no way to tell from the infinitive alone that a verb is strong: you learn it with the verb. That is why every strong verb in this course comes with its principal parts. Tap {finden} or {treffen} and the word card shows them.

## Angerufen, eingekauft: separable verbs

With a separable verb, the **ge-** goes *between* the prefix and the rest, and the whole participle is written as one word: an|rufen → an·ge·rufen.

| Infinitive | Present | Perfect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {anrufen} | {Ich rufe dich morgen an.} | {Ich habe dich gestern angerufen.} |
| {einkaufen} | {Wir kaufen heute ein.} | {Wir haben heute eingekauft.} |
| {abholen} | {Ich hole Ben ab.} | {Ich habe Ben abgeholt.} |
| {aufstehen} | {Er steht um sechs auf.} | {Er ist um sechs aufgestanden.} |

The base verb decides everything else. {rufen} is strong ({gerufen}), so {anrufen} gives {angerufen}; {kaufen} is weak ({gekauft}), so {einkaufen} gives {eingekauft}. Learn the base verb and its separable relatives come free.

:::grammar[haben or sein can change]
The prefix can change the auxiliary. {Ich habe lange gestanden} (I stood for a long time) takes {haben}, but {Ich bin früh aufgestanden} (I got up early) takes {sein}, because getting up is a change from lying to standing. More on this below.
:::

```words
auf|stehen (steht auf, stand auf, ist aufgestanden) | to get up
an|rufen (ruft an, rief an, hat angerufen) | to ring, to phone
ab|holen | to pick up, to collect
auf|räumen | to tidy up
an|kommen (kommt an, kam an, ist angekommen) | to arrive
ein|laden (lädt ein, lud ein, hat eingeladen) | to invite
```

## Verstanden, bezahlt, studiert: no ge- at all

Here is the rule that explains the exceptions: **a participle only gets ge- if the verb is stressed on its first syllable.** {machen} is stressed on *ma*, so {gemacht}; {anrufen} is stressed on *an*, so {angerufen} (the ge- comes straight after the stressed prefix). Verbs that begin with an unstressed syllable get no ge-. There are two groups.

**1. Inseparable prefixes: be-, ver-, er-, ent-, zer-, ge-, emp-, miss-.** These prefixes are never stressed and never split off, and their verbs have no ge-:

| Prefix | Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| be- | {besuchen} → {besucht}, {bezahlen} → {bezahlt} | {bekommen} → {bekommen}, {beginnen} → {begonnen} |
| ver- | {verpassen} → {verpasst} | {verstehen} → {verstanden}, {vergessen} → {vergessen}, {verlieren} → {verloren} |
| er- | {erzählen} → {erzählt}, {erklären} → {erklärt} | |
| ent-, zer- | {zerstören} → {zerstört} | {entscheiden} → {entschieden} |
| ge-, emp-, miss- | {gehören} → {gehört} | {gefallen} → {gefallen}, {empfehlen} → {empfohlen}, {missverstehen} → {missverstanden} |

**2. Verbs in -ieren.** They are stressed on the -ie-, so they have no ge-, and they are always weak: {studieren} → {studiert}, {telefonieren} → {telefoniert}, {passieren} → {passiert}, {reparieren} → {repariert}, {probieren} → {probiert}.

Strong verbs keep their strong habits without the ge-: a new vowel and -en ({verlieren} → {verloren}). Some participles even look exactly like the infinitive: {Ich habe es vergessen}, {Ich habe ein Paket bekommen}, {Der Film hat mir gefallen}. The {habe} or {hat} in second position tells you it is the perfect.

:::mistake
**{bekommen}** does not mean "become". It means "to get, to receive": {Ich habe eine Nachricht bekommen} is "I got a message". "To become" is {werden}. It is a classic: {Ich bekomme ein Schnitzel} is a perfectly good order in a restaurant (I'll have a schnitzel), but {Ich werde ein Schnitzel} means "I am turning into a schnitzel".
:::

## Gebracht, gedacht, gewusst: mixed verbs

A small group of very common verbs mixes the two families: the weak ending **-t**, but a changed vowel, like a strong verb.

| Infinitive | Präteritum | Participle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {bringen} | {brachte} | {gebracht} |
| {denken} | {dachte} | {gedacht} |
| {wissen} | {wusste} | {gewusst} |
| {kennen} | {kannte} | {gekannt} |

English does exactly the same thing with the same verbs: bring, brought; think, thought. They share their history. Separable relatives follow along: {mitbringen} → {mitgebracht}.

{wissen} is knowing a fact, {kennen} is knowing a person, a place or a thing: {Ich habe das nicht gewusst} (I didn't know that), {Ich habe ihn nicht gekannt} (I didn't know him). Like {sein} and {haben}, {wissen} is often used in the Präteritum in speech too: {Das wusste ich nicht!}

```words
bringen (bringt, brachte, hat gebracht) | to bring; to take
mit|bringen (bringt mit, brachte mit, hat mitgebracht) | to bring (along)
denken (denkt, dachte, hat gedacht) | to think
wissen (weiß, wusste, hat gewusst) | to know (a fact)
kennen (kennt, kannte, hat gekannt) | to know (a person, a place)
```

## haben oder sein?

Most verbs make their perfect with {haben}, and every verb with an accusative object does. A smaller group takes {sein}: verbs with no object that describe something happening *to* the subject, so that afterwards it is somewhere else or something else.

| sein for… | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| movement from A to B | {Ich bin nach Hause gegangen.} {Wir sind nach Basel gefahren.} {Sie ist nach Rom geflogen.} {Er ist in den See gefallen.} |
| a change of state | {Ich bin um sieben aufgewacht.} {Das Baby ist endlich eingeschlafen.} {Du bist aber groß geworden!} |
| three special verbs | {Ich bin in Wien gewesen.} {Wir sind zu Hause geblieben.} {Was ist passiert?} |

Compare {schlafen} and {einschlafen}: {Ich habe gut geschlafen} (a state: {haben}), but {Ich bin sofort eingeschlafen} (falling asleep is a change: {sein}).

And watch {fahren}. Going somewhere is movement: {Ich bin nach Hause gefahren}. But driving *someone* or *something* gives the verb an accusative object, and then it takes {haben}: {Ich habe meine Mutter zum Bahnhof gefahren}.

:::mistake
"I have gone" tempts English speakers to say {Ich habe gegangen}. German does not follow English here: {Ich bin gegangen}. If the sentence gets someone from A to B, think {bin}.
:::

:::culture
In southern Germany, Austria and Switzerland, {stehen}, {sitzen} and {liegen} take {sein} too: {Ich bin zwei Stunden im Zug gesessen}. In the north you hear {Ich habe zwei Stunden im Zug gesessen}. Both are standard German; copy the people around you.
:::

::perfect-builder

## Zuerst, dann, danach: telling a story

A story needs signposts. These words put events in order:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| {zuerst} | first |
| {dann} | then |
| {danach} | after that |
| {später} | later |
| {plötzlich} | suddenly |
| {zum Glück} / {leider} | luckily / unfortunately |
| {zum Schluss} | in the end, finally |

Each of them takes position 1, so the verb ({habe}, {bin}) comes straight after it and the subject moves behind the verb: {Dann habe ich gewartet}, never {Dann ich habe gewartet}. The one exception is {und}, which stands outside the sentence and does not count: {Und dann bin ich nach Hause gegangen.}

:::mistake
{Zuerst ich bin aufgestanden} is the most common mistake in learners' stories. German keeps the verb in second place, whatever comes first: {Zuerst bin ich aufgestanden.}
:::

```words
zuerst | first, first of all
danach | after that, afterwards
später | later
zum Schluss | in the end, finally
plötzlich | suddenly
die Geschichte, -n | story; history
```
