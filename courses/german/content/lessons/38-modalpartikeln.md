Compare these two requests from a flatmate:

- {Komm her.}
- {Komm doch mal her.}

Same meaning, very different feel. The first sounds like an order; the second like a friendly invitation. The two little words that make the difference, {doch} and {mal}, are **modal particles** (Modalpartikeln), and spoken German is full of them. They have no dictionary meaning you can translate; they show the speaker's attitude: surprise, impatience, reassurance, a shared assumption, a softening "go on".

Many learners never use them, and that is one reason learners sound brusque in German even when their grammar is perfect. You will not master particles in one lesson (Germans acquire them over years), but you can learn the ten most common, start using three or four, and stop being puzzled when you hear the rest.

## What particles do

Most particles are ordinary words with a second life. {doch} is also "but" and "yes (contradicting)"; {ja} is "yes"; {denn} is "because"; {mal} comes from {einmal}; {eben} also means "flat" and "just now". As particles they behave differently:

- they are **unstressed** (with a few exceptions noted below),
- they sit in the **middle field**, after the verb and usually after pronouns, never in first position,
- they can be left out without changing the facts, only the tone.

{Das ist ja interessant!} {Wo wohnst du denn?} {Komm doch mit!} In each case the sentence would be grammatical without the particle, but it would sound flatter, or ruder.

## ja and doch

**ja** says "as we both know" or "look at that!":

- {Du weißt ja, dass ich am Montag keine Zeit habe.} You know I'm busy on Monday (I've told you).
- {Sie ist ja Ärztin, sie muss es wissen.} She's a doctor, after all.
- {Du bist ja ganz nass!} You're soaking wet! (surprise at something you can see)

**doch** says "surely you know / you should agree", or, in an imperative, "go on":

- {Das habe ich dir doch gestern gesagt!} I told you yesterday! (reproach or reminder)
- {Das ist doch Unsinn!} That's nonsense, surely! (indignation)
- {Du kommst doch morgen, oder?} You are coming tomorrow, aren't you?
- {Komm doch mit!} {Setzen Sie sich doch!} Why don't you come along? Do sit down! (encouragement)

The difference is subtle: **ja** treats something as shared knowledge; **doch** treats it as something the other person seems to have forgotten or doubted.

:::note
Stressed, both words change again. {Mach das JA nicht!} is a warning: "don't you dare". And you already know stressed {DOCH} as the "yes" that contradicts a negative question: {Kommst du nicht mit? – Doch!}
:::

## mal, denn, eigentlich

**mal** (from {einmal}) softens requests and suggestions. It is the most useful particle of all:

- {Kannst du mal kurz helfen?} {Schau mal!} {Sag mal, …} (Tell me… / Hang on…)
- {Ich muss mal telefonieren.} I just need to make a call.

**denn** lives only in **questions**. It adds friendly interest ({Wie geht's dir denn?}), or surprise and a touch of reproach ({Was ist denn hier los?} {Hast du denn keinen Schirm dabei?}). Questions without it are not wrong, but in conversation {denn} is very frequent.

**eigentlich** in a question changes the subject or asks something you have been wondering about, like English "by the way" or "actually": {Wie spät ist es eigentlich?} {Was machst du eigentlich beruflich?} In a statement it means "actually, in principle": {Eigentlich wollte ich heute joggen, aber es regnet.}

:::mistake
{denn} as a particle never appears in a statement. {Ich habe denn keine Zeit} is wrong; in a statement {denn} can only be the conjunction "because" at the start of a clause: {Ich bleibe zu Hause, denn ich habe keine Zeit.}
:::


## eben, halt, schon, wohl, bloß

**eben** and **halt** both say "that's just how it is; nothing to be done". {halt} is especially southern (Bavaria, Austria, Switzerland) but has spread everywhere; {eben} is more northern and slightly more formal.

- {Das ist halt so.} / {Das ist eben so.} That's just the way it is.
- {Dann nehmen wir halt den Bus.} Well, we'll just take the bus then.

**schon** reassures ({Das wird schon klappen.} It'll work out, you'll see), concedes ({Das ist schon schön hier, aber sehr teuer.} It is nice here, but…) or presses ({Jetzt komm schon!} Come on!).

**wohl** marks an assumption: {Er ist wohl krank.} He's probably ill, I suppose. {Das wird wohl nichts.} That's not going to work, I guess.

**bloß** and **nur** add urgency: a warning ({Mach bloß keinen Fehler!}), a puzzled question ({Wo habe ich bloß meinen Schlüssel hingelegt?}), or a wish ({Wenn ich nur mehr Zeit hätte!}).

Two more you will hear: **etwa** in a question expects or fears the answer yes ({Hast du etwa mein Bier getrunken?} You haven't drunk my beer, have you?), and **ruhig** gives permission ({Du kannst ruhig das Fenster aufmachen.} Feel free to open the window).

When particles combine, they come in a fixed order, roughly **ja – denn – doch – eben/halt – mal**: {Komm doch mal vorbei!} {Was machst du denn eigentlich hier?}

## Er muss krank sein: modal verbs and certainty

At B1 you used modal verbs for ability, obligation and permission. They have a second job: showing **how sure the speaker is**, or **where the information comes from**. This is called their subjective use.

| sentence | meaning | certainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {Er muss krank sein.} | I'm practically sure he's ill. | very high |
| {Er dürfte krank sein.} | He's probably ill. | high |
| {Er wird wohl krank sein.} | I expect he's ill. | high |
| {Er kann / könnte krank sein.} | He may / might be ill. | possible |
| {Er kann nicht krank sein.} | He can't be ill. | (impossible) |
| {Er soll krank sein.} | He's said to be ill. (I heard it) | hearsay |
| {Er will krank gewesen sein.} | He claims he was ill. | his claim |

Note {dürfte} (Konjunktiv II of {dürfen}): in this sense it has nothing to do with permission. And {sollen} and {wollen} are the spoken cousins of Konjunktiv I: {Er soll krank sein} reports what others say; {Er will krank gewesen sein} reports what *he* says, with a hint of disbelief.

**About the past**, the modal stays in the present and the main verb goes into the **perfect infinitive** (participle + {haben}/{sein}):

- {Er muss den Zug verpasst haben.} He must have missed the train.
- {Sie soll gestern abgereist sein.} She is said to have left yesterday.
- {Er will nichts gesehen haben.} He claims not to have seen anything.

:::mistake
Don't confuse {Er musste den Zug nehmen} (he had to take the train: an obligation in the past) with {Er muss den Zug genommen haben} (he must have taken the train: my conclusion now). English "must have" is always the second.
:::

## Hab, is, 'ne: German as it is spoken

Relaxed spoken German, and the chat messages that imitate it, shorten words in regular ways:

| written | spoken / chat | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {ich habe}, {ich gehe} | {ich hab}, {ich geh} | the -e of the ich-form drops |
| {ist}, {nicht} | {is}, {nich} | final -t drops |
| {eine}, {einen}, {ein} | {'ne}, {'nen}, {'n} | articles shrink |
| {hast du}, {kannst du}, {willst du} | {haste}, {kannste}, {willste} | the verb swallows {du} |
| {Wie geht es?}, {Gibt es …?} | {Wie geht's?}, {Gibt's …?} | {es} becomes 's |
| {auf dem}, {so ein} | {aufm}, {so'n} | prepositions and articles merge |
| {nein} | {nee} | |

And people tag their statements with a little question: **ne?** (northern Germany), **gell?** (south-west Germany and Austria; in Austria also {gö?}), **oder?** (Switzerland and the south) and the neutral, slightly formal **nicht wahr?** {Schönes Wetter heute, ne?}

:::tip
Recognise all of these; use them when you speak casually, if you like. In **writing**, keep them to chat messages with friends. In an email to a landlord, a colleague you say Sie to, or an exam, write {Ich habe}, {eine} and {Hast du}.
:::

## In the kitchen

```dialogue
title: Morgens in Stuttgart
Mama: Noah, steh doch endlich auf! Es ist schon halb acht. | Noah, get up, will you! It's already half past seven.
Noah: Ja, ja, ich komm ja schon. | Yes, yes, I'm coming.
Mama: Wo ist denn eigentlich deine Sporttasche? | Where's your sports bag, anyway?
Noah: Die liegt wohl noch im Auto. | It's probably still in the car.
Mama: Na toll. Dann musst du halt ohne Sportsachen in die Schule. | Great. Then you'll just have to go to school without your sports kit.
Noah: Kannst du mich nicht schnell mal fahren? | Can't you just give me a quick lift?

Noah: Schon gut, schon gut. Aber gib mir bloß noch fünf Minuten. | All right, all right. But just give me five more minutes.
Mama: Na gut. Aber nimm wenigstens ein Brot mit, gell? | Fine. But at least take a sandwich with you, OK?
```

Almost every line has a particle, and none of them could be translated word for word. {ich komm ja schon}: as you can see, I'm on my way (stop nagging). {Wo ist denn eigentlich…}: by the way, and I'm a bit surprised. {wohl}: I assume. {halt}: that's how it is now. {doch nicht dein Ernst}: you surely can't mean that. {ja zwei gesunde Beine}: as we both know. And the Swabian mother's {gell?} closes the deal.
