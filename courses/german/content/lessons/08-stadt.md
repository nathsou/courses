Almost every town in the German-speaking world is built on the same plan: a {Bahnhof}, a {Marktplatz} or {Rathausplatz} in the old centre, a bakery on every other corner, and buses or trams that run to a timetable (in Switzerland, to the minute; in Germany, let's say usually). Learn a dozen words and a handful of phrases, and you can find your way round any of them.

This lesson also gives you your first look at the **dative**, the third of German's four cases. Don't panic: we take only what you need to get around, a few short phrases like {mit dem Bus}, {zum Bahnhof} and {in der Stadt}. The full story comes in lesson 14.

A few notes:

- {die Ampel} is singular: one set of traffic lights.
- A German {Apotheke} sells medicines and little else. For shampoo, toothpaste and the like you go to a {Drogerie}.
- {die Bank} (the bank, plural {Banken}) has a twin: {die Bank} (the bench, plural {Bänke}).
- The main station of a city is {der Hauptbahnhof}, written **Hbf** on signs.

## Getting around: gehen or fahren?

```words
der Bus, -se | bus
die U-Bahn, -en | underground, tube
die Straßenbahn, -en | tram
der Zug, ¨-e | train
das Auto, -s | car
das Fahrrad, ¨-er | bicycle
fahren (fährt, fuhr, ist gefahren) | to go (by vehicle), to drive, to ride
zu Fuß | on foot
die Arbeit, -en | work
```

English "go" covers everything. German splits it: {gehen} is to go **on foot**; {fahren} is to go **in or on anything with wheels**, from a bike to a train.

- {Ich gehe zu Fuß.} I'm walking.
- {Ich fahre mit dem Bus.} I'm going by bus.
- {Ich fahre mit dem Fahrrad zur Arbeit.} I cycle to work.

{fahren} is a vowel-change verb: **a** becomes **ä** in the du and er/sie/es forms: {du fährst}, {er fährt}, but {ihr fahrt}.

:::mistake
{Ich gehe mit dem Bus} is one of the most common mistakes English speakers make. As soon as there's a vehicle, it's {fahren}. (In everyday speech {Ich gehe in die Stadt} is fine even if you take the tram: the focus is on where you're going, not how.)
:::

:::culture
In Vienna the tram is affectionately {die Bim}, after the sound of its bell. In Switzerland it is {das Tram}, neuter, and a bicycle is {das Velo}. The Swiss are among the world's keenest train travellers, and their national railway is famous for its punctuality.
:::

## mit dem Bus, zum Bahnhof: a first look at the dative

After certain prepositions, German articles change. Here are the six you need in town:

| Preposition | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **mit** | with, by (transport) | {mit dem Bus}, {mit der U-Bahn} |
| **zu** | to (a place or a person) | {zum Bahnhof}, {zur Post} |
| **bei** | at (someone's home or business), near | {bei Lena}, {beim Bahnhof} |
| **von** | from | {vom Bahnhof}, {von der Arbeit} |
| **in** | in (where?) | {im Park}, {in der Stadt} |
| **an** | at, by (where?) | {am Bahnhof}, {an der Ampel} |

```words
mit (+ D) | with; by (transport)
zu (+ D) | to
bei (+ D) | at (someone's place); near
von (+ D) | from; of
```

After them, the noun is in the **dative** case, and the articles look like this:

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {der Bus} | {die U-Bahn} | {das Auto} | {die Züge} |
| dative: the | {dem Bus} | **{der U-Bahn}** | {dem Auto} | {den Zügen} |
| dative: a | {einem Bus} | {einer U-Bahn} | {einem Auto} | {Zügen} |

The surprise is the feminine: {die} becomes {der}. Masculine and neuter share {dem}, and the plural takes {den} plus an extra -n on the noun.

Some of these prepositions melt into the article, and in everyday German the short forms are the normal ones:

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {zu dem} → **{zum}** | {zu der} → **{zur}** | {von dem} → **{vom}** |
| {in dem} → **{im}** | {an dem} → **{am}** | {bei dem} → **{beim}** |

:::tip
{zum} or {zur}? Ask the gender. {der Bahnhof} → {zum Bahnhof}, {das Kino} → {zum Kino}, but {die Post} → {zur Post}. Only feminine nouns get {zur}.
:::

:::note
For towns and countries, "to" is {nach}: {Ich fahre nach Berlin}, {nach Österreich}. And {nach Hause} means "home" (where to?), {zu Hause} "at home" (where?). Lesson 15 sorts out all the ways of saying "to".
:::

## Wo or wohin? in der Stadt, in die Stadt

German asks two different questions where English often uses one: {Wo?} (where? a location) and {Wohin?} (where to? a direction). With {in} and {an}, the answer changes the case:

| | **wo?** (location: dative) | **wohin?** (direction: accusative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {der Park} | {Ich bin im Park.} | {Ich gehe in den Park.} |
| {die Stadt} | {Ich bin in der Stadt.} | {Ich fahre in die Stadt.} |
| {das Kino} | {Ich bin im Kino.} | {Ich gehe ins Kino.} |

{ins} is {in das}, as {im} is {in dem}.

:::grammar[A photo or a film?]
Think of {wo?} as a photo: you are in a place, still. Think of {wohin?} as a film: you are moving into it. The photo takes the dative ({im Kino}), the film the accusative ({ins Kino}). {mit}, {zu}, {bei} and {von} don't play this game: they always take the dative, so it's {zum Bahnhof} even though you are moving. In lesson 15 you will meet seven more prepositions that work like {in} and {an}.
:::

In speech, {wohin} often splits: {Wo fährst du hin?} is as common as {Wohin fährst du?}

## Asking the way

```words
Wie komme ich zum …? | How do I get to the …?
in der Nähe | nearby
geradeaus | straight on
links | (on the) left
rechts | (on the) right
weit | far
```

| Asking | |
| --- | --- |
| {Entschuldigung, wie komme ich zum Bahnhof?} | Excuse me, how do I get to the station? |
| {Entschuldigung, wo ist hier eine Apotheke?} | Excuse me, where's a pharmacy round here? |
| {Gibt es hier in der Nähe eine Bank?} | Is there a bank near here? |
| {Ist das weit?} | Is it far? |

| Understanding the answer | |
| --- | --- |
| {Gehen Sie geradeaus.} | Go straight on. |
| {Nehmen Sie die erste Straße links.} | Take the first street on the left. |
| {Gehen Sie an der Ampel rechts.} | Turn right at the traffic lights. |
| {Gehen Sie bis zur Kreuzung.} | Go as far as the crossroads. |
| {Das ist nicht weit, nur fünf Minuten zu Fuß.} | It isn't far, only five minutes on foot. |
| {Das ist zu weit zu Fuß. Nehmen Sie den Bus.} | It's too far to walk. Take the bus. |

:::tip
Directions come fast. Repeat them back, it's what Germans do too: {Also geradeaus und an der Ampel links?} The other person will confirm with {Genau!} (exactly) or correct you.
:::

```dialogue
title: Wo ist der Bahnhof?

Frau Keller: Zum Bahnhof? Gehen Sie hier geradeaus bis zur Ampel. | To the station? Go straight on here as far as the traffic lights.
Frau Keller: An der Ampel gehen Sie links, und dann nehmen Sie die zweite Straße rechts. | At the lights turn left, and then take the second street on the right.
Mehmet: Also an der Ampel links und dann die zweite Straße rechts. Ist das weit? | So left at the lights and then the second right. Is it far?

Mehmet: Ich gehe zu Fuß. Vielen Dank! | I'll walk. Thank you very much!

```

## Geh! Gehen Sie! The imperative

All those directions were **imperatives**, the form for telling people what to do. German has three, one for each word for "you".

- **Sie**: the verb first, then {Sie}. It looks like a question, but the voice goes down: {Gehen Sie geradeaus!} {Nehmen Sie den Bus!}
- **ihr**: the ihr form without {ihr}: {Geht geradeaus!} {Nehmt den Bus!}
- **du**: the du form without {du} and without -st: {du gehst} → {Geh!}, {du kommst} → {Komm!}

| | du | ihr | Sie |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {gehen} | {Geh!} | {Geht!} | {Gehen Sie!} |
| {kommen} | {Komm!} | {Kommt!} | {Kommen Sie!} |
| {nehmen} | **{Nimm!}** | {Nehmt!} | {Nehmen Sie!} |
| {essen} | **{Iss!}** | {Esst!} | {Essen Sie!} |
| {fahren} | **{Fahr!}** | {Fahrt!} | {Fahren Sie!} |
| {warten} | **{Warte!}** | {Wartet!} | {Warten Sie!} |
| {aussteigen} | {Steig aus!} | {Steigt aus!} | {Steigen Sie aus!} |
| {sein} | **{Sei!}** | **{Seid!}** | **{Seien Sie!}** |

The details are in the bold forms:

- Verbs that change **e** to **i** or **ie** keep it in the du imperative: {Nimm!} {Iss!} {Sieh mal!} Verbs that change **a** to **ä** do not: {Fahr!}, never "Fähr!".
- Verbs whose stem ends in -t or -d add -e: {Warte!} {Arbeite!} Others may add it too ({Gehe!}, {Komme!}), but it sounds formal or old-fashioned.
- Separable verbs send their prefix to the end, as always: {Steig am Rathaus aus!} {Kommt mit!}
- {sein} is irregular: {Sei leise!} {Seid pünktlich!} {Seien Sie bitte vorsichtig!}

A bare imperative can sound brusque. {bitte} softens it, and so does the little word {mal}: {Nimm bitte den Bus.} {Komm mal her!}
