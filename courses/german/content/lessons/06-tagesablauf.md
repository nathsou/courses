Ask someone in Hamburg the time at 2:30 and you may hear {halb drei}. To a British ear that is "half three", 3:30. To a German it is "half (the way) to three": 2:30. That one phrase has made more English speakers miss more trains than anything else in the language. By the end of this lesson it won't catch you.

You will also describe your day, from getting up to switching off the television, and meet two things that make German sentences look German: **separable verbs** ({Ich stehe um sieben auf}) and the **verb-second rule** ({Um sieben stehe ich auf}). If you learnt German at school, you know both by sight. This time you will know why they work.

## Wie spät ist es?

```words
Wie spät ist es? | What time is it?
die Uhr, -en | clock, watch; o'clock
die Stunde, -n | hour
die Minute, -n | minute
das Viertel, - | quarter
halb | half
kurz | short; just (before or after)
```

German has two ways of telling the time, and you need both.

**The official way** uses the 24-hour clock: hours, then {Uhr}, then minutes. It is what you hear on the radio, at the station and when you make an appointment: {Es ist vierzehn Uhr dreißig.} (14:30) {Der Zug fährt um neunzehn Uhr fünf.} (19:05)

**The everyday way** uses the 12-hour clock with {nach} (past), {vor} (to), {Viertel} and {halb}:

| | everyday | official |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3:00 | {drei Uhr}, {drei} | {drei Uhr}, {fünfzehn Uhr} |
| 3:05 | {fünf nach drei} | {drei Uhr fünf} |
| 3:15 | {Viertel nach drei} | {drei Uhr fünfzehn} |
| 3:20 | {zwanzig nach drei} | {drei Uhr zwanzig} |
| 3:25 | {fünf vor halb vier} | {drei Uhr fünfundzwanzig} |
| 3:30 | **{halb vier}** | {drei Uhr dreißig} |
| 3:35 | {fünf nach halb vier} | {drei Uhr fünfunddreißig} |
| 3:45 | {Viertel vor vier} | {drei Uhr fünfundvierzig} |
| 3:50 | {zehn vor vier} | {drei Uhr fünfzig} |
| 3:58 | {kurz vor vier} | {drei Uhr achtundfünfzig} |

:::key
{halb} looks **forward** to the next hour: {halb vier} is 3:30, "half towards four". Around the half hour, Germans count from it: {fünf vor halb vier} (3:25), {fünf nach halb vier} (3:35).
:::

:::mistake
1:00 is {ein Uhr} or simply {eins}, never "eins Uhr": {Es ist eins.} {Wir essen um ein Uhr.}
:::

:::culture
In Austria, Bavaria and much of eastern Germany you will also hear {viertel vier} for 3:15 and {dreiviertel vier} for 3:45. It is the same logic as {halb vier}: you count towards the coming hour. If someone in Vienna says {um viertel acht}, be there at 7:15.
:::

To ask the time, say {Wie spät ist es?} or {Wie viel Uhr ist es?} To ask when something happens: {Um wie viel Uhr …?} or simply {Wann …?}

## Days, months and um, am, im

```words
der Tag, -e | day
die Woche, -n | week
das Wochenende, -n | weekend
der Montag, -e | Monday
der Dienstag, -e | Tuesday
der Mittwoch, -e | Wednesday
der Donnerstag, -e | Thursday
der Freitag, -e | Friday
der Samstag, -e | Saturday
der Sonntag, -e | Sunday
der Morgen, - | morning
der Vormittag, -e | (late) morning
der Mittag, -e | midday, noon
der Nachmittag, -e | afternoon
der Abend, -e | evening
die Nacht, ¨-e | night
```

All the days are masculine, and so are the parts of the day, except {die Nacht}. {Mittwoch} is simply "mid-week". In northern and eastern Germany Saturday is also called {der Sonnabend}.

The months are masculine too, and you can probably read them already:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {der Januar} | {der Februar} | {der März} | {der April} |
| {der Mai} | {der Juni} | {der Juli} | {der August} |
| {der September} | {der Oktober} | {der November} | {der Dezember} |

In Austria January is {der Jänner}. And because {Juni} and {Juli} sound alike on the phone, Germans often say {Juno} and {Julei} to keep them apart.

Which little word goes before a time? It depends on the kind of time:

| | for | examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **um** | clock times | {um acht Uhr}, {um halb drei} |
| **am** | days and parts of the day | {am Montag}, {am Abend}, {am Wochenende} |
| **im** | months and seasons | {im Juli}, {im Winter} |
| **in der** | the night | {in der Nacht} |
| (nothing) | years | {2026}, {im Jahr 2026} |

:::mistake
"In 2026" is English, not German. Say simply {2026} ({Ich bin 2026 in Wien.}) or {im Jahr 2026}.
:::

:::tip
Three words that look alike: {morgen} (small m) means **tomorrow**; {am Morgen} means **in the morning**; and {morgens} means **in the mornings, every morning**. The -s turns any day or part of the day into a habit: {abends} (in the evenings), {montags} (on Mondays), {sonntags} (on Sundays).
:::

## Mein Tag: separable verbs

```words
auf|stehen (steht auf, stand auf, ist aufgestanden) | to get up
frühstücken | to have breakfast
duschen | to shower
an|fangen (fängt an, fing an, hat angefangen) | to begin, to start
ein|kaufen | to go shopping, to shop
an|rufen (ruft an, rief an, hat angerufen) | to phone, to call
mit|kommen (kommt mit, kam mit, ist mitgekommen) | to come along
fern|sehen (sieht fern, sah fern, hat ferngesehen) | to watch TV
schlafen (schläft, schlief, hat geschlafen) | to sleep
gehen (geht, ging, ist gegangen) | to go; to walk
```

Here is Jonas's morning:

- {Ich stehe um sieben auf.} I get up at seven.
- {Ich dusche und frühstücke.} I have a shower and breakfast.
- {Die Arbeit fängt um neun an.} Work starts at nine.

The verb is {aufstehen}, but in the sentence it splits: {stehe} sits in second place, and {auf} goes to the very **end**, however long the sentence is: {Ich stehe am Montag immer um sechs Uhr auf.} English does something similar with "get up" and "ring up", but German sends the little word all the way to the end.

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {ich stehe auf} | I get up | {wir stehen auf} | we get up |
| {du stehst auf} | you get up | {ihr steht auf} | you get up |
| {er/sie/es steht auf} | he/she/it gets up | {sie/Sie stehen auf} | they get up / you get up |

How to spot a separable verb:

- The prefix is a little word that also exists on its own ({auf}, {an}, {ein}, {mit}, {fern}) and it carries the **stress**: AUFstehen, EINkaufen, FERNsehen. The word lists show it with a bar: {auf|stehen}.
- Unstressed prefixes such as be-, ver- and er- never separate: {bezahlen} → {Ich bezahle.} {verstehen} → {Ich verstehe.}
- Vowel changes happen in the verb part as usual: {fernsehen} → {er sieht fern}; {anfangen} → {er fängt an}.
- Questions work the same way: {Wann stehst du auf?} {Kommst du mit?}

:::mistake
{Ich aufstehe} and {Ich stehe auf um sieben} are the two classic errors. The verb part stays in second place, and the prefix closes the sentence: {Ich stehe um sieben auf.}
:::

## Verb second: Um sieben stehe ich auf

Here is the most important rule of German word order: in a statement, **the verb is the second element**. Not the second word: the second element. The first element can be the subject, but it can just as well be a time, a place or an object. When something else comes first, the subject moves to just after the verb.

| 1 | 2: verb | 3 | … | end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {Ich} | {stehe} | {um sieben} | | {auf.} |
| {Um sieben} | {stehe} | {ich} | | {auf.} |
| {Am Sonntag} | {schlafe} | {ich} | {lange.} | |
| {Dann} | {frühstücke} | {ich.} | | |

Starting with the time is extremely common, because it is how Germans tell a story: {Um sieben stehe ich auf. Dann dusche ich. Um acht gehe ich los.}

:::mistake
English happily says "At seven I get up", with the subject before the verb. German can't: {Um sieben ich stehe auf} is wrong. Think of the verb as a hinge: whatever comes first, the verb comes next.
:::

:::grammar[What about und and aber?]
{und}, {aber} and {oder} join sentences without counting as an element: {Ich stehe auf, und dann dusche ich.} After {und}, {dann} is element 1 and {dusche} is element 2. And if you remember "time, manner, place" from school: it is a good default for the *middle* of a sentence ({Ich fahre morgen mit dem Bus in die Stadt}), but the verb-second rule is the one that never bends.
:::

## Wie oft? Always, often, never

```words
immer | always
meistens | usually, mostly
oft | often
manchmal | sometimes
selten | rarely, seldom
nie | never
morgens | in the mornings
abends | in the evenings
dann | then
früh | early
spät | late
```

From always to never: **{immer}** → **{meistens}** → **{oft}** → **{manchmal}** → **{selten}** → **{nie}**.

In a simple sentence the frequency word comes straight after the verb, or after the subject when the subject follows the verb: {Ich frühstücke nie.} {Ich trinke oft Kaffee.} {Morgens trinke ich immer Kaffee.} Like any element, it can also come first, and then the verb-second rule applies: {Manchmal koche ich.}

For a regular rhythm, use {jeden Tag} (every day), {jeden Morgen}, {jeden Abend} or {einmal pro Woche} (once a week).

{die Semmel} is the Austrian and Bavarian bread roll, and {das Kipferl} is the Austrian crescent, the ancestor of the French croissant (or so the Viennese say).
