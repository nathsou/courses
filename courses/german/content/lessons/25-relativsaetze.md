{Das ist der Mann, der neben uns wohnt.} {Das ist die Wohnung, die ich gemietet habe.}

English has a whole toolbox for this: "who", "which", "that", "whom", "whose", or nothing at all ("the flat I rented"). German has one tool, and you know it already: {der}, {die}, {das}. A relative clause hangs extra information on a noun: the man **who lives next door**, the flat **I rented**, the woman **whose bag I found**. Real German texts are full of them, and once you can build them, your sentences stop sounding like a list.

This lesson is set around things people lose and the places that find them again: lost-property offices in Vienna and Zürich, a party in Berlin, and a few proverbs that are older than all of them.

## Der Mann, der …: the basic idea

Here are two sentences, and the same information in one:

- {Ich habe eine Mitbewohnerin. Sie spricht fünf Sprachen.}
- {Ich habe eine Mitbewohnerin, **die** fünf Sprachen spricht.}

The relative pronoun {die} does two jobs: it points back to {Mitbewohnerin}, and it starts a subordinate clause. Four rules cover almost everything:

1. The relative pronoun looks like the definite article: {der}, {die}, {das}, plural {die}.
2. Its **gender and number** come from the noun it refers to: {Mitbewohnerin} is feminine, so {die}.
3. Its **case** comes from its job **inside the relative clause** (next section).
4. A relative clause is a subordinate clause: the verb goes to the end, and commas mark it off.

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {der Bus, der …} | {die Frau, die …} | {das Kind, das …} | {die Leute, die …} |

:::mistake
English can leave the pronoun out: "the film I saw". German never can: {der Film, den ich gesehen habe}. And English "that" covers every gender; German must choose. If you catch yourself writing {der Film, ich gesehen habe} or {die Frau, das …}, stop and ask: which noun, and which gender?
:::

The last item is the whole secret of relative clauses. The case of the noun in the main clause does not matter. Only the pronoun's job in its own clause does.

## Den, dem, denen: the case comes from the clause

{Der Film, **der** gestern im Fernsehen lief, war langweilig.} The film ran: the pronoun is the subject, so nominative.

{Der Film, **den** ich gestern gesehen habe, war langweilig.} I saw it: the pronoun is the object, so accusative.

{Die Frau, **der** ich im Zug geholfen habe, hat mir eine Karte geschrieben.} I helped her ({helfen} + dative): so dative.

Here is the whole table. It is the definite article with **two differences**: the dative plural is {denen}, not {den}, and the genitive has its own forms (next-but-one section).

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {der} | {die} | {das} | {die} |
| accusative | {den} | {die} | {das} | {die} |
| dative | {dem} | {der} | {dem} | **{denen}** |
| genitive | **{dessen}** | **{deren}** | **{dessen}** | **{deren}** |

:::key
Two questions, every time:

1. **Which noun?** That gives you the gender and number: masculine, feminine, neuter or plural.
2. **What job in my clause?** Subject → nominative. Direct object → accusative. After a dative verb ({helfen}, {danken}, {gehören}, {gefallen}) or as indirect object → dative.

Then read the answer off the table.
:::

## Where the clause goes

A relative clause normally stands right after its noun, with a comma before it and, if the main clause carries on, a comma after it too:

{Der Rucksack, den ich verloren habe, war ganz neu.}

But German does not like to keep a listener waiting for a single word at the end. When only a participle or an infinitive would be left over, the relative clause usually moves **behind** it:

- {Ich habe den Rucksack gefunden, den du gesucht hast.} (natural)
- {Ich habe den Rucksack, den du gesucht hast, gefunden.} (correct, but heavier)

Both are correct; in speech the first is far more common.

:::tip
Read your commas aloud as tiny pauses. If a relative clause sits in the middle of a sentence and you only have one comma, you have probably forgotten the second.
:::

## Whose: dessen and deren

{Das ist der Autor, **dessen** Roman ich gerade lese.} That's the author whose novel I'm reading.
{Das ist die Autorin, **deren** Roman ich gerade lese.} That's the author (a woman) whose novel I'm reading.

"Whose" is {dessen} or {deren}, and the rule is the one English uses for "his" and "her": the **owner** decides, not the thing owned.

- masculine or neuter owner → {dessen}: {der Mann, dessen Frau …}, {das Kind, dessen Fahrrad …}
- feminine or plural owner → {deren}: {die Frau, deren Mann …}, {die Nachbarn, deren Katze …}

The noun that follows has **no article**: {dessen Roman}, never {dessen der Roman}. And {dessen} and {deren} never change, whatever the case of that noun: {die Kollegin, mit deren Bruder ich Tennis spiele}.

:::mistake
The classic slip is to make {dessen}/{deren} agree with the thing owned: {die Frau, dessen Mann …} because {Mann} is masculine. Think of {ihr Mann}: the woman owns the husband, so feminine, so {deren}: {die Frau, deren Mann …}.
:::

:::tip
An adjective after {dessen} or {deren} takes the ending it would have with no article at all: {der Mann, dessen alter Hund …}, {die Frau, deren neues Auto …}. It is the same rule you learnt at A2: if no article shows the case, the adjective must.
:::

```words
dessen | whose (for masculine and neuter nouns)
deren | whose (for feminine and plural nouns)
der Gegenstand, ¨-e | object, item
der Inhalt, -e | contents
wertvoll | valuable
```

## With prepositions, and wo for places

{Das ist die Freundin, **mit der** ich in Wien studiert habe.} That's the friend I studied with in Vienna.
{Das Hotel, **in dem** wir gewohnt haben, lag direkt am See.} The hotel we stayed in was right on the lake.

English can leave the preposition dangling at the end ("the friend I studied **with**"). German cannot: the preposition goes **first**, at the start of the relative clause, and it decides the case of the pronoun as usual:

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {mit} + dative | {der Kollege, mit dem ich arbeite} | the colleague I work with |
| {bei} + dative | {die Leute, bei denen ich gewohnt habe} | the people I stayed with |
| {für} + accusative | {die Freundin, für die ich das Geschenk gekauft habe} | the friend I bought the present for |
| {in} + dative | {die Stadt, in der ich aufgewachsen bin} | the town I grew up in |
| {an} + dative | {der Tag, an dem wir uns kennengelernt haben} | the day we met |

For **places**, {wo} can replace {in dem} / {in der}: {die Stadt, wo ich aufgewachsen bin}. After a place name with no article, such as a city or most countries, {wo} is the only choice: {Ich fahre nach Graz, wo meine Schwester wohnt.} Likewise {woher} and {wohin}: {die Stadt, woher ich komme}.

:::mistake
In standard German, {wo} is for places only. For times use {an dem}, {in dem} or {als}: {der Tag, an dem wir uns kennengelernt haben}. You will hear {der Tag, wo …} in conversation, and in southern dialects even {der Mann, wo …}, but neither belongs in writing.
:::

## was and wer

After certain words the relative pronoun is not {das} but **{was}**:

- after **alles, etwas, nichts, vieles, manches**: {Alles, was du brauchst, ist hier.} {Es gibt nichts, was ich lieber esse.}
- after a **neuter superlative or number word used as a noun**: {das Beste, was …}, {das Schönste, was …}, {das Einzige, was …}, {das Erste, was …}
- after a **whole clause**: {Er hat pünktlich angerufen, was selten passiert.} The {was} refers to the fact that he rang on time, not to any one noun.

:::note
After {etwas} you will also see {das}, especially when something quite specific is meant: {etwas, das ich dir zeigen will}. {was} is never wrong there. If the {was} needs a preposition, it turns into a {wo(r)}-word: {Das ist etwas, worauf ich mich freue}. Those come in lesson 29.
:::

**{wer}** means "whoever" or "anyone who", and turns up above all in proverbs. The main clause can pick it up again with {der}, or {was} with {das}:

- {Wer zuerst kommt, mahlt zuerst.} First come, first served (literally: whoever comes first grinds his corn first).
- {Wer rastet, der rostet.} Whoever rests, rusts.
- {Was du heute kannst besorgen, das verschiebe nicht auf morgen.} Don't put off till tomorrow what you can do today. (The word order is old-fashioned, for the rhyme.)

When the two halves need different cases, the second pronoun has to be there: {Wem nicht zu raten ist, dem ist nicht zu helfen.} If someone won't take advice, there's no helping them.

## Die Frau, die alles findet

Here is a magazine portrait in which relative clauses do much of the work. Before you read: lost-property offices in German-speaking cities are busy places, and some hold regular auctions of things nobody collects.

```read

setting: A short portrait from the weekend magazine of a Zürich newspaper.
text: |
  Menschen in Zürich: Die Frau, die alles findet

  Seit 22 Jahren arbeitet Regula Meier im Zürcher Fundbüro. Hier landet alles,
```
