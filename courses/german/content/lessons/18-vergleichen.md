goals:

Is Vienna bigger than Zurich? Is the Zugspitze higher than the Großglockner? (No: the Großglockner wins by more than eight hundred metres.) Comparing is one of the most useful things you can do in a new language, and here German is kinder than English. There is no "more" and "most" for long words: one pattern works for every adjective. {schön}, {schöner}, {am schönsten}; {interessant}, {interessanter}, {am interessantesten}.

Returners usually remember the -er. What tends to go wrong is **als** versus **wie**, the umlauts ({älter}, not "alter"), and the two kinds of superlative ({am schnellsten} and {der schnellste}). This lesson sorts out all three, with a tour of the German-speaking world on the way.

## Schneller, billiger, bequemer

Mia and Ben are in Hamburg and want to go to Berlin for the weekend.

```dialogue
title: Zug oder Bus?
Mia: Fahren wir mit dem Zug oder mit dem Bus nach Berlin? | Shall we go to Berlin by train or by coach?
Ben: Der Bus ist billiger. Er kostet nur 19 Euro. | The coach is cheaper. It only costs 19 euros.
Mia: Ja, aber der Zug ist schneller. Mit dem ICE sind wir in zwei Stunden da. | Yes, but the train is faster. With the ICE we're there in two hours.
Ben: Und der Bus braucht über drei Stunden. Hm. Der Zug ist auch bequemer. | And the coach takes over three hours. Hm. The train is more comfortable too.
Mia: Und ruhiger! Im Bus ist es immer so laut. | And quieter! It's always so noisy on the coach.
Ben: Okay, okay, wir nehmen den Zug. Aber du bezahlst den Kaffee! | OK, OK, we'll take the train. But you're paying for the coffee!
```

To compare two things, add **-er** to the adjective: {billig} → {billiger}, {schnell} → {schneller}, {bequem} → {bequemer}. That is the whole rule, and it applies to long adjectives too:

| adjective | | comparative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {schnell} | fast | {schneller} |
| {billig} | cheap | {billiger} |
| {ruhig} | quiet | {ruhiger} |
| {interessant} | interesting | {interessanter} |
| {gefährlich} | dangerous | {gefährlicher} |
| {teuer} | expensive | {teurer} (the e before r drops out) |
| {dunkel} | dark | {dunkler} (the e before l drops out) |

The same form works as an adverb: {Der Zug fährt schneller.} The train goes faster. {Sprich bitte langsamer!} Please speak more slowly! English sometimes switches to "more …ly"; German never does.

:::mistake
English says "more interesting", "more comfortable", "more dangerous". German never does: **{interessanter}**, **{bequemer}**, **{gefährlicher}**. {Mehr} means "more" of a quantity ({mehr Zeit}, more time), never "more" in front of an adjective. And {teuer} becomes {teurer}, not "teuerer".
:::

```words
schnell | fast, quick
langsam | slow
billig | cheap
teuer (teurer, am teuersten) | expensive
bequem | comfortable
ruhig | quiet, calm
laut | loud, noisy
leicht | light; easy
schwer | heavy; difficult
praktisch | practical
gefährlich | dangerous
heiß | hot
```

## Als oder wie?

German uses two little words for comparing, and English speakers mix them up because English uses "as" for one and "than" for the other:

| | German | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| different | {Berlin ist größer als Wien.} | bigger **than** |
| the same | {Wien ist so schön wie Berlin.} | **as** beautiful **as** |
| not the same | {Bern ist nicht so groß wie Zürich.} | **not as** big **as** |
| exactly the same | {Ich bin genauso alt wie du.} | **just as** old **as** |
| twice | {Österreich ist doppelt so groß wie die Schweiz.} | **twice as** big **as** |

So: a comparative (-er) goes with **als**; the plain adjective with **so … wie**.

:::mistake
You will hear {größer wie} and even {größer als wie} in Germany, especially in speech and in the south. Both count as non-standard: in writing, and in any exam, it is always **-er als**.

And a trap for English ears: "than" sounds like {dann}, but {dann} means "then". "Bigger than" is {größer als}.
:::

After {als} and {wie} a pronoun is in the **nominative**, because it is really the subject of a shortened sentence: {Du bist größer als ich} (… than I am). English says "than me", which tempts learners into {als mich}: that is wrong.

:::tip
Two words make a comparison stronger. **{viel}** means "much": {Zürich ist viel teurer als Wien.} **{noch}** means "even": {Der ICE ist schnell, aber das Flugzeug ist noch schneller.} And **{immer}** + comparative means "more and more": {Es wird immer kälter.} It's getting colder and colder.
:::

```words
als | than
so … wie | as … as
genauso | just as
doppelt | double, twice
vergleichen (vergleicht, verglich, hat verglichen) | to compare
gleich | same; equal
```

## Älter, größer, wärmer: the umlaut

Many short adjectives (one syllable, with **a**, **o** or **u**) add an umlaut as well as -er. These are some of the commonest words in the language, so they are worth learning as pairs:

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {alt} → {älter} | old | {jung} → {jünger} | young |
| {groß} → {größer} | big, tall | {kurz} → {kürzer} | short |
| {lang} → {länger} | long | {warm} → {wärmer} | warm |
| {kalt} → {kälter} | cold | {stark} → {stärker} | strong |
| {schwach} → {schwächer} | weak | {arm} → {ärmer} | poor |
| {klug} → {klüger} | clever | {oft} → {öfter} | often |

Not every short adjective does it, though: {laut} → {lauter}, {voll} → {voller}, {klar} → {klarer}, {toll} → {toller}, and longer words never do ({langsam} → {langsamer}). When you are unsure, the word list (and the word card) gives the forms.

:::grammar[Why the umlaut?]
The umlaut is a fossil. Over a thousand years ago the comparative ending had an i-sound in it, and that i pulled the vowel before it forward in the mouth: a towards ä, o towards ö, u towards ü. English had the same thing once: old → elder, the old comparative of "old". German kept it in its most frequent adjectives, which is why the commonest words are the irregular ones.
:::

```words
alt (älter, am ältesten) | old
jung (jünger, am jüngsten) | young
groß (größer, am größten) | big, tall
lang (länger, am längsten) | long
kurz (kürzer, am kürzesten) | short
warm (wärmer, am wärmsten) | warm
kalt (kälter, am kältesten) | cold
stark (stärker, am stärksten) | strong
schwach (schwächer, am schwächsten) | weak
arm (ärmer, am ärmsten) | poor
klug (klüger, am klügsten) | clever, wise
```

## Gut, besser, am besten: the irregular ones

A handful of adjectives change more than an ending, just as English has good, better, best. The superlative ({am besten}) is the subject of the next section, but it belongs in the table:

| | comparative | superlative | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {gut} | {besser} | {am besten} | good, better, best |
| {viel} | {mehr} | {am meisten} | much, more, most |
| {gern} | {lieber} | {am liebsten} | like doing, prefer, like best |
| {hoch} | {höher} | {am höchsten} | high (the c disappears) |
| {nah} | {näher} | {am nächsten} | near (a c appears) |

{Gern}, {lieber}, {am liebsten} is how German says what you like doing, prefer, and like best: {Ich schwimme gern, ich fahre lieber Rad, aber am liebsten liege ich in der Sonne.}

:::mistake
**{mehr}** and **{weniger}** never take an ending: {mehr Zeit}, {mehr Leute}, {weniger Geld}. Be careful with {mehrere}, which looks related but means "several": {mehrere Leute} is "several people", not "more people".
:::

```words
gut (besser, am besten) | good
viel (mehr, am meisten) | much, a lot
gern (lieber, am liebsten) | gladly, like doing
hoch (höher, am höchsten) | high
nah (näher, am nächsten) | near, close
wenig | little, not much
```

```cloze
    en: The food here is good, but at my grandma's it's better.
  - de: Was machst du am Wochenende [am liebsten](gern)?
    en: What do you like doing best at the weekend?
  - de: Wer isst bei euch [am meisten](viel)? – Mein Bruder Jonas!
    en: Who eats the most in your family? – My brother Jonas!
```

## Am schnellsten: the superlative

To say something is the fastest, cheapest or best, use **am** + adjective + **-sten**:

- {Der Bus ist billig, das Fahrrad ist billiger, aber zu Fuß gehen ist am billigsten.}
- {Der ICE fährt am schnellsten.}
- {Im Juli ist es hier am heißesten.}

After **d, t, s, ß** and **z** an extra e makes it pronounceable: {am ältesten}, {am leichtesten}, {am heißesten}, {am kürzesten}, {am interessantesten}. One famous exception: {groß} → {am größten}.

The umlaut and the irregular forms carry over from the comparative: {älter} → {am ältesten}, {besser} → {am besten}, {höher} → {am höchsten}.

```words
der Berg, -e | mountain
der See, -n | lake
der Fluss, ¨-e | river
das Land, ¨-er | country
die Hauptstadt, ¨-e | capital city
der Einwohner, - | inhabitant
ungefähr | about, roughly
wichtig | important
verdienen | to earn
das Fahrrad, ¨-er | bicycle
```


## Der höchste Berg: superlatives before a noun

The superlative can also stand in front of a noun, like "the highest mountain". Then it is **der/die/das** + adjective + **-ste**:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| {der höchste Berg} | the highest mountain |
| {die größte Stadt} | the biggest city |
| {das älteste Haus} | the oldest house |
| {die schönsten Städte} | the most beautiful cities (plural: -en) |

Those endings, -e and -en, are adjective endings: the subject of the next lesson. For now, the pattern above is all you need in the nominative: **-e** after {der}, {die} and {das}, **-en** in the plural.

After {sein} you can say it either way, and the meaning is the same: {Die Zugspitze ist am höchsten} or {Die Zugspitze ist der höchste Berg in Deutschland}. After any other verb, only {am} works: {Der ICE fährt am schnellsten.}
