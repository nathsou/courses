---
title: 'Hello! The sounds of German'
goals:
  - greet people and say goodbye at any time of day, formally and informally, in Germany, Austria and Switzerland
  - ask how someone is, answer, and say please, thank you and sorry
  - give your name, ask for someone else's, and spell it with the German alphabet
  - read any German word aloud, with the right long and short vowels, ä ö ü, ei and ie, ch, z, w and r
---

German has a reputation for being hard to pronounce. It is not, really. In English, "though", "through" and "tough" all end in the same four letters and sound nothing alike; German is spelt almost exactly as it sounds. Learn the rules in this lesson once and you can read aloud any German word you will ever meet, even {Donaudampfschifffahrt} (Danube steamship navigation).

You will also learn what you need in your first five minutes in any German-speaking country: hello and goodbye, please and thank you, sorry, and how to give your name and spell it.

If you learnt German at school, this is the lesson that fixes the sounds that drifted over the years: the {ei} and {ie} that swapped places, the {w} that became an English w, the {ch} that turned into a k. Ten minutes on sounds now saves years of sounding not-quite-right.

## Guten Tag! Greetings through the day

```words
Hallo | hello, hi
Guten Morgen | good morning
Guten Tag | hello, good day (formal, during the day)
Guten Abend | good evening
Gute Nacht | good night (when going to bed)
Tschüss | bye
Auf Wiedersehen | goodbye (formal)
Bis morgen | see you tomorrow
der Morgen, - | morning
der Tag, -e | day
der Abend, -e | evening
die Nacht, ¨-e | night
```

| | formal: strangers, shops, work | informal: friends, family |
| --- | --- | --- |
| in the morning, until about 10 | {Guten Morgen!} | {Morgen!} or {Hallo!} |
| during the day | {Guten Tag!} | {Hallo!} or {Tag!} |
| from about 6 p.m. | {Guten Abend!} | {Hallo!} |
| going to bed | {Gute Nacht!} | {Gute Nacht!} or {Nacht!} |
| saying goodbye | {Auf Wiedersehen!} | {Tschüss!} {Bis morgen!} |

{Guten Morgen}, {Guten Tag} and {Guten Abend} are short for "I wish you a good morning, day, evening", which is why {gut} has the ending -en. {Nacht} belongs to a different gender, so it is {Gute Nacht}. You will see why in lesson 5; for now, learn them as they are.

:::key
Look at {Tag}, {Morgen}, {Abend} and {Nacht}: every German noun starts with a capital letter, wherever it stands in the sentence. It is the easiest rule in the language, and it helps you read: a capital in mid-sentence means "this is a thing".
:::

:::mistake
{Gute Nacht} is only for parting late in the evening, or going to bed. When you arrive at a dinner party at nine, you say {Guten Abend!}. Likewise, {Guten Tag} is only a greeting: when you leave, it is {Auf Wiedersehen} or {Tschüss}.
:::

German is spoken in three countries (and parts of three more), and every region has its favourite greeting:

```words
Servus | hi; bye (Bavaria and Austria, informal)
Grüß Gott | hello (southern Germany and Austria)
Grüezi | hello (Switzerland, formal)
Moin | hi (northern Germany, at any time of day)
```

- **{Moin!}** is the north: Hamburg, Bremen, Kiel. It works at any hour, even at ten at night. Northerners joke that {Moin moin!} is already chatty.
- **{Grüß Gott!}** ("may God greet you") is said by everybody in Bavaria and Austria, religious or not. Say it in a Munich bakery and you sound local.
- **{Servus!}** (from Latin, "your servant") is the friendly hello *and* goodbye of Bavaria and Austria, among people on first-name terms.
- **{Grüezi!}** is the Swiss hello to someone you do not know well; to a group it is {Grüezi mitenand!}, and friends say {Hoi!} or {Sali!}.

{Hallo}, {Guten Tag} and {Tschüss} are understood everywhere, so you can never go wrong with them.

:::culture
In Austria the formal goodbye is usually {Auf Wiederschauen!}, often just {Wiederschauen!}; in Vienna friends say {Baba!}. The Swiss say {Merci} for thank you, and {Ciao} or {Tschau} is heard all over the German-speaking world.
:::

## Wie geht's?

```words
Wie geht's? | How are you? (informal)
Wie geht es Ihnen? | How are you? (formal)
gut (besser, am besten) | good; well
sehr | very
schlecht | bad; badly
Es geht. | So-so. Not too bad.
nicht | not
und | and
auch | also, too
danke | thank you
bitte | please; you're welcome
Vielen Dank! | Thank you very much!
die Entschuldigung, -en | excuse me; sorry; apology
Wie bitte? | Pardon? Sorry?
```

{Wie geht's?} is short for {Wie geht es?}, literally "How goes it?" To a friend you say {Wie geht's?} or {Wie geht es dir?}; to someone you do not know well, {Wie geht es Ihnen?} The answers run from {Sehr gut!} through {Gut, danke.} and {Es geht.} to {Nicht so gut.} and {Schlecht.} Then you bounce the question back: {Und dir?} to a friend, {Und Ihnen?} to everyone else. The two kinds of "you" behind {dir} and {Ihnen} are the subject of lesson 2.

:::culture
{Wie geht's?} is a real question, not a greeting. Germans ask it of people they know, and may answer honestly: {Nicht so gut} is a perfectly normal reply. A shop assistant will not ask how you are, and nobody expects a stranger to.
:::

**{Bitte}** is the most useful word in German. It means *please* ({Ein Kaffee, bitte}), *you're welcome* ({Danke! – Bitte!}), *here you are* ({Bitte schön}), and, said as a question, *sorry?* For *you're welcome* you will also hear {Gern geschehen} or simply {Gerne!}

**{Entschuldigung}** is "excuse me" to get someone's attention and "sorry" when you bump into them. Its stress is on the second syllable: ent-SCHUL-di-gung. And when you did not catch something, say {Wie bitte?} rather than {Was?}, which sounds as blunt as "What?" does in English.

```dialogue
title: Guten Morgen! (8 a.m., on the stairs of a block of flats in Vienna)
Herr Berger: Guten Morgen, Frau Novak! | Good morning, Mrs Novak!
Frau Novak: Guten Morgen, Herr Berger! Wie geht es Ihnen? | Good morning, Mr Berger! How are you?
Herr Berger: Danke, sehr gut. Und Ihnen? | Very well, thank you. And you?
Frau Novak: Ach, es geht. Montag! | Oh, so-so. It's Monday!
Herr Berger: Ja, ja, Montag … Na dann, auf Wiedersehen! | Yes, Monday … Well then, goodbye!
Frau Novak: Auf Wiederschauen! | Goodbye!
```

## Ich heiße …

```words
Ich heiße … | My name is …
Wie heißt du? | What's your name? (informal)
Wie heißen Sie? | What's your name? (formal)
Freut mich! | Nice to meet you!
der Name, -n | name
der Herr, -en | Mr; gentleman
die Frau, -en | woman; wife; Mrs, Ms
ja | yes
nein | no
```

German does not so much say "my name is" as "I am called": {Ich heiße Lena.} {Mein Name ist Lena} is also correct and a little more formal; {Ich bin Lena} is casual and very common.

To ask, you choose between two questions. {Wie heißt du?} is for children, friends, fellow students and anyone your age at a party; {Wie heißen Sie?} is for adults you do not know, in a shop, an office or a hotel. Why German has two words for "you" is the first thing lesson 2 explains. For now, learn both questions as they are.

:::mistake
"What is your name?" is not {Was ist dein Name?}: Germans ask *how* you are called, {Wie heißt du?}, or more formally {Wie ist Ihr Name?}
:::

**{Herr}** and **{Frau}** go before surnames: {Herr Yilmaz}, {Frau Becker}. {Frau} is for every adult woman, married or not: the old {Fräulein} ("Miss") is dated and can offend. At work and on the phone, Germans often give only their surname: they pick up with {Becker, guten Tag!}, and you may hear simply {Becker.}

```dialogue
title: Wie heißt du? (the first evening of a language course in Zurich)
Luca: Hallo! Ich heiße Luca. Und du? | Hi! I'm Luca. And you?
Zeynep: Hallo, Luca! Ich heiße Zeynep. | Hi, Luca! I'm Zeynep.
Luca: Entschuldigung, wie bitte? | Sorry, what was that?
Zeynep: Zeynep. Zett – E – Ypsilon – En – E – Pe. | Zeynep. Z-E-Y-N-E-P.
Luca: Ah, Zeynep! Freut mich. | Ah, Zeynep! Nice to meet you.
Zeynep: Freut mich auch! | Nice to meet you too!
```

## Das Alphabet

```words
der Buchstabe, -n | letter (of the alphabet)
buchstabieren | to spell out
Wie schreibt man das? | How do you spell that?
```

The German alphabet has the same 26 letters as English, plus {ä}, {ö}, {ü} and {ß}. The letters are mostly named as in English, but the German way, and a handful are traps:

| Letter | German name | Sounds roughly like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | A | "ah" |
| E | E | "ay" (as in "day", without the y) |
| I | I | "ee" |
| R | Er | "air" |
| G | Ge | "gay" |
| H | Ha | "hah" |
| J | Jot | "yot" (in Austria, "yay") |
| V | Vau | "fow", rhyming with "cow" |
| W | We | "vay" |
| Y | Ypsilon | "ÜP-see-lon" |
| Z | Zett | "tset" |
| ß | Eszett | "ess-TSET" |

The classic confusion: the German A sounds like an English R, the German E like an English A, and the German I like an English E. Spelling your name aloud is where this bites, so practise it now.

::alphabet

To spell a word, people say double letters as {Doppel-} ({Anna}: A, Doppel-N, A) and name the umlauts: {Müller mit Ü}. To ask, say {Wie schreibt man das?}

:::culture
On the phone Germans make letters clear with a word: {B wie Berlin}, {M wie München}. Since 2022 Germany's official spelling alphabet uses towns, from {Aachen} to {Zwickau}, but you will still hear the older names ({A wie Anton}, {B wie Berta}). Austria and Switzerland have their own lists.
:::

## Vowels: long, short and with dots

Every German vowel comes in a long and a short version, and the spelling tells you which:

| Long when … | Examples | Short when … | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| the vowel is doubled | {Tee}, {Boot} | a double consonant follows | {Bett}, {Mann}, {kommen} |
| an h follows | {Uhr}, {Sohn}, {sehr} | two or more consonants follow | {Hund}, {Stadt}, {acht} |
| i is written ie | {Sie}, {vier} | it is one of many small words | {in}, {mit}, {das}, {es} |
| usually, one consonant follows | {Tag}, {Name}, {gut} | | |

Long German vowels are **pure**: held steady, without the glide English adds. English "no" slides from o to oo; German {so} stays on o. English "day" slides into a y; German {Tee} does not. Keep your mouth still, and you already sound more German.

The **umlauts** are not exotic:

- **{ä}** is a sound you already have: short like the e in "bed" ({Männer}), long like "air" without the r ({Käse}, {spät}).
- **{ö}**: say the long e of {Tee}, hold it, and round your lips as if to say "o": {schön}, {hören}. Short in {zwölf} and {Köln}.
- **{ü}**: say the ee of {Sie}, keep your tongue exactly there, and round your lips tightly as if to whistle: {für}, {müde}. Short in {fünf}. It is the French u of "tu".

The dots matter: {schon} is "already", {schön} "beautiful"; {Mutter} is "mother", {Mütter} "mothers"; {zahlen} is "to pay", {zählen} "to count". With no umlauts on your keyboard, write ae, oe, ue (Mueller for {Müller}, as in {Goethe}), but never just drop the dots.

:::grammar[ß or ss?]
{ß}, called {Eszett} or "sharp s", sounds exactly like ss. It is written after a long vowel or after ei, au, eu: {Straße}, {groß}, {heißen}, {Fuß}. After a short vowel you write ss: {dass}, {muss}, {Wasser}, {Tschüss}. This has been the rule since the 1996 spelling reform, so if you learnt "daß" and "muß" at school, those spellings are now out of date. Switzerland does without ß altogether and writes {Strasse}.
:::

## ei or ie?

Four vowel pairs, and the pronunciation of each never changes:

| Spelling | Sounds like | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ei (and ai, ey) | "eye" | {ein}, {drei}, {nein}, {heißen}, {Mai}, {Meyer} |
| ie | "ee" | {Sie}, {wie}, {vier}, {Wien}, {Bier} |
| eu, äu | "oy", as in "boy" | {neu}, {neun}, {Deutsch}, {Euro}, {Häuser} |
| au | "ow", as in "how" | {Haus}, {Frau}, {auch}, {Auf Wiedersehen} |

:::tip
Say the English name of the **second** letter. In {ie}, the second letter is e, "ee": {Wien} is "veen". In {ei}, it is i, "eye": {Wein} is "vine". So the physicist is EYEN-shtyne, not "Einsteen".
:::

:::mistake
A few borrowed words end in -ie with two separate sounds: {Familie} is fa-MEE-lee-uh, and so are {Italien} and {Ferien}. In {Familie}, the e is a syllable of its own.
:::

## Consonants that surprise

Most consonants are as in English. These are the ones to watch:

| Letters | Sound | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| w | English v | {wie}, {Wien}, {Wasser} |
| v | usually English f | {Vater}, {vier}, {viel}; but v in borrowed words: {Video}, {Vase} |
| j | English y | {ja}, {Jahr}, {Jonas} |
| z, tz | ts, as in "cats" | {zwei}, {Zürich}, {Katze} |
| s before a vowel | English z in the north; s in the south, Austria and Switzerland | {Sie}, {sehr}, {so} |
| sch | sh | {schön}, {Tschüss} |
| sp, st at the start of a word | shp, sht | {Sport}, {Stadt}, {Straße} |
| ch after a, o, u, au | rough, as in Scottish "loch" | {acht}, {Nacht}, {Buch}, {auch} |
| ch anywhere else | soft, like a strongly breathed h in "huge" | {ich}, {nicht}, {Milch}, {Mädchen} |
| chs | ks | {sechs} |
| b, d, g at the end | p, t, k | {ab}, {und}, {Hund}, {Tag} |
| h after a vowel | silent: it makes the vowel long | {sehr}, {Uhr} |
| pf | p and f together | {Apfel} |
| qu | kv | {Qualität} |

Three of these deserve a word more:

- **Endings harden.** A b, d or g at the end of a word is said as p, t, k: {Tag} sounds like "tahk", {Hund} like "hoont". Add an ending and the soft sound comes back: {Tage}, {Hunde}.
- **The r.** At the start of a syllable, most Germans say r at the back of the throat, close to a gargle; in Austria, Switzerland and Bavaria many roll it with the tip of the tongue. Both are standard. After a vowel at the end of a syllable, r turns into a short "uh": {Vater} sounds close to "FAH-tuh", {hier} to "hee-uh".
- **-ig at the end** is said like -ich in the standard language: {zwanzig} is "TSVAN-tsich". In southern Germany, Austria and Switzerland you will hear "-ik", and that is correct too.

**Stress** falls on the first syllable of most German words: {Morgen}, {Abend}, {Vater}, {arbeiten}. The exceptions are words beginning with an unstressed prefix such as be-, ver- or ent- ({Entschuldigung}: ent-SCHUL-di-gung), verbs in -ieren ({buchstabieren}: buch-sta-BIE-ren), and many borrowed words ({Hotel}, {Musik}, {Student}, {Familie}), which keep their stress further back.

## Alles zusammen

Time to put it all together: greetings, names, spelling and sounds.

```read

  Ben: Oh nein! Bis morgen?
  Mia: Ja, bis morgen! Gute Nacht!
  Ben: Gute Nacht, Mia! Tschüss!
en: |
  Mia: Hi, Ben! How are you?
  Ben: Hi! Fine, thanks. And you?
```
