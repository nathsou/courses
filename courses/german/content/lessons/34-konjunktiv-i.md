Listen to five minutes of German radio news and you will hear a verb form you almost never hear in a pub: {Die Ministerin sagte, die Lage sei unter Kontrolle.} Not {ist}, but {sei}. That one syllable is **Konjunktiv I**, and it does a job English leaves to inverted commas and tone of voice. It says: *this is what she said; I am reporting it, not vouching for it.*

If you learnt German at school, you may remember "indirect speech takes the subjunctive" and a fog of forms like {du kommest} and {ihr habet}. The reality is friendlier. In practice you need a dozen forms, nearly all of them in the third person, plus one clear rule for when to borrow from Konjunktiv II. By the end of this lesson you will read a German news report the way a German does, and know exactly how far to go with Konjunktiv I in your own speech (not very far).

## Sie sagte, sie sei krank

Here is the whole idea in three sentences:

- {Lena: „Ich bin krank.“} (direct speech, with German quotation marks „…“)
- {Lena sagt, sie ist krank.} (everyday speech: perfectly normal in conversation)
- {Lena sagt, sie sei krank.} (reported speech in Konjunktiv I: neutral, written, a little formal)

The Konjunktiv I version marks the statement as *Lena's*. The writer neither confirms nor doubts it. That is why newspapers, news broadcasts, minutes of meetings and academic writing are full of it: they report a great deal that they cannot personally check.

:::grammar[No backshift: the big difference from English]
English moves the tense back after a past reporting verb: "I'm ill" becomes "She said she *was* ill". German does not. Konjunktiv I keeps the time of the original words, whatever the tense of {sagen}:

- {„Ich bin krank.“} → {Sie sagt, sie sei krank.} and {Sie sagte, sie sei krank.}
- {„Ich war krank.“} → {Sie sagte, sie sei krank gewesen.}

The mood, not the tense, does the work of showing that this is reported.
:::

Three more things to notice:

1. **Pronouns and possessives shift** to the reporter's point of view, as in English: {Jonas: „Ich habe meinen Schlüssel verloren.“} → {Jonas sagt, er habe seinen Schlüssel verloren.}
2. **With or without dass.** Without {dass}, the reported clause keeps verb-second order: {Er sagt, er sei krank.} With {dass}, the verb goes to the end: {Er sagt, dass er krank sei.} News writers prefer the version without {dass}: it is shorter.
3. **It carries on.** Once a report has started, Konjunktiv I keeps signalling "still reported" across several sentences, with no further {sagte}: {Die Sprecherin äußerte sich am Abend. Die Firma habe keine Fehler gemacht. Man prüfe aber alle Vorwürfe.} English needs "she said" again and again; German does not.

The verb you choose to introduce the report matters as much as the mood. {sagen} is neutral; {behaupten} (claim) quietly suggests you are not convinced; {betonen} (stress) and {versichern} (assure) report emphasis; {einräumen} and {zugeben} report a reluctant admission.

## The forms: sei, habe, komme

Konjunktiv I is the most regular thing in German. Take the **infinitive stem** and add **-e, -est, -e, -en, -et, -en**. There is no vowel change and no umlaut, even for verbs that have them in the present tense:

| infinitive | indicative (er/sie/es) | Konjunktiv I (er/sie/es) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {kommen} | {er kommt} | {er komme} |
| {fahren} | {er fährt} | {er fahre} |
| {nehmen} | {sie nimmt} | {sie nehme} |
| {wissen} | {sie weiß} | {sie wisse} |
| {können} | {er kann} | {er könne} |
| {müssen} | {es muss} | {es müsse} |
| {wollen} | {sie will} | {sie wolle} |
| {haben} | {er hat} | {er habe} |
| {werden} | {sie wird} | {sie werde} |

Only one verb is irregular, and it is the most important: **sein**.

| | {sein} | {haben} | {werden} | {können} | {kommen} |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ich | {sei} | habe* | werde* | {könne} | komme* |
| du | {seist} | {habest} | {werdest} | {könnest} | {kommest} |
| er/sie/es | **{sei}** | **{habe}** | **{werde}** | **{könne}** | **{komme}** |
| wir | {seien} | haben* | werden* | können* | kommen* |
| ihr | {seiet} | {habet} | werdet* | {könnet} | {kommet} |
| sie/Sie | {seien} | haben* | werden* | können* | kommen* |

The forms marked * are identical to the ordinary present tense, so nobody can tell they are Konjunktiv I. The {du} and {ihr} forms exist but sound like a nineteenth-century sermon. That leaves the column in bold, plus every form of {sein}.

:::key
Almost all the Konjunktiv I you will ever read is **third person singular** ({er}, {sie}, {es}, {man}, or a noun) or a form of **sein**. Learn {sei, habe, werde, könne, müsse, wisse, gebe} by heart and you can read any German newspaper.
:::

## When Konjunktiv I looks like the indicative

Report {„Wir haben keine Zeit.“} in Konjunktiv I and you get {Sie sagen, sie haben keine Zeit.} That is indistinguishable from the indicative, so it no longer signals anything. German solves this with a simple three-step rule:

1. Use **Konjunktiv I** if it is visibly different from the indicative: {er habe}, {sie seien}.
2. If it is not, use **Konjunktiv II**: {sie haben} → {sie hätten}; {wir kommen} → {wir kämen}.
3. If Konjunktiv II is not distinct either (all regular verbs: {sie arbeiteten} looks like the past tense), or would sound stilted ({sie hülfen}, {sie stürben}), use **würde + infinitive**: {sie würden arbeiten}.

| original | Konjunktiv I | problem? | what you write |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| {„Ich habe Hunger.“} | {er habe} | none | {Er sagt, er habe Hunger.} |
| {„Wir haben Hunger.“} | {sie haben} | = indicative | {Sie sagen, sie hätten Hunger.} |
| {„Wir kommen später.“} | {sie kommen} | = indicative | {Sie sagen, sie kämen später.} |
| {„Wir arbeiten zu viel.“} | {sie arbeiten} | = indicative, and {arbeiteten} = past | {Sie sagen, sie würden zu viel arbeiten.} |
| {„Wir sind müde.“} | {sie seien} | none | {Sie sagen, sie seien müde.} |

:::note
Common Konjunktiv II forms ({hätte}, {wäre}, {käme}, {gäbe}, {könnte}, {müsste}, {wüsste}) are used freely in reports, and many writers use them even where Konjunktiv I would be distinct. In news writing, though, editors prefer Konjunktiv I wherever it is available: {er wäre krank} instead of {er sei krank} can sound as if the reporter doubts it.
:::

## What happened and what will happen

Konjunktiv I has only **one past tense**: {habe} or {sei} + past participle. Präteritum, Perfekt and Plusquamperfekt in the original all collapse into it, and the choice of {haben} or {sein} is the same as in the perfect.

| original | reported |
| --- | --- |
| {„Ich war krank.“} / {„Ich bin krank gewesen.“} | {Er sagt, er sei krank gewesen.} |
| {„Ich wusste es nicht.“} / {„Ich hatte es nicht gewusst.“} | {Sie sagt, sie habe es nicht gewusst.} |
| {„Wir sind zu spät angekommen.“} | {Sie sagen, sie seien zu spät angekommen.} |
| {„Wir hatten Glück.“} | {Sie sagen, sie hätten Glück gehabt.} |
| {„Ich werde pünktlich sein.“} | {Er versichert, er werde pünktlich sein.} |
| {„Wir werden streiken.“} | {Sie kündigen an, sie würden streiken.} |

The future uses {werde} + infinitive; in the plural {werden} looks like the indicative, so it becomes {würden}. Here {würden} does not mean "would": it is simply the reported future.

:::mistake
A modal verb in the past keeps its **double infinitive**: {„Ich musste lange warten.“} → {Er sagt, er habe lange warten müssen.} Not {er habe lange warten gemusst}. The auxiliary is always {haben}, even with a verb of motion: {Sie sagt, sie habe nach Hause gehen wollen.}
:::

## Questions and requests

**Questions** are reported like any indirect question (you met {ob} and the verb at the end at A2), with the verb in Konjunktiv I:

- {„Hast du Zeit?“} → {Sie fragte, ob er Zeit habe.}
- {„Wann kommt der Bus?“} → {Er wollte wissen, wann der Bus komme.}

**Commands and requests** have no imperative in reported speech. Use {sollen} for an instruction and {mögen} for a polite request; the {mögen} version is formal and slightly old-fashioned, typical of letters and official reports:

- {„Warte hier!“} → {Er sagte, sie solle hier warten.}
- {„Rufen Sie bitte zurück.“} → {Sie bat, er möge zurückrufen.}

Often the simplest solution is a zu-infinitive after a verb like {bitten} or {auffordern}: {Sie bat ihn zurückzurufen.}

## In den Nachrichten

News German has its own little vocabulary for sources. Two prepositions are worth learning properly:

- **{laut}** + dative (or genitive), placed before the noun, often without an article: {laut Polizei}, {laut einer Umfrage}.
- **{zufolge}** + dative, placed **after** the noun: {der Polizei zufolge}, {einem Bericht zufolge}.

Both mean "according to", and both often come with Konjunktiv I: {Einem Bericht zufolge habe das Unternehmen Millionen verloren.} The adverb {angeblich} ("allegedly") does the same job in one word: {Der Minister wusste angeblich von nichts.}

## How people really report things

Nobody chatting over coffee says {Sie sagte, sie sei krank}. In conversation, people report with the indicative, with or without {dass}, or with Konjunktiv II and {würde}, which in speech has taken over much of the job of Konjunktiv I. Listen for it:

```dialogue
title: In der Kaffeeküche
Zeynep: Hast du schon gehört? Frau Krüger hat gesagt, dass sie im Sommer aufhört. | Have you heard? Frau Krüger said she's leaving in the summer.
Ben: Echt? Mir hat sie gestern noch erzählt, sie hätte keine Pläne. | Really? Only yesterday she told me she had no plans.
Zeynep: Na ja, Jonas meint, sie würde zu einer Firma in München wechseln. | Well, Jonas reckons she's moving to a company in Munich.
Ben: Jonas erzählt viel, wenn der Tag lang ist. | Jonas says all sorts of things. (literally: tells a lot when the day is long)
Zeynep: Stimmt. Aber sie hat mich gefragt, ob ich mir vorstellen kann, ihr Team zu übernehmen. | True. But she asked me whether I could imagine taking over her team.
Ben: Wie bitte? Und was hast du gesagt? | Sorry? And what did you say?
Zeynep: Ich hab gesagt, ich muss darüber nachdenken. | I said I'd have to think about it.
Ben: Typisch du. Ich hätte sofort Ja gesagt! | Typical you. I'd have said yes straight away!
```

Not one Konjunktiv I, and nothing wrong with it. Here is the range, from formal to relaxed:

| where | how it is reported |
| --- | --- |
| news, reports, minutes, academic writing | {Frau Krüger teilte mit, sie höre im Sommer auf.} |
| careful speech, emails, much everyday writing | {Frau Krüger hat gesagt, sie würde im Sommer aufhören.} |
| relaxed conversation | {Frau Krüger hat gesagt, dass sie im Sommer aufhört.} |

:::tip
For your own German: use Konjunktiv I when you **write** a report, a summary, minutes or a formal email, and above all **recognise** it when you read. When you **speak**, the indicative or {hätte}, {wäre}, {würde} are what native speakers use. A Konjunktiv I in a café sounds like someone reading the news aloud.
:::
