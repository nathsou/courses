In 2004 a book about German grammar became a bestseller. Its title was {Der Dativ ist dem Genitiv sein Tod}, "The dative is the death of the genitive", and the joke is that the title itself uses the colloquial dative construction that is crowding out the genitive. Germans love to complain that their fourth case is dying. In relaxed speech it is indeed retreating. But open a newspaper, read an official letter, listen to the news or sit a B1 exam, and the genitive is everywhere.

The good news for returners: the genitive is the most regular of the four cases. There are only two article forms to learn, one noun ending, and one adjective ending that is almost always the same. This lesson also tidies up a small group of masculine nouns, {der Kollege}, {der Student}, {der Herr}, that you have probably been getting slightly wrong for years.

## Das Fahrrad meines Bruders

- {Das ist das Fahrrad meines Bruders.} That's my brother's bike.
- {Die Wohnung der Familie Yilmaz ist im dritten Stock.} The Yilmaz family's flat is on the third floor.
- {Das Ende des Films war traurig.} The end of the film was sad.
- {Die Meinung der Kinder ist uns wichtig.} The children's opinion matters to us.

The genitive answers the question {wessen?}, "whose?", and links one noun to another, like English "of" or "'s". Notice the order: the genitive comes **after** the noun it belongs to, as with English "of": {das Ende des Films}, the end of the film.

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {der Vater} | {die Mutter} | {das Kind} | {die Kinder} |
| genitive | {des Vaters} | {der Mutter} | {des Kindes} | {der Kinder} |
| | {eines Vaters} | {einer Mutter} | {eines Kindes} | – |
| | {meines Vaters} | {meiner Mutter} | {meines Kindes} | {meiner Kinder} |

That is the whole system. Masculine and neuter: **{des}** / **{eines}** / **{meines}**, and the noun adds **-s** or **-es**. Feminine and plural: **{der}** / **{einer}** / **{meiner}**, and the noun stays as it is. {dieser}, {jeder} and {kein} follow the same pattern: {dieses Hauses}, {jeder Woche}, {keines Wortes}.

::article-table{kind=definite cases=nom,acc,dat,gen}

### -s or -es?

- **-es** after s, ß, x, z and tz, because you could not pronounce it otherwise: {des Hauses}, {des Fußes}, {des Platzes}. Nouns in {-nis} double the s: {des Ergebnisses}.
- **-es** is also usual with short nouns of one syllable: {des Mannes}, {des Kindes}, {des Jahres}. {des Jahrs} is not wrong, just less common.
- **-s** with longer nouns, especially those ending in -er, -el, -en and -chen: {des Vaters}, {des Lehrers}, {des Mädchens}, {des Computers}.

```words
die Mitte (Sg.) | middle
die Hälfte, -n | half
```


## Wessen? Names and time

To ask "whose?", use {wessen} followed directly by the noun: {Wessen Jacke ist das?} – {Das ist die Jacke meiner Kollegin.} {wessen} sounds rather formal, though. In conversation people ask {Wem gehört die Jacke?} (dative, which you know from A2) or {Von wem ist die Jacke?}

With **names**, the genitive -s goes on the name, which comes *before* the noun, and without an apostrophe: {Annas Jacke}, {Peters Geburtstag}. If the name already ends in an s-sound, write only an apostrophe: {Jonas' Jacke}, {Max' Fahrrad}. In speech most people avoid this and say {die Jacke von Jonas}.

:::mistake
The English apostrophe has been creeping into German shop signs ({Gabi's Imbiss}), and Duden tolerates it with names. But the standard, and what examiners like, is still **{Gabis Imbiss}**, {Annas Wohnung}: no apostrophe.
:::

A few fixed expressions of time are in the genitive. They are worth learning as phrases:

- {eines Tages} one day, {eines Abends} one evening, {eines Morgens} one morning
- {Anfang des Jahres}, {Mitte der Woche}, {Ende des Monats}: at the beginning of the year, in the middle of the week, at the end of the month (no article and no preposition in front)

:::fun
{die Nacht} is feminine, so its genitive should be {der Nacht}. But in {eines Nachts}, "one night", it has borrowed the masculine -s from {eines Tages} and {eines Abends}. Grammar bowed to rhythm, and Duden accepts it.
:::

## Adjectives in the genitive

After an article in the genitive, the adjective ending is always **-en**, in every gender and in the plural. The article has already shown the case, so the adjective has nothing left to do:

- {das Ende des langen Winters}, {die Wohnung meiner besten Freundin}, {der Sohn eines berühmten Schauspielers}, {die Eltern der kleinen Kinder}

Without an article, the A2 principle applies, "if the article does not show the case, the adjective must": feminine and plural take **-er** ({trotz großer Probleme}, {wegen schlechter Luft}). Masculine and neuter are the one exception: they take **-en**, because the noun's own -s already marks the genitive ({trotz starken Regens}, {wegen schlechten Wetters}).

| | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| with article | {des alten Mannes} | {der alten Frau} | {des alten Hauses} | {der alten Leute} |
| without article | {starken Regens} | {großer Hitze} | {schlechten Wetters} | {hoher Kosten} |

## Wegen, trotz, während

Six prepositions take the genitive, and they are the reason you cannot avoid this case at B1:

| Preposition | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| {wegen} | because of | {Wegen des Streiks fahren heute keine Züge.} |
| {trotz} | despite, in spite of | {Trotz des Regens sind wir spazieren gegangen.} |
| {während} | during | {Während des Films hat sie geschlafen.} |
| {statt}, {anstatt} | instead of | {Statt eines Kuchens habe ich Muffins gebacken.} |
| {innerhalb} | within, inside | {Bitte antworten Sie innerhalb einer Woche.} |
| {außerhalb} | outside | {Wir wohnen außerhalb der Stadt.} |

They are the noun versions of conjunctions you already know: {weil es regnet} → {wegen des Regens}; {obwohl es regnet} → {trotz des Regens}; {während wir aßen} → {während des Essens}. Formal German, in news reports and official letters, prefers the short noun version.

:::tip
When there is no article to show the genitive, use {von} + dative after {innerhalb} and {außerhalb}: {innerhalb von zwei Wochen}, not {innerhalb zweier Wochen}, which sounds like a legal document from 1900.
:::

:::note
With pronouns, {wegen} has its own forms: {meinetwegen}, {deinetwegen}, {seinetwegen}, {unseretwegen}… {Bist du meinetwegen gekommen?} Did you come because of me? On its own, {Meinetwegen!} means "Fine by me!", often with a shrug. In speech you will also hear {wegen mir}, which is colloquial.
:::

## What people really say

In relaxed speech, the genitive often gives way to the dative:

- Possession becomes **{von}** + dative: {das Auto von meinem Vater} instead of {das Auto meines Vaters}. This is perfectly good German and fine in writing too, if a little less elegant.
- **{wegen}**, **{trotz}**, **{während}** and **{statt}** often take the dative in speech: {wegen dem Wetter}, {trotz dem Regen}. This is colloquial (and in parts of the south and in Austria very common), but in anything you write, use the genitive.
- In some dialects you will hear {meinem Vater sein Auto}, literally "to my father his car". That is the construction the bestseller title makes fun of. Understand it; do not write it.

And sometimes {von} is not just casual but required: when there is no article or adjective to carry the genitive. {der Bau von Wohnungen} (the building of flats), {die Mutter von drei Kindern}, {ein Freund von mir} (a friend of mine). With an adjective, the genitive works again: {der Bau neuer Wohnungen}.

```dialogue
title: Wem gehört das Fahrrad?
Sophie: Du, Mehmet, wem gehört eigentlich das rote Fahrrad im Flur? | Mehmet, who does the red bike in the hall actually belong to?
Mehmet: Das ist das Fahrrad von meinem Cousin Emre. Er hat es letzte Woche hier vergessen. | It's my cousin Emre's bike. He forgot it here last week.
Sophie: Vergessen? Wie vergisst man denn ein Fahrrad? | Forgot it? How do you forget a bike?
Mehmet: Er ist mit dem Taxi nach Hause gefahren. Wegen dem Regen. | He went home by taxi. Because of the rain.
Sophie: Na super. Und wegen seines Fahrrads komme ich jetzt nicht an meinen Schrank. | Great. And because of his bike I can't get to my cupboard now.
Mehmet: Okay, okay. Ich ruf ihn gleich an. Er kann es heute Abend nach dem Spiel holen. | OK, OK. I'll ring him right away. He can fetch it this evening after the match.
Sophie: Am besten noch vor dem Spiel! | Preferably before the match!
```

Mehmet speaks the way most people do among friends: {von meinem Cousin}, {wegen dem Regen}. Sophie's {wegen seines Fahrrads} is the standard form, and here it sounds a little pointed, which is exactly how she means it.

## Der Kollege, den Kollegen

A small group of masculine nouns, the **n-nouns** (or "weak nouns"), add **-n** or **-en** in every form except the nominative singular. And unlike other masculine nouns, they take no -s in the genitive.

| | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | {der Kollege} | {die Kollegen} |
| accusative | {den Kollegen} | {die Kollegen} |
| dative | {dem Kollegen} | {den Kollegen} |
| genitive | {des Kollegen} | {der Kollegen} |

Which nouns? Almost all of them are masculine nouns for **people and animals**, and you can recognise most of them by their ending:

1. masculine nouns in **-e** for people and animals: {der Kollege}, {der Junge}, {der Kunde}, {der Neffe}, {der Löwe}
2. masculine nouns from Latin or Greek in **-ent, -ant, -ist, -at, -oge, -graf**: {der Student}, {der Präsident}, {der Journalist}, {der Polizist}, {der Kandidat}, {der Biologe}, {der Fotograf}
3. a handful of others to learn by heart: {der Mensch}, {der Herr}, {der Nachbar}, {der Held}, {der Bär}

A few abstract nouns add **-ns** in the genitive: {der Name} → {des Namens}, {der Gedanke} → {des Gedankens}, and the one neuter: {das Herz} → {des Herzens}, {dem Herzen}.

:::mistake
{Ich habe einen neuen Kollege} sounds like a learner at once: it must be {einen neuen Kollegen}. Watch out for {Herr} in letters, too. You write {Sehr geehrter Herr Wagner}, but {an Herrn Wagner}, {mit Herrn Wagner}, {Kennen Sie Herrn Wagner?}: {Herr} takes **-n** in the singular and **-en** in the plural ({meine Damen und Herren}). In casual speech you will hear {dem Präsident} or {den Bär}; in writing, keep the -en.
:::

