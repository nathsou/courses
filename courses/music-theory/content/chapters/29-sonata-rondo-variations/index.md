## Sonata form

The most important form of the Classical period is not a form for whole sonatas but for single movements, usually
the first movement of a sonata, symphony or string quartet. **Sonata form** grows out of rounded binary
(Chapter 28): a first half that modulates, a second half that wanders and returns. It has three main parts.

1. **Exposition.** The **first subject** (or first theme group) sets out the tonic key. A **transition**
   modulates. The **second subject** comes in the new key, usually the dominant (or, in a minor key, the relative
   major), and a closing section confirms that key with cadences. The exposition is usually repeated.
2. **Development.** The themes are broken into fragments, sequenced (Chapter 22) and carried through other keys.
   It ends by preparing the return: a long stretch on the dominant, the **retransition**.
3. **Recapitulation.** The exposition returns, but the second subject now stays in the tonic. The transition is
   rewritten so that it no longer modulates. A **coda** may follow.

The drama of the form is a matter of keys: the exposition opens a conflict between two keys, the development
heightens it, and the recapitulation resolves it by bringing everything home.

### A sonatina by Clementi

A **sonatina** is a small sonata, with a short development. Muzio Clementi’s six Sonatinas, Op. 36 (1797), written
for his pupils, are still among the first sonatas pianists learn. The first movement of No. 1 is sonata form in
38 :t[bar]s. The labels mark the sections; listen for :n[F#] arriving at :t[bar] 7 and the music settling in :n[G] major,

### Mozart’s “Sonata facile”

Mozart called his Sonata in :n[C], K. 545 (1788), a little sonata “for beginners”; it is now known as the *Sonata
facile*. Its first movement is a full-scale sonata form, and its opening is a lesson in Classical texture: a
simple tune in the right hand, an **Alberti bass** (Chapter 17) in the left.

```score caption="Mozart, Sonata in C, K. 545, first movement, bars 1–4: the first subject over an Alberti bass (the left hand is written in the treble clef in most editions)." tempo="132"
\key c \major \time 4/4 \staff treble c''2 e''4 g''4 | b'4. c''16 d''16 c''4 r4 | a''2 g''4 c'''4 | g''4 f''8 e''16 f''16 e''4 r4 | \staff bass c'8 g'8 e'8 g'8 c'8 g'8 e'8 g'8 | d'8 g'8 f'8 g'8 c'8 g'8 e'8 g'8 | c'8 a'8 f'8 a'8 c'8 g'8 e'8 g'8 | b8 g'8 d'8 g'8 c'8 g'8 e'8 g'8 |
```

The harmony of those four bars is I | V43 – I | IV64 – I | V6 – I: the Alberti figure spells each chord out,
and the left hand barely moves. After them come running scales (the transition), a second subject in :n[G] major,
and the closing section, all in 28 bars.

## Rondo

A **rondo** alternates a recurring theme, the **refrain** (A), with contrasting **episodes**: ABACA, or ABACABA. The
refrain is usually tuneful and in the tonic; the episodes visit other keys and moods. It is the classic form for
lively last movements: the finale of K. 545 is a rondo, and so is Beethoven’s *Für Elise*, whose famous opening is
the refrain, framing two contrasting episodes.

## Theme and variations

A **theme and variations** states a tune (often a binary form of 16 or 32 :t[bar]s) and then repeats it many times,
each time changed: the melody decorated, the rhythm quickened, the mode switched to minor, the texture or the
harmony altered, while the phrase structure and the main cadences stay. Mozart’s twelve variations on the French song
“Ah, vous dirai-je, maman” (K. 265), the tune English speakers know as “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star”, are the
famous example.

A second kind repeats not a melody but a **bass line**: variations over a **ground bass** (Chapter 15), as in the
Baroque *passacaglia* and *chaconne*, and in Pachelbel’s Canon.

## Canon III: variations over a ground

Pachelbel’s Canon is a set of variations over its two-:t[bar] ground: the violins’ lines change character every
two :t[bar]s, from slow notes to quick figures, while the ground repeats underneath. Here are three variations over
the ground written for the course in the same spirit: the first violin’s opening line in crotchets, then a line
in :t[quaver]s, then broken chords in :t[semiquaver]s. Each uses only notes of the chords, so they can be
combined.

:::key
- **Sonata form:** exposition (first subject in the tonic, transition, second subject in the dominant or relative
  major), development (fragments, sequences, modulation, retransition), recapitulation (everything in the tonic).
- A **sonatina** is a small sonata form with a short development.
- **Rondo:** a refrain alternating with episodes, ABACA.
- **Theme and variations** keep a tune's structure while changing its surface; **ground-bass** variations keep a
  bass line.
:::
