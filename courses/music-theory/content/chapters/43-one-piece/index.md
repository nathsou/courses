## One piece

The course ends where it began, at the piano with one piece, but this time with every tool in hand. Frédéric
Chopin’s Prelude in E minor, Op. 28 No. 4, published in 1839 with the other twenty-three preludes (one in every
major and minor key, round the circle of fifths, each major key followed by its relative minor), is 25 :t[bar]s long and marked *Largo*. Its right hand
plays a melody that hardly moves; its left hand plays repeated chords that change one note at a time. It is one of
the first Chopin pieces many pianists learn, and at Chopin’s funeral in Paris in 1849 it was played on the organ,
with the sixth prelude. The nickname printed on some editions, “Suffocation”, is not Chopin’s.

form and cadence.

## The melody

For the first four bars the melody has two notes, :n[B4] and :n[C5]: a long :n[B], then :n[C] as an upper neighbour
(Chapter 16), three times. In bar 4 the neighbour becomes :n[Bb], and the line sinks a semitone to :n[A]
and :n[B], then in bar 8 to :n[G#]. After the octave of the upbeat, it moves only by step until bar 9, where it
rises quickly to :n[D5] and falls away; in bar 12 a little figure leads back to the beginning.

That is the first lesson of the piece. A melody does not need to go anywhere to be expressive: here the expression
is in what happens underneath each long note.

This is voice leading (Chapter 19) taken to its limit: three voices, each sliding down by semitones in turn, so
that the hand barely moves. Some of the stages are chords you know. With the melody’s :n[B] on top, bar 4 begins
with :c[E7], the dominant seventh of :n[A] minor (V7/iv, Chapter 23); bar 5 has :c[Am7] and then :c[F#m7b5], the iiø7 of
:n[E] minor; bar 6 reaches :n[D#] :n[F#] :n[A] :n[C], the diminished seventh vii°7 (Chapter 27); bar 7 is :c[D7], the
dominant of :n[G] major (V7/III). Others are hard to name at all: they are passing stages between chords, made by the
lines rather than chosen for themselves.

That is the second lesson. In the Prelude in C minor (Chapter 27), the chromatic descent was in the bass. Here it is spread through the whole left hand, and the harmony is a by-product of
three slow lines. Functional harmony returns only at the end of the phrase: in bars 10 to 12 the left hand rocks
between :n[B] :n[D#] :n[A] (V7) and :n[C] :n[E] :n[A] (iiø7 with the melody’s :n[F#]), and stops on V7.

## The form

Bar 12 stops on the dominant, :c[B7], with the left hand silent: a **half cadence**, and the end of the first phrase.
Bar 13 starts again exactly as bar 1 did. So the prelude is a **period** (Chapter 18): two phrases with the same
beginning, the first ending on V, the second on I. But the second phrase is not a copy:

- **It is faster.** The chromatic descent that took eight bars takes four (bars 13–16).
- **It breaks out.** In bar 16 the melody leaps up a seventh, *stretto* (pressing forward), and bar 17, the only
  *forte* in the piece, reaches the highest note, :n[C6], over the lowest bass so far, an octave :n[B1]–:n[B2]. The
  climax is a dominant: :n[B] in the bass, :n[A] :n[C] :n[F#] above it, V with a minor ninth (Chapter 31’s ♭9) and no
  third.
- **It refuses to end.** Then come three cadences, and only the last one closes.

## Three endings

**Bars 20–21: interrupted.** Bar 20 settles on V7 (:n[B] :n[D#] :n[A]) and bar 21 moves, not to the tonic, but to :c[C]
major, VI: the **interrupted cadence** of Chapter 13.

**Bars 22–23: evaded.** Bar 22 reaches the tonic chord with :n[B] in the bass, :n[B] :n[E] :n[G]: the **cadential six-four**
(Chapter 21), which promises V and then i. Instead the bass slips down to :n[Bb] under :n[C] :n[E] :n[G], and the
music stops on a fermata. Written, the chord is :c[C7] in third inversion. Heard in :n[E] minor, with its :n[Bb]
wanting to rise to :n[B], it sounds like a **German sixth** (Chapter 26) on :n[C], whose augmented sixth would be
spelled :n[A#].

**Bars 24–25: perfect.** After the silence, *pianissimo*, the dominant: :n[B] in the bass, :n[E] above it falling
to :n[D#], a 4–3 **suspension** (Chapter 16). Then the tonic. There is one more thing to notice here, and the table
above already hints at it: the tonic chord of :n[E] minor in root position, with :n[E] in the bass, appears nowhere
else in the piece. The prelude begins on i6 and reaches the home chord, the thing every cadence in the course has
been about, only in its last bar.

## All the way down

Here is the prelude through the whole course, from the outside in:

- **Sound and keyboard (Part I).** :n[E] minor, one sharp; *Largo* in 2/2, so two slow beats in a :t[bar], with a
  crotchet upbeat.
- **Scales and keys (Part I).** :n[E] minor with its raised leading note :n[D#], and chromatic notes almost everywhere.
- **Chords and function (Parts II and III).** A first phrase that sets off from i6 and stops on V7; a second that ends
  with an interrupted cadence, an evaded one and a perfect one.
- **Voice leading (Part IV).** Three left-hand voices moving by semitone, one at a time; a 4–3 suspension at the end.
- **Chromatic harmony (Part V).** Secondary dominants and a diminished seventh passing by in the descent; a German
  sixth in disguise.
- **Form (Part V).** A period whose consequent is compressed and extended, with its climax just before the end.
- **Beyond (Parts VI and VII).** The climax chord is a dominant with a ♭9; the left hand’s one-note-at-a-time motion is
  the smooth voice leading that Chapter 40 measured between triads, here between three-note chords of every kind.

And it needs equal temperament (Chapter 42), or something close to it. In quarter-comma meantone, where the black
key between :n[D] and :n[E] is tuned as :n[Eb], the leading note :n[D#] would be about 41 cents sharp, and every chord
containing it badly out of tune.

:::key
- The melody hardly moves; the expression is in the **chords underneath** it.
- The left hand descends **one note at a time, by semitones**: voice leading first, harmony as a result.
- The form is a **period**: a half cadence in bar 12, then a second phrase that is compressed, rises to a climax and
  ends with an **interrupted**, an **evaded** and finally a **perfect** cadence.
- The tonic chord in root position appears **only in the last bar**.
:::

## Where next

You have reached the end of the course, but not of the piece. Play it again in a week, and in a year: you will hear
more of it each time. Then take any piece you love, public domain or not, and do the same: listen first, find the
key, the cadences, the phrases, the one chord that surprises you, and play it slowly enough to hear why. The practice
room keeps every skill in the course ready for when you need it.
