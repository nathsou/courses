Some arithmetic progressions obviously contain few primes: $6, 10, 14, \ldots$ ($a = 6$, $q = 4$) contains none, because every term is even. In general, if $d = \gcd(a, q) > 1$ then every term of $a, a + q, a + 2q, \ldots$ is divisible by $d$, so at most one is prime. Dirichlet proved in 1837 that this is the only obstruction.:cite[dirichlet1837]

:::theorem{name="Dirichlet’s theorem" who="Dirichlet" year=1837 status=proved}
If $\gcd(a, q) = 1$, there are infinitely many primes $p \equiv a \pmod q$. In fact $\sum_{p \equiv a\ (q)}\frac1p$ diverges.
:::

The proof introduced characters and $L$-functions, two of the most important ideas in number theory. Before it, some special cases have Euclid-style proofs.

## Special cases by hand

**Primes $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$.** If $p_1, \ldots, p_k$ were all of them, consider $N = 4p_1\cdots p_k - 1 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. It is odd, and a product of primes all $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ would be $\equiv 1$, so some prime factor of $N$ is $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$; it is none of the $p_i$, since they divide $N + 1$.

**Primes $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$.** If $p_1, \ldots, p_k$ were all of them, consider $N = (2p_1\cdots p_k)^2 + 1$. Any prime factor $p$ of $N$ is odd and has $-1$ as a square modulo $p$, so $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ (Chapter 9); and it is not one of the $p_i$.

Similar tricks handle $a \equiv 1 \pmod q$ for every $q$ (using cyclotomic polynomials, exercise 3), and a few other cases, but no such trick is known for general $a$. For that, Dirichlet adapted Euler’s proof that $\sum_p 1/p$ diverges (Chapter 4), using a function that singles out the residue class $a$.

## Characters

A **Dirichlet character** modulo $q$ is a function $\chi : \Z \to \C$ such that
- $\chi(n)$ depends only on $n \bmod q$;
- $\chi(n) = 0$ if $\gcd(n, q) > 1$, and $\chi(n) \ne 0$ otherwise;
- $\chi(mn) = \chi(m)\chi(n)$ for all $m, n$.

On the units it is a character of the finite abelian group $(\Z/q\Z)^\times$ in the sense of Appendix C, so its nonzero values are roots of unity and there are exactly $\varphi(q)$ characters. The **principal character** $\chi_0$ is $1$ on all $n$ coprime to $q$. A character is **real** if all its values are real (so $\pm1$ or $0$), and **complex** otherwise.

For example, modulo $4$ there are two characters: $\chi_0$, and $\chi_{-4}$ with $\chi_{-4}(1) = 1$, $\chi_{-4}(3) = -1$. Modulo $5$, the group $(\Z/5\Z)^\times$ is cyclic, generated by $2$, and a character is fixed by $\chi(2) \in \{1, i, -1, -i\}$.

::character-table

The key property is the second orthogonality relation of Appendix C: for $\gcd(a, q) = 1$,
$$\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi\overline{\chi(a)}\,\chi(n) = \begin{cases}1 & n \equiv a \pmod q,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$
It turns the condition “$n \equiv a$” into a combination of multiplicative functions, and multiplicative functions have Euler products.

## L-functions

For a character $\chi$ modulo $q$ and $\operatorname{Re}s > 1$, the **Dirichlet $L$-function** is
$$L(s, \chi) = \sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^s} = \prod_p\frac{1}{1 - \chi(p)p^{-s}},$$
the Euler product holding because $\chi$ is completely multiplicative (Chapter 19). Two facts about the behaviour near $s = 1$:

:::lemma{status=proved}
1. $L(s, \chi_0) = \zeta(s)\prod_{p \mid q}(1 - p^{-s})$, so $L(s, \chi_0)$ has a simple pole at $s = 1$.
2. If $\chi \ne \chi_0$, the series $\sum\chi(n)n^{-s}$ converges for $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$ and defines a holomorphic function there. In particular $L(1, \chi) = \sum_{n \ge 1}\chi(n)/n$ converges.
:::

:::proof
(1) Remove the Euler factors at the primes dividing $q$ from the product for $\zeta$. (2) By the first orthogonality relation, $\sum_{n = m+1}^{m+q}\chi(n) = 0$ for any $m$, so the partial sums $A(x) = \sum_{n \le x}\chi(n)$ satisfy $|A(x)| \le \varphi(q)$. By Abel summation, $\sum_{n \le x}\chi(n)n^{-s} = A(x)x^{-s} + s\int_1^xA(t)t^{-s-1}dt$, and the integral converges absolutely and locally uniformly for $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$.
:::

## The proof, assuming L(1, χ) ≠ 0

Take logarithms of the Euler product. For real $s > 1$ define
$$\log L(s, \chi) = \sum_p\sum_{k \ge 1}\frac{\chi(p)^k}{k\,p^{ks}} = \sum_p\frac{\chi(p)}{p^s} + R(s, \chi), \qquad |R(s, \chi)| \le \sum_p\sum_{k \ge 2}\frac{1}{k\,p^k} < 1.$$
(This is a logarithm of $L(s, \chi)$: its exponential is the Euler product.) Multiply by $\overline{\chi(a)}$ and average over $\chi$; by orthogonality, only the primes $p \equiv a$ survive:
$$\sum_{p \equiv a\ (q)}\frac{1}{p^s} = \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi\overline{\chi(a)}\log L(s, \chi) + O(1).$$
Now let $s \to 1^+$.

- For $\chi_0$: $\log L(s, \chi_0) = \log\zeta(s) + O(1) \to +\infty$.
- For $\chi \ne \chi_0$: $L(s, \chi) \to L(1, \chi)$. **If $L(1, \chi) \ne 0$**, then $L(s, \chi)$ for $s \in [1, 2]$ traces a path that avoids $0$, so any continuous logarithm of it, in particular $\log L(s, \chi)$, stays bounded.

So the right side tends to $+\infty$, and therefore so does $\sum_{p \equiv a}p^{-s}$. That is impossible if there are finitely many primes $p \equiv a \pmod q$, and it shows that $\sum_{p \equiv a}\frac1p$ diverges. Everything hinges on the nonvanishing of $L(1, \chi)$.

## L(1, χ) ≠ 0

:::theorem{name="Non-vanishing at s = 1" who="Dirichlet" year=1837 status=proved}
$L(1, \chi) \ne 0$ for every character $\chi \ne \chi_0$.
:::

The proof is in two parts, because complex and real characters behave differently.

:::lemma{status=proved}
For real $s > 1$, $\prod_\chi L(s, \chi) \ge 1$.
:::

:::proof
Sum the logarithms: $\sum_\chi\log L(s, \chi) = \sum_{p, k}\frac{1}{kp^{ks}}\sum_\chi\chi(p^k)$. By orthogonality (with $a = 1$), the inner sum is $\varphi(q)$ if $p^k \equiv 1 \pmod q$ and $0$ otherwise. So the total is $\varphi(q)\sum_{p^k \equiv 1}\frac{1}{kp^{ks}} \ge 0$, and the product is at least $e^0 = 1$.
:::

:::proof[Proof of the theorem for complex χ]
If $L(1, \chi) = 0$ for a complex character, then also $L(1, \bar\chi) = \overline{L(1, \chi)} = 0$, and $\bar\chi \ne \chi$. In the product $\prod_\chi L(s, \chi)$, as $s \to 1^+$: the factor $L(s, \chi_0)$ grows like $\frac{C}{s - 1}$; the factors $L(s, \chi)$ and $L(s, \bar\chi)$ are holomorphic and vanish at $1$, so each is $O(s - 1)$; and the others are bounded. The product is $O(s - 1) \to 0$, contradicting the lemma. (This is the same “two zeros beat one pole” count as in Chapter 25.)
:::

For a real character $\chi \ne \chi_0$, only one factor could vanish, and one zero exactly cancels the pole. A different idea is needed: positivity of coefficients, and Landau’s theorem from Chapter 30 in its form for series.

:::proof[Proof of the theorem for real χ]
Consider $F(s) = \zeta(s)L(s, \chi) = \sum_{n \ge 1}a_nn^{-s}$, where $a_n = \sum_{d \mid n}\chi(d)$ (Dirichlet convolution, Chapter 19). The function $a$ is multiplicative, and at a prime power $p^k$, since $\chi(p) \in \{1, -1, 0\}$,
$$a_{p^k} = 1 + \chi(p) + \cdots + \chi(p)^k = \begin{cases}k + 1 & \chi(p) = 1,\\ 1 \text{ or } 0 & \chi(p) = -1 \ (k \text{ even or odd}),\\ 1 & \chi(p) = 0.\end{cases}$$
So $a_n \ge 0$ for all $n$, and $a_{m^2} \ge 1$ for every $m$.

Suppose $L(1, \chi) = 0$. Then $F$, which is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$ except possibly at $s = 1$, has no pole at $1$: the zero of $L$ cancels the simple pole of $\zeta$. So $F$ is holomorphic on the whole half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$.

**Landau’s theorem** for Dirichlet series with nonnegative coefficients says: if $\sum a_nn^{-s}$ converges for $\operatorname{Re}s > \sigma_c$ but not to the left, then its sum cannot be continued holomorphically to a neighbourhood of the real point $\sigma_c$. (The proof is the one given in Chapter 30: expand in a Taylor series at a real point to the right of $\sigma_c$, and use positivity to exchange the sums.) Since $F$ is holomorphic on the real interval $(0, \infty)$, the series $\sum a_nn^{-s}$ must converge for every real $s > 0$. But at $s = \frac12$,
$$\sum_n\frac{a_n}{\sqrt n} \ge \sum_m\frac{a_{m^2}}{m} \ge \sum_m\frac1m = \infty.$$
This contradiction shows $L(1, \chi) \ne 0$.
:::

That completes the proof of Dirichlet’s theorem. Dirichlet’s own argument for real characters was different: he computed $L(1, \chi)$ in terms of the class number of a quadratic form, which is a positive integer. That **class number formula** is a deep result in its own right; for example $L(1, \chi_{-4}) = 1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \cdots = \frac\pi4$ corresponds to the class number $1$ of the form $x^2 + y^2$.

## Equidistribution

Dirichlet’s theorem says each admissible class gets infinitely many primes. In fact they share them equally.

::residue-shares

:::theorem{name="PNT for arithmetic progressions" who="de la Vallée Poussin" year=1896 status=stated}
If $\gcd(a, q) = 1$, then $\pi(x; q, a) = \#\{p \le x : p \equiv a \pmod q\} \sim \dfrac{\pi(x)}{\varphi(q)}$.
:::

The proof combines this chapter with Chapters 24–26: one needs $L(1 + it, \chi) \ne 0$ for all real $t$ (the 3–4–1 argument works, exercise 5) and Newman’s method for each $L$-function. Quantitative versions raise new problems. The error term depends on the zeros of $L(s, \chi)$, and the **generalised Riemann Hypothesis** (all nontrivial zeros on $\operatorname{Re}s = \frac12$) would give $\pi(x; q, a) = \frac{\operatorname{Li}(x)}{\varphi(q)} + O(\sqrt x\log(qx))$. Unconditionally, a possible real zero of $L(s, \chi)$ very close to $s = 1$ (a **Siegel zero**) cannot yet be ruled out, and it limits what can be proved when $q$ is large compared with $x$.

How far must one go to find the first prime $\equiv a \pmod q$? Linnik proved in 1944 that it is at most $q^L$ for an absolute constant $L$; the current record is $L = 5$ (Xylouris, 2011). GRH would give about $q^2\log^2q$, and it is conjectured that $q^{1 + \varepsilon}$ suffices.

:::key
- If $\gcd(a, q) = 1$ there are infinitely many primes $\equiv a \pmod q$, and $\sum_{p \equiv a}1/p$ diverges.
- Characters $\chi$ modulo $q$ isolate the class $a$: $\frac1{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi\overline{\chi(a)}\chi(n) = [n \equiv a]$. Their $L$-functions have Euler products, and $\sum_{p \equiv a}p^{-s} = \frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_\chi\overline{\chi(a)}\log L(s, \chi) + O(1)$.
- The principal character gives the divergence; the others must stay bounded, which is $L(1, \chi) \ne 0$.
- Complex $\chi$: $\prod_\chi L(s, \chi) \ge 1$ and two zeros would beat one pole. Real $\chi$: $\zeta(s)L(s, \chi)$ has nonnegative coefficients with $a_{m^2} \ge 1$, and Landau’s theorem forbids a cancellation of the pole.
- The primes are equidistributed among the $\varphi(q)$ classes; error terms involve GRH and possible Siegel zeros.
:::
