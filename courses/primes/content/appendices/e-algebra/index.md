The course uses a small amount of abstract algebra, mostly as vocabulary: it lets one argument cover many situations at once. This appendix collects the definitions and the few theorems needed, each illustrated by the example from the course that uses it. Nothing here is needed before Chapter 5.

## Groups

:::definition
A **group** is a set $G$ with an operation $\cdot$ (written as multiplication) such that

1. $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ for all $a, b, c$ (**associativity**);
2. there is an **identity** $e$ with $e \cdot a = a \cdot e = a$ for all $a$;
3. every $a$ has an **inverse** $a^{-1}$ with $a \cdot a^{-1} = a^{-1} \cdot a = e$.

The group is **abelian** (commutative) if $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all $a, b$. Its **order** $|G|$ is its number of elements.
:::

Every group in this course is abelian except the permutations in Chapter 6's necklace proof. The main examples:

| Group | Operation | Identity | Order | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | $+$ | $0$ | infinite | throughout |
| $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ | $+$ modulo $n$ | $0$ | $n$ | Chapter 5 |
| $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, the units mod $n$ | $\times$ modulo $n$ | $1$ | $\varphi(n)$ | Chapters 6, 8 |
| Dirichlet characters mod $q$ | pointwise $\times$ | $\chi_0$ | $\varphi(q)$ | Chapter 33 |
| Points of an elliptic curve over $\mathbb{F}_p$ | chord and tangent | point at infinity | $\approx p$ | Chapter 32 |

Two quick consequences of the axioms: the identity is unique, and so is each inverse (if $b$ and $c$ are both inverses of $a$ then $b = b(ac) = (ba)c = c$). And you can cancel: if $ab = ac$ then multiplying on the left by $a^{-1}$ gives $b = c$. That is the step used in both proofs of Fermat’s little theorem.

## Subgroups, cosets and Lagrange

A **subgroup** of $G$ is a subset $H$ that contains $e$ and is closed under the operation and inverses. Examples: the even integers in $\mathbb{Z}$; the squares among the units modulo $p$ (Chapter 9); the powers of a single element $a$, written $\langle a \rangle$.

For a subgroup $H$ and an element $g$, the **coset** $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$ is a “shifted copy” of $H$.

:::theorem{name="Lagrange’s theorem" status=proved}
If $H$ is a subgroup of a finite group $G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$.
:::

:::proof
The cosets of $H$ cover $G$, since $g \in gH$. Each coset has exactly $|H|$ elements, because $h \mapsto gh$ is one-to-one (cancel $g$). Two cosets are either equal or disjoint: if $x \in g_1 H \cap g_2 H$, then $x = g_1 h_1 = g_2 h_2$, so $g_1 = g_2 h_2 h_1^{-1}$ and $g_1 H = g_2 (h_2 h_1^{-1} H) = g_2 H$. So $G$ is a disjoint union of cosets of equal size $|H|$, and $|H|$ divides $|G|$.
:::

The **order** of an element $a$ is the smallest $k \ge 1$ with $a^k = e$, if one exists. In a finite group it always does, and it equals $|\langle a \rangle|$, the size of the subgroup of powers of $a$. So:

:::corollary{status=proved}
In a finite group $G$, the order of every element divides $|G|$, and $a^{|G|} = e$.
:::

With $G = (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ this is Euler’s theorem $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$; with $n = p$ prime it is Fermat’s little theorem. The cycles in Chapter 6's widget are the cosets of $\langle a \rangle$.

## Cyclic groups

A group is **cyclic** if it consists of the powers of a single element $g$, a **generator**. $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ under addition is cyclic, generated by $1$. Chapter 6 proves that $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic for every prime $p$; its generators are the primitive roots. More generally, $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic exactly when $n$ is $1$, $2$, $4$, $p^k$ or $2p^k$ for an odd prime $p$ (Gauss; stated here without proof). For example, $(\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{1, 3, 5, 7\}$ is not cyclic, since every element squares to $1$.

In a cyclic group of order $n$ generated by $g$, the element $g^k$ has order $n / \gcd(k, n)$, and so there are exactly $\varphi(d)$ elements of order $d$ for each divisor $d$ of $n$ (Chapter 6 proves these facts in the case it needs).

Every finite abelian group is a product of cyclic groups (the **structure theorem**, stated without proof). For the units modulo $n$, the Chinese remainder theorem gives the decomposition explicitly: $(\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z})^\times \cong (\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^\times \times (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ for coprime $m, n$. The course’s library uses exactly this to build Dirichlet characters (Chapter 33).

## Homomorphisms

A **homomorphism** is a map $f : G \to H$ between groups with $f(ab) = f(a) f(b)$. A bijective homomorphism is an **isomorphism**, written $G \cong H$: the two groups are the same up to renaming. Examples:

- reduction modulo $n$, $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$;
- the Chinese remainder map $x \mapsto (x \bmod m, x \bmod n)$, an isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ for coprime $m, n$;
- for a primitive root $g$ modulo $p$, $k \mapsto g^k$, an isomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}/(p-1)\mathbb{Z}$ (under $+$) to $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ (under $\times$); its inverse is the discrete logarithm;
- the Legendre symbol $a \mapsto \left(\frac ap\right)$, a homomorphism from $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ to $\{\pm 1\}$ (Chapter 9); Dirichlet characters generalise it (Chapter 33).

## Rings and fields

:::definition
A **ring** (in this course: commutative, with $1$) is a set with addition and multiplication such that it is an abelian group under addition, multiplication is associative and commutative with identity $1$, and $a(b + c) = ab + ac$. A **unit** is an element with a multiplicative inverse. A **field** is a ring with $1 \neq 0$ in which every non-zero element is a unit.
:::

Rings: $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (Chapter 35), $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ (Chapter 3), polynomials. Fields: $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for $p$ prime. $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a field exactly when $n$ is prime: for composite $n = ab$, the elements $a$ and $b$ are **zero divisors** ($ab = 0$ with $a, b \ne 0$), which can never be units.

There are finite fields with $p^k$ elements for every prime power, unique up to isomorphism, written $\mathbb{F}_{p^k}$ (stated without proof). Chapter 32 counts points on curves over them. Note that $\mathbb{F}_{p^k}$ is *not* $\mathbb{Z}/p^k\mathbb{Z}$, which has zero divisors when $k \ge 2$.

## Polynomials over a field

Polynomials with coefficients in a field $F$ can be divided with remainder, just like integers: if $g \ne 0$, then $f = qg + r$ with $\deg r < \deg g$. (Long division works because you can always divide by the leading coefficient of $g$.) Consequently there are a Euclidean algorithm, a Bézout identity and unique factorisation into irreducible polynomials, with the same proofs as in Chapters 2 and 3.

Two facts about roots are used repeatedly:

:::theorem{status=proved}
Let $F$ be a field and $f \in F[x]$.

1. (**Factor theorem**) $f(r) = 0$ if and only if $x - r$ divides $f$.
2. (**Lagrange**) If $f \ne 0$ has degree $d$, then $f$ has at most $d$ roots in $F$.
:::

:::proof
1. Divide: $f = (x - r) q + c$ with $c$ constant; evaluating at $r$ gives $c = f(r)$.
2. Induction on $d$. If $r$ is a root, $f = (x - r) q$ with $\deg q = d - 1$. Any other root $s$ satisfies $(s - r) q(s) = 0$, and $s - r \ne 0$ is invertible in a field, so $q(s) = 0$. By induction $q$ has at most $d - 1$ roots.
:::

Over a ring with zero divisors, part 2 fails: $x^2 - 1$ has four roots modulo $8$. This is exactly where the hypothesis "$p$ prime" enters the proofs of primitive roots (Chapter 6), Euler’s criterion (Chapter 9) and the Miller–Rabin test (Chapter 8).

A consequence worth remembering: over $\mathbb{F}_p$, Fermat’s little theorem says every element is a root of $x^p - x$, which has degree $p$, so
$$x^p - x = \prod_{a \in \mathbb{F}_p} (x - a) \qquad\text{in } \mathbb{F}_p[x].$$
Comparing the coefficients of $x$ on both sides gives Wilson’s theorem again.

```quiz
q: Which of these is a field?
options:
  - text: $\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}$
    correct: true
    why: $7$ is prime, so every non-zero residue is coprime to $7$ and has an inverse.
  - text: $\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$
    why: $3 \cdot 3 = 9 \equiv 0$, so $3$ is a zero divisor and has no inverse. (There is a field with $9$ elements, $\mathbb{F}_9$, but it is not $\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}$.)
  - text: $\mathbb{Z}$
    why: $2$ has no inverse in $\mathbb{Z}$. The integers form a ring, not a field.
```
