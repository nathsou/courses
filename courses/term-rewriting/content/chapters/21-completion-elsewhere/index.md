Completion is not only a tool for universal algebra. The same idea (orient, find the overlaps, add their consequences, simplify) appears in computational group theory, in the decision procedures inside SMT solvers, and in computer algebra. This optional chapter visits all three.

## Groups given by generators and relations

A group can be presented by **generators** and **relations**: the symmetric group $S_3$, the six permutations of three objects, is generated by two transpositions $a$ and $b$ subject to
$$ a^2 = e, \qquad b^2 = e, \qquad (ab)^3 = e. $$
Elements are words in $a$ and $b$, and two words denote the same element when the relations (with the group axioms) prove them equal. That is a word problem, and completion can solve it. Writing products right-nested (`#infixr`), words become terms such as `A * (B * A)`:

```girafe title="A presentation of the symmetric group" tools="completion"
#infixr 7 *
#precedence * > B > A > E
(x * y) * z == x * (y * z)
E * x == x
x * E == x
A * A == E
B * B == E
A * (B * (A * (B * (A * B)))) == E
? B * (A * B) == A * (B * A)
? A * (B * (A * B)) == B * A
```

Completion succeeds with nine rules, among them $bab \to aba$, the braid relation. The irreducible words are $e, a, b, ab, ba, aba$: six of them, exactly the six elements of $S_3$, so the rules decide the word problem and even count the group. Rewriting systems of this kind are called **string rewriting systems** (each term is really a string of generators), and completion for them is a standard tool of computational group theory, as in Derek Holt's KBMAG package for GAP. Part X shows the limit: some finitely presented groups have an undecidable word problem (Novikov and Boone, in the 1950s), so for them no finite convergent system exists, for any order.:cite[novikov1955,boone1959]

## Ground equations and congruence closure

When the equations have no variables, completion becomes much better behaved.

:::theorem{status=stated}
For ground equations and a reduction order that is total on ground terms (such as LPO with a total precedence), completion always terminates, with a convergent ground system.
:::

The reason is that ground critical pairs only arise when one left-hand side is a subterm of another, and inter-reduction then shrinks the system; no new terms bigger than the original ones are ever created. Baader and Nipkow give the proof.:cite[baader-nipkow1998]

The same problem (decide whether $s = t$ follows from finitely many ground equations, in a theory where function symbols only need to respect equality) is solved in SMT solvers by **congruence closure**, the algorithm of Greg Nelson and Derek Oppen and of Peter Downey, Ravi Sethi and Robert Tarjan.:cite[nelson-oppen1980,downey-sethi-tarjan1980] It represents all subterms in a graph, merges classes with union-find and propagates congruence: if $a = b$ then $f(a) = f(b)$. Congruence closure is ground completion with a clever data structure, and that data structure, the **e-graph**, is the subject of Chapter 23. (The collection's course on formal verification builds congruence closure inside an SMT solver, in its chapter on equality and functions.)

```girafe title="Ground equations" tools="completion"
#precedence F > A > B > C
F(F(F(A))) == A
F(F(F(F(F(A))))) == A
? F(A) == A
```

From $f^3(a) = a$ and $f^5(a) = a$ it follows that $f(a) = a$ (since $\gcd(3, 5) = 1$). Ground completion finds it.

## Gröbner bases

Bruno Buchberger, in his 1965 thesis, gave an algorithm for multivariate polynomial equations that is completion in disguise.:cite[buchberger1965] Fix an order on monomials (say lexicographic, with $x > y$). A polynomial $p$ becomes a rewrite rule: its leading monomial rewrites to minus the rest. For example $x^2 - y$ becomes $x^2 \to y$, which reduces $x^3 + x$ to $xy + x$.

| Completion | Buchberger's algorithm |
|---|---|
| equations | polynomials (generating an ideal) |
| reduction order | monomial order |
| orient an equation | take the leading monomial |
| critical pair of two rules | S-polynomial of two polynomials (where their leading monomials overlap) |
| joinable critical pair | S-polynomial reduces to 0 |
| convergent system | Gröbner basis |
| word problem | ideal membership |

A **Gröbner basis** is a set of polynomials whose rewrite system is confluent: every polynomial in the ideal reduces to 0, and every polynomial has a unique remainder. Buchberger's algorithm computes S-polynomials, reduces them, and adds the non-zero remainders, exactly as completion adds non-joinable critical pairs. Unlike general completion, it always terminates (by Dickson's lemma, a cousin of Higman's lemma from Chapter 13). Gröbner bases are the workhorse of computer algebra systems for solving polynomial systems, and the parallel with Knuth–Bendix completion was noticed early and made precise in the 1980s.

:::key
- Group presentations: completion of string rewriting systems decides the word problem and can enumerate a finite group.
- Ground equations always complete; congruence closure on e-graphs is the efficient version.
- Buchberger's algorithm is completion for polynomials: S-polynomials are critical pairs, Gröbner bases are convergent systems.
:::
