An adult can find food, travel, repair tissues and raise children. A young child needs energy for many of the same processes but cannot acquire enough food alone. Human reproduction therefore involves a problem that extends beyond the mother’s body: how can a community support several people whose consumption exceeds their production?

In this chapter, follow energy from a landscape into a body, then across a lifetime and between people. You will distinguish an energy constraint from a complete explanation of fertility, longevity or social organisation.

## Session 1 · From a landscape to a body

### Food is energy with a history

Imagine two landscapes of the same area. One has abundant edible plants throughout the year. The other has a short productive season, scattered resources and large animals that take time to locate. Equal areas need not support equal populations. What matters is accessible production, its timing, and the work needed to obtain it.

Most food webs begin with photosynthesis. Plants turn some incoming solar energy into chemical energy. Herbivores consume part of that production, and predators consume part of the herbivores. At each transfer, energy is used in maintenance and movement, lost as heat, or remains in uneaten material and waste. Decomposers also participate in these flows. Energy does not circulate indefinitely in the way atoms can cycle through an ecosystem.

This gives a useful first explanation of why a predator may need a large territory. Feeding high in a food web generally draws on a smaller share of primary production. But a trophic diagram is not a map of actual food availability. Humans combine resources from different levels, move between patches, store food and transform it. Transfer efficiencies differ; the familiar “10% rule” is an illustration, not a universal coefficient.

**Carrying capacity** means the population an environment can support under specified conditions. Change the technology, diet, access rights, season or acceptable risk, and the estimate changes. Standing biomass—the material present now—is also different from the rate at which edible resources are renewed. A calculation based on all the animals currently present could consume the very stock that sustains future reproduction.

Hublin begins with these ecological constraints because human energy acquisition is not simply a question of appetite. A calorie that is chemically present in a landscape may be inaccessible, seasonally absent or too costly to extract.

### Keep the accounts straight

Food energy is usually expressed in kilocalories, abbreviated **kcal**. One nutritional kilocalorie is 4.184 kilojoules. A watt measures energy per second, so energy over a day and average power describe related quantities, but they are not interchangeable units.

An individual account distinguishes:

- **Metabolizable intake:** food energy available after digestive losses.
- **Resting expenditure:** energy used to maintain the organism at rest; basal metabolism is measured under more restrictive standard conditions.
- **Activity above rest:** the additional expenditure associated with movement and other tasks.
- **The thermic effect of food:** expenditure associated with processing and assimilating a meal.
- **Stored or retained energy:** the balance that can enter reserves or contribute to growth and reproduction.

For an illustrative adult with no growth or reproductive investment, intake minus expenditure gives the change in stored energy. This is an accounting identity, not a prediction that every extra calorie becomes a fixed amount of body mass.

Organs differ sharply in expenditure per unit mass. The brain is a small fraction of adult mass, yet accounts for roughly one fifth of resting expenditure. Liver, kidneys and heart are also metabolically costly. Muscles contribute to resting expenditure as well as to the additional work of movement. A large organ is not necessarily expensive per gram, and a small one is not necessarily cheap.

The brain’s cost is **inside** resting expenditure. It must not be added again to a total that already includes it. Likewise, much metabolic expenditure eventually appears as heat; “energy spent” and “heat produced” are often two views of the same process, not independent bills.

### A bigger budget can matter too

Trade-offs are real, but evolution need not redistribute a permanently fixed pie. An organism can also acquire and process more energy. [Pontzer and colleagues’ 2016 comparison](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature17654), using doubly labelled water to estimate total expenditure, found higher human expenditure than in the sampled great apes after accounting for body size and activity. Their comparison helps explain why brains, reproduction and long lives can coexist without a single compensating reduction paying for everything.

That evidence concerns a comparative sample, not a universal numerical budget for every past human. We will return to the difference between redistributing expenditure and increasing throughput in the next chapter.

## Session 2 · The timing of a life

### Growth, maintenance and reproduction

An organism uses energy to become larger, maintain itself and produce offspring. Those demands also have a schedule. Investing in growth now may postpone reproduction but increase later reproductive capacity. Maintenance can improve survival, while producing many offspring may leave less investment available for each. The consequences depend on mortality, food and the organism’s other traits.

**Life-history theory** studies these patterns: size at birth, developmental pace, age at first reproduction, birth spacing, parental investment, survival and longevity. It concerns evolved patterns and individual responses to conditions; it does not imply conscious planning by a species.

The original lecture contrasts relatively fast and slow strategies using the historical **r/K** vocabulary. That distinction offers an entry into the subject, but actual life histories vary along several dimensions. Species do not all fit two boxes. Body size, environment and evolutionary relatedness also shape comparisons.

Great apes generally develop slowly, reproduce relatively late and invest heavily in a small number of offspring. Humans extend some of those tendencies while combining them with relatively early weaning and, in many populations, shorter birth intervals than other great apes. The combination is the puzzle: prolonged dependence does not prevent another child from arriving.

### Read a survival curve carefully

For a cohort of newborns, a survival curve describes the fraction still alive at each age. Its early decline can substantially lower life expectancy at birth even when some adults live for decades. “Life expectancy was low” is therefore not equivalent to “everyone died young.” Maximum lifespan, average lifespan and the probability of surviving to a particular age answer different questions.

![Three schematic survival curves distinguish high early mortality, constant mortality rate and a late increase. They show fractions surviving, not the distribution of ages at death.](../../figures/survival.svg)

*Original schematic. The area under a survival curve relates to average lifespan; the curves are not data for named species. A constant mortality rate produces an exponential decline in survival, not a straight-line survival curve.*

Fossil samples add another complication. A collection of deaths is not automatically a representative population. Preservation, burial, recovery, age estimation and the circumstances that produced the assemblage all affect the distribution. Tooth wear can help classify adults, but wear rates also depend on diet and behaviour. An apparent change in the share of older fossils needs these alternatives considered before it becomes a demographic conclusion.

The same caution applies to fertility. Energy availability can affect the ability to reproduce, but the number and timing of births also depend on health, mortality, institutions, preferences and access to resources. A correlation between national energy consumption and fertility cannot establish that metabolism caused the demographic pattern. Household expenditure, industrial fuel use and biological expenditure are different accounts.

## Session 3 · Raising children together

### Weaning is not independence

Weaning ends dependence on breast milk. It does not necessarily end dependence on other people’s food, work or knowledge. After weaning, a child may still consume more than they acquire. Food transfers allow overlapping dependants to be supported while adults pursue tasks with delayed or uncertain returns.

**Alloparental care** means care provided by individuals other than the parents. Parents, siblings, grandparents and other community members can contribute in different ways. Provisioning includes more than handing over calories: protection, carrying, preparing food and teaching can change a child’s needs and eventual acquisition.

These observations support the importance of cooperation in human life history. They do not determine one ancestral family structure, a universal division of labour or the identity of every helper. Contemporary foraging communities are living societies with their own histories, not direct replicas of the Pleistocene.

:::figure sharing

The model reveals two distinct problems. A group may have enough food in total while particular individuals remain short. Transfers can solve that allocation problem. A poor season may instead leave the whole group below its needs. Sharing can then distribute the shortfall, but it cannot create resources. Storage, movement, alternative foods and broader exchange networks may become important.

### Why live beyond reproduction?

Humans often survive well beyond the end of female reproduction. The **grandmother hypothesis** proposes that helping descendants can contribute to the evolution of prolonged survival. It has motivated observations and demographic models, but showing that a grandmother helps is not the same as fully explaining why reproduction stops. Other explanations consider reproductive conflict, mortality risks and the relationships between lifespan and ovarian ageing.

### Keep these distinctions

An individual can depend on transfers despite an adequate group-wide supply. Early weaning can coexist with prolonged dependence. A long post-reproductive life is an observation; a hypothesis about its evolutionary advantages needs additional tests.
