Food leaves traces even when the food itself is gone. A tooth’s shape, scratches on enamel, residues in calculus and isotope ratios in bone each record a different part of an individual’s relationship with food.

In this chapter, reconstruct a diet from those imperfect records, then examine the returns from acquisition and processing. You will learn why “animal protein was important” and “every calorie came from meat” are very different claims.

## Session 1 · Reconstructing a vanished diet

### Several records, several time windows

Tooth shape influences the foods an animal can process. Large crushing surfaces, cutting edges and enamel thickness suggest different mechanical demands. But anatomy can be especially important for **fallback foods** eaten during a difficult season, rather than the foods most commonly eaten. A robust tooth does not prove a lifetime of eating only hard objects.

**Microwear** consists of small pits and scratches produced during food processing. Its texture can help distinguish mechanical properties of recent foods, while grit and other behaviour complicate the interpretation. It often reflects a relatively short period before death. Two animals with the same seasonal diet may have different microwear if they died in different seasons.

**Dental calculus** is mineralised plaque. It can trap starch grains, phytoliths and other residues. These can establish contact with particular materials, subject to contamination, preservation and identification. A residue is evidence of food use or contact; its count is not a direct measure of the share of calories consumed. Teeth could also be used as tools.

Bones and teeth differ in their recording windows. Enamel forms during development and does not remodel like bone. Bone collagen can integrate later years, with rates depending on the tissue. Comparing an enamel value, a collagen value and recent microwear means comparing different periods as well as different kinds of evidence.

### Isotopes: a comparison, not a menu

Isotopes are forms of an element with different numbers of neutrons. Stable isotopes do not decay over the timescale of the investigation. Biological and environmental processes affect their relative abundance, making isotope ratios useful tracers.

Researchers often express small differences from a reference as **δ values**, in parts per thousand, written **‰**. A value of 5‰ is not 5%. Carbon and nitrogen isotopes answer related but distinct questions.

Carbon pathways distinguish some plant resources: **C₃** and **C₄** photosynthesis produce different isotopic patterns. An animal may acquire those signals by eating plants directly or by eating animals that consumed them. A C₄ contribution alone therefore does not prove meat eating. Marine and freshwater food webs also require appropriate baselines.

Nitrogen isotope values often rise along food chains, but the magnitude varies. Climate, local vegetation, tissue, nursing and physiological conditions can influence the result. The question is not simply “is the value high?” but “high relative to which local organisms, measured in which tissue, from which period?”

Bone collagen is particularly informative about dietary protein. Much of a diet’s energy can come from fat or carbohydrates whose contribution is not represented in the same way. A high collagen nitrogen value cannot be converted directly into a percentage of total calories from meat.

### Preservation determines where we can look

Collagen often survives better in some environments than others. A dataset dominated by colder sites may therefore tell us more about those settings than about a species’ full range. **Diagenesis**—change after burial—can alter a signal; preservation tests are part of the measurement rather than an optional afterthought.

The lecture discusses mineral-based approaches, including zinc isotopes, as promising ways to study contexts without preserved collagen. Different chemical proxies require their own calibration; using a more durable tissue does not remove all uncertainty.

## Session 2 · The return from acquiring food

### Meat eating has a deeper history

Chimpanzees sometimes hunt and consume animal foods, with variation between communities and individuals. Meat eating is therefore not a behaviour that appeared suddenly with modern humans. Around two million years ago, archaeological evidence for hominin carcass processing becomes substantially more prominent, alongside changes within early *Homo*.

Cut marks, percussion damage and associated tools can establish carcass processing. Distinguishing hunting from scavenging requires more: the order of marks, carcass parts, carnivore involvement, site formation and ecological context. A processed bone is not a film of the acquisition event.

Animal foods provide protein, fat and micronutrients, but carcasses vary in composition. Lean tissue, fatty tissue and marrow have different energetic and nutritional consequences. Food availability is not only “how much meat?” Seasonal animal condition and access to fat can matter. Plant foods, underground storage organs, nuts and aquatic resources can also supply important energy and nutrients.

### A large package can be an uncertain return

Foraging returns depend on the whole sequence: searching, pursuing, capturing, handling, transporting and processing. Dividing a successful carcass’s food energy by the time spent killing it leaves out failed searches and unsuccessful hunts.

An expected return averages successes and failures. It does not erase risk. A strategy can offer a high average while regularly delivering nothing on a particular day. Storage and sharing may buffer those fluctuations, especially when acquisitions are not perfectly correlated between people.

The lecture considers why people sometimes pursue large prey even when a simple energy-per-hour ranking favours smaller resources. Distribution rules, cooperation, prestige, knowledge and social relationships can influence behaviour. Describing such considerations is not evidence that energetic constraints are irrelevant; it shows that calories are not the only variable in a behavioural explanation.

Archaeological remains also reflect site function. A hunting location, a processing site and a residential camp can preserve different parts of the same food system. A pile of particular bones may record selective transport rather than the entire diet. Seasonal indicators, including tooth cementum, can help investigate when animals were taken.

## Session 3 · Processing food, reading fire

### Technology can move work outside the body

Cutting and pounding alter particle size and mechanical resistance. Heating can change starch and protein accessibility. These processes can reduce chewing effort or change the energy available after digestion, although effects vary with food and treatment.

[Zink and Lieberman’s 2016 experiment](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature16990) measured the effects of meat and mechanical processing on chewing. It makes an important point for evolutionary explanations: some benefits of processing do not require cooking. An observed reduction in chewing work is a mechanism, while the archaeological date of a technology is a separate question.

Preparation itself requires work. Fuel must be obtained, food processed and fires maintained. Costs can be shared across several eaters. Human metabolic effort and fuel combustion are different energy flows: the person gathering wood expends food energy, while the wood contributes external heat.

### Four questions about fire

Evidence for fire **occurrence**, fire **maintenance**, deliberate **ignition** and obligatory **dependence** answers four different questions. Burnt material can originate in natural fire, be moved after burning or lose its original context. Even a secure hearth does not establish that every meal was cooked.

### Return to the Neanderthal menu

Hublin ends by questioning a simple interpretation of Neanderthals as exclusive meat eaters. High animal-protein signals can coexist with plant use documented in calculus and other remains. Preservation differences limit comparisons across climates; ecological variation limits extrapolation across regions.

:::question diet

Combining proxies strengthens an account when their recording windows and inferential limits are respected. The strongest conclusion may be that animal protein was important in a particular population, while the shares of fat, plant carbohydrate and other calories remain uncertain. Next, we ask what it cost to reach those foods.
