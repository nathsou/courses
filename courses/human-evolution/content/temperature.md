Most of the energy a moving body uses eventually becomes heat. Keeping internal conditions within a workable range therefore connects metabolism to the environment. Cooling can require water; keeping warm can require additional metabolism, insulation or a changed microclimate.

This chapter follows the heat, examines what body geometry can explain, and then investigates the evidence for hair loss, clothing and shelter. You will distinguish a physiological mechanism from an archaeological date.

## Session 1 · Balancing heat

### Producing heat and keeping a temperature

**Endothermy** means generating substantial body heat metabolically. **Homeothermy** means maintaining a relatively stable temperature. The two concepts are related but distinct: an endotherm’s temperature can vary, and an organism can be thermally stable under particular conditions without producing most of its own heat.

Mammals regulate temperature through interacting physiological and behavioural responses. The hypothalamus contributes to this regulation. Blood flow changes the distribution of heat between the core and skin. Sweating, shivering, metabolic heat production, posture and choices of location or activity change the balance.

Heat crosses the body’s boundary by **radiation**, **convection**, **conduction** and **evaporation**. Radiation depends on surrounding radiant conditions, not only air temperature. Convection depends on the skin–air temperature difference and air movement. Conduction involves direct contact. Evaporation removes heat when water changes phase.

If the environment is hotter than the skin, some routes can bring heat into the body. An outward arrow is not a permanent property of a pathway. Clothing changes exchange with the environment, and shade changes radiant exposure.

### Sweat has to evaporate

Producing sweat does not guarantee cooling. Water that drips away has not removed the same latent heat as water evaporating at the skin. Humidity, wind and clothing affect evaporation through the vapour-pressure gradient and the resistance to water-vapour transfer.

Human sweating can support sustained activity, but it also involves a water cost. That connection helps explain why heat, distance travel and food acquisition cannot be considered independently. A route rich in food might still impose water and thermal constraints.

### Several ways to increase heat production

Shivering involves muscle activity. **Brown adipose tissue**, or brown fat, generates heat through mitochondrial processes that can dissipate energy without using it all to produce ATP. The high mitochondrial content contributes to its appearance and function.

Brown fat is especially relevant to infants, whose surface area relative to volume is high and whose thermoregulatory capacities differ from adults. Active brown fat also occurs in adults; its amount and activation vary. [Cypess and colleagues (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa0810780) documented this with imaging evidence. Its existence is not a fixed extra metabolic allowance that can be assigned to every fossil population.

## Session 2 · Hair, proportions and climate

### Hair loss has indirect evidence

Reduced body hair can affect heat exchange and evaporation, while scalp hair, pigmentation and other traits have their own functions and histories. The sequence of their evolution is difficult to recover because hair rarely fossilises.

Parasites offer indirect evidence. Humans and other apes host related lice whose evolutionary relationships can help reconstruct changes in habitat and host use. [Reed and colleagues (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1186/1741-7007-5-7) investigated the relationship between human pubic lice and gorilla lice. Such results can support a history of host transfer and different hair habitats, but they do not directly preserve the date of a completely hairless body.

Molecular dates depend on evolutionary-rate assumptions, calibration and the interpretation of the ecological event. A parasite divergence, a host transfer and a change in human hair distribution are related possible events, not necessarily the same event.

### Geometry changes exchange opportunities

For geometrically similar bodies, volume increases faster than surface area as linear dimensions increase. A larger body therefore has less surface per unit volume. Changing proportions while holding volume fixed can also change the exposed area.

:::figure geometry

The classic **Bergmann** and **Allen** patterns connect climatic variation to body size and appendage proportions. Broadly, a relatively compact build can reduce exchange opportunities, while longer extremities can increase them. But these are comparative tendencies, not universal laws that determine each person’s anatomy.

Developmental conditions, nutrition, ancestry, activity and culture also influence human variation. Skin blood flow and insulation affect actual exchange, while a geometric ratio by itself does not measure temperature tolerance. Body proportions are one part of a climatic account, not a ranking of populations.

### A fossil body is not a thermometer

Neanderthal proportions have often been interpreted in relation to cold conditions. The original lecture makes an important qualification: a cold-associated body form does not prove that Neanderthals routinely occupied the most extreme Arctic environments. Distribution also depends on resources, seasonality, technology and population history.

The nose provides a further example of the need to specify mechanisms. External dimensions alone do not measure the conditioning of inspired air. Internal passages, airflow and respiratory demands matter. [Wroe and colleagues (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2018.0085) used computational reconstructions to investigate aspects of Neanderthal facial function. Their results depend on model geometry and boundary conditions; they are not direct measurements of a living Neanderthal.

The same reasoning applies to body energy estimates. A reconstruction can compare explicit assumptions and test plausibility. Its numerical precision should not conceal uncertainty about soft tissues, insulation and actual behaviour.

## Session 3 · Making a habitable microclimate

### A changed environment can protect a body

Fire, shelter and clothing alter the surroundings against which physiology works. They can reduce wind exposure, change radiant conditions and retain air near the skin. They also require materials, skill, maintenance and energy. The costs connect this chapter to acquisition and processing in chapter 3.

Archaeological evidence for shelter can include spatial arrangements, postholes, building materials and activity patterns. An arrangement of large bones may support a structural interpretation, but its formation needs examination. Bones can accumulate through several processes; a reconstruction of a hut is an interpretation of preserved traces.

Clothing evidence is also often indirect. Hide-processing tools, use-wear, animal remains, awls and needles contribute different clues. A species valued for its fur may have been obtained for more than one purpose. A tool capable of piercing skin does not preserve the garment it produced.

### Lice and needles record different things

Body lice live in clothing. Their divergence from head lice therefore supplies a possible constraint on clothing use, under the study’s evolutionary and ecological assumptions. [Toups and colleagues (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msq234) placed that origin well before the earliest surviving eyed needles. The broad dating uncertainty is part of the inference; the estimate is not a direct date for the first garment.

Eyed needles document a technology that can support fine sewing. Clothing can precede eyed needles: skins may be draped, fastened, bound or stitched using other implements. The earliest preserved implement is also a minimum known antiquity, not necessarily an invention date.

<div class="update"><strong>Since the lecture · Needles and the history of dress.</strong> Gilligan and colleagues (2024) reviewed Palaeolithic eyed needles, distinguishing their technical capabilities from the origins of clothing itself. The presence or absence of a needle should therefore be interpreted with other evidence, not used as a binary test for whether people wore clothes. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adp2887">Read the review</a>.</div>

The lecture compares clothing and occupation in Neanderthal and later *Homo sapiens* contexts. Those comparisons remain valuable when framed as evidence for particular technologies and environments. They should not become an assumption that every Neanderthal wore one simple kind of covering, or that every modern human group had the same sewn garments.

### The body and the setting belong together

No physiological trait alone explains the colonisation of a climate. Food, water, thermal conditions, mobility, materials and social support all contribute. A shelter does not eliminate the energy problem; it changes the terms of it.

This is the bridge to the final lecture. Humans repeatedly changed their environments, and those changes could then alter the conditions of survival, reproduction and selection.
