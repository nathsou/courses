The brain is costly even while its owner is resting. Maintaining cells, transmitting signals and restoring ion gradients require a continuous supply of energy. A growing child must support this organ while also developing the rest of the body.

This chapter separates brain size, energetic demand and maturation, then asks what teeth and skulls can reveal about extinct children. The main goal is to understand why a developmental measurement is not automatically a complete life history.

## Session 1 · Measuring an expensive organ

### Large compared with what?

A larger animal usually has a larger brain. Comparing two species by brain mass alone therefore confuses overall body size with relative investment in the brain. Researchers study **allometry**: how one measurement changes with another, often by fitting a power law such as brain mass = a × body massᵇ. On logarithmic axes, that relationship becomes a straight line.

An **encephalization quotient** compares observed brain size with the size expected from a chosen reference relationship. Change the reference species or fitted relationship, and the quotient changes. It is a comparative measure, not a universal score of intelligence.

Humans have a large brain relative to body size within primates. Hominin fossils also show that brain and body size did not change in lockstep. Endocranial volume generally increased within parts of the genus *Homo*, with considerable variation and overlap. Neanderthals had large brains too; a larger fossil volume alone does not establish superior cognition or modern human-like development.

### What pays for the brain?

The **expensive-tissue hypothesis** proposed a relationship between dietary quality, a smaller digestive tract and a larger brain. The mechanism is plausible as a specific energetic trade-off: a diet that requires less digestive investment could permit spending elsewhere.

But several mechanisms can contribute. Food processing changes acquisition and digestion. Locomotion can change the work required to reach food. Growth and reproduction have a schedule. Fat reserves and transfers buffer unreliable supply. Total energy throughput can increase.

[Navarrete and colleagues’ 2011 comparative study](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10629) did not support a general brain–gut trade-off across their mammalian sample. [Pontzer and colleagues’ 2016 measurements](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature17654) support higher overall human expenditure relative to the sampled apes. Neither result makes trade-offs disappear. Together they argue against treating one reduced organ as the universal payment for the human brain.

Hublin’s lecture also considers differences in muscular investment and other bodily costs. Those comparisons need their own measurements. An energetic account can show that demands must be met; it cannot infer every evolutionary change from conservation alone.

### Volume and demand follow different curves

The brain’s energetic requirement is not a constant fraction of its volume. Synaptic activity, developmental processes and tissue composition change with age. A brain can be close to adult size while using more glucose than an adult brain.

[Kuzawa and colleagues (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1323099111) combined developmental brain glucose-uptake estimates with energy expenditure and body-growth data. They reported a childhood peak in glucose demand expressed as an energy equivalent of about **66% of resting expenditure** and **43% of daily energy requirements**. The high-demand period coincided with relatively slow body growth.

Those denominators matter. The figures are neither interchangeable nor a claim that two thirds of every child’s food always feeds the brain. Glucose uptake is also a specific measure; converting it into an energetic equivalent requires assumptions. The study supports an energetic developmental relationship, while a correlation between curves alone cannot prove that brain demand uniquely determines growth in every context.

## Session 2 · Birth, growth and dependence

### Birth has several constraints

A large infant head must pass through a birth canal whose dimensions also relate to locomotion and pelvic support. Maternal physiology must support fetal growth and the demands of pregnancy. These are connected constraints, but they are not one measurement.

The **obstetrical dilemma** emphasises the relationship between a bipedal pelvis and delivering a large-brained infant. The **energetics of gestation and growth** hypothesis emphasises the increasing maternal metabolic demands of pregnancy. Anatomical variation, fetal size, pelvic-floor support, nutritional conditions and the mechanisms that initiate labour all complicate a single explanation.

[Dunsworth and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1205282109) made the maternal energetic hypothesis explicit. A [later review by Haeusler and colleagues (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1111/brv.12744) evaluates the continuing role of anatomical and physiological constraints. The evidence does not justify a universal switch in which every pregnancy ends precisely when a fixed metabolic ceiling is reached. Nor does it make every aspect of pelvic form a simple compromise between walking and birth.

### A small ratio can have a large denominator

A human newborn’s brain is a smaller proportion of its eventual adult size than in several other primates. It is tempting to interpret that ratio as a direct measure of premature birth. But the ratio contains both neonatal and adult size. Extensive later enlargement can lower it even without relocating every developmental event outside the womb.

Their analysis attributes much of the unusual human neonatal/adult brain-size ratio to postnatal enlargement and shows why that ratio is insufficient to measure neurodevelopmental maturity. Human infants remain highly dependent; the revision concerns what a size ratio proves about the timing of neural development. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-023-02253-z">Read the study</a>.</div>

Maturation also continues after most enlargement. Changes in connections, myelination and functional organisation extend beyond the period of rapid volume growth. Experiences during development matter, but a fossil skull cannot reveal a child’s entire learning environment.

### The energetic role of other people

Food acquisition and consumption change at different rates during childhood. Weaning is followed by years in which children may need provisioning while acquiring skills and strength. The transfers introduced in chapter 1 therefore connect reproduction to brain development.

This is a collective constraint, not a complete history of social institutions. A household model can demonstrate a shortfall and a possible transfer. It cannot establish whether a particular fossil child was cared for by a grandmother, sibling or unrelated adult.

## Session 3 · Reading childhood in fossils

### Teeth contain a clock

Teeth preserve incremental growth features. Daily increments and longer-period lines can be counted to reconstruct formation. A **neonatal line**, where preserved and identified, can help connect a tooth’s growth record to birth. Researchers synchronise different teeth and distinguish crown formation, root growth and eruption.

Long-period increments do not represent one universal number of days. Their periodicity needs calibration against finer features. Knowing a crown took a certain time to form is not enough to know the individual’s age at death: one must also know when formation began and what later development is represented.

:::figure tooth

Traditional thin sections can expose growth features but require cutting a specimen. **Synchrotron microtomography** uses intense X-rays to inspect internal structure at fine resolution and can provide virtual sections. The resulting image still needs interpretation: preservation, resolution, growth landmarks and reconstruction affect the estimate.

The lecture’s methodological point is important. Dental development supplies a more direct timing constraint than simply assigning a juvenile the age of a modern child with a similar-looking skeleton. However, teeth, body growth and brain development need not all share one schedule.

### A mosaic of developmental histories

Juvenile australopith fossils help examine the relationship between relatively small brains and growth. The Nariokotome *Homo erectus* skeleton preserves much of a larger body, but estimates of its age and eventual adult size depend on which developmental model is used. A tall juvenile does not by itself establish a modern adolescent growth spurt.

<div class="update"><strong>Since the lecture · Early Homo development was not a simple binary.</strong> Zollikofer and colleagues (2024) examined a juvenile from Dmanisi, in a context about 1.8 million years old. Its dental history combined slow early development with later faster maturation. The result supports a mosaic of developmental changes, some predating major brain enlargement; it does not establish a fully modern childhood or the species’ complete social organisation. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08205-2">Read the study</a>.</div>

Neanderthal juveniles provide another useful comparison. Their large brains do not require that every developmental detail matched *Homo sapiens*. Dental estimates and endocranial shape can constrain different aspects of growth. An **endocast**—a cast or digital reconstruction of the interior of the braincase—records aspects of external brain shape, not neurons, synapses or a complete cognitive profile.

Work on the postnatal **globularization** of the modern human braincase examines shape changes rather than merely increasing volume. [Neubauer, Hublin and Gunz (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aao5961) also documented changes in *Homo sapiens* brain shape over evolutionary time. Distinguish shape, volume, chronology and proposed functional consequences.

:::question tooth

The lecture closes by considering mortality and older individuals in fossil samples. As in chapter 1, inferred age distributions depend on recovery and ageing methods. A fossil assemblage can contribute to a demographic argument, but its ratio of younger to older deaths is not a direct census of a living population.

### What survives the evidence?

The strongest account combines independent measurements. Brain energetics, dental formation and body growth each constrain a different part of childhood. A developmental history becomes more convincing when several proxies agree—and more interesting when they reveal a mosaic.
