A body’s constraints do not arise in a setting that remains unchanged. Organisms modify environments, and some modifications persist long enough to affect later generations. Humans combine this with learned practices, tools and extensive cooperation.

The final lecture brings the course’s questions together: how do bodily demands, environmental change and relationships between people influence one another? You will distinguish a plausible feedback from evidence that it actually occurred.

## Session 1 · Changing the conditions of life

### Niche construction is not uniquely human

Beaver dams alter water flow. Corals create structures that change habitats. Organisms can modify the distribution of nutrients, materials and other species. **Niche construction** refers to such modifications of an organism’s environment, including changes that can influence ecological and evolutionary processes.

An **ecological inheritance** consists of conditions left by previous generations: a modified landscape, structures or altered resources. Human **cultural inheritance** additionally transmits practices, information and skills. These forms of inheritance can interact with genetic inheritance without becoming the same mechanism.

Humans cut, burn, cultivate, build and move organisms. Some changes are deliberate; others are incidental consequences. A modification can benefit one group while harming another, or have a short-term benefit and a later cost. Niche construction does not mean foresight or inevitable improvement.

### Predation and altered landscapes

Hublin examines the ecological effects of human predation, including changes in animal communities and resource size. Selective harvesting can alter the distribution of sizes left in a population. But an archaeological change in shell size, for example, also requires checking species, habitat, climate and collection methods.

Late-Quaternary large-animal extinctions differ between regions. Human arrival, hunting pressure, climate and ecological interactions can contribute at different times and scales. A coincidence between arrival and extinction is a starting point; stronger tests compare chronologies, mechanisms and alternative expectations. [A global analysis by Lemoine and colleagues (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ancene.2023.100403) strengthened the case for a substantial human role, while a global association does not settle each local sequence.

Landscape burning can change vegetation and resource availability. Charcoal records may reflect natural as well as anthropogenic fire. To infer purposeful management, researchers combine the fire history with occupation, vegetation and other evidence rather than treating any charcoal as a human signature.

<div class="update"><strong>Since the lecture · A Neanderthal landscape case.</strong> Roebroeks and colleagues (2021) combined archaeological and environmental evidence at Neumark-Nord, Germany, to argue that Neanderthals helped maintain a relatively open landscape during the Last Interglacial. It is a contextual case for environmental modification, not proof that all Neanderthal landscapes were managed in the same way. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abj5567">Read the study</a>.</div>

### Agriculture changes more than food

Cultivation and animal husbandry alter productivity, species distributions and exposure to pathogens. Settlements can create habitats for commensal animals; moving organisms can spread both useful resources and disease. Changing a microenvironment can therefore generate new constraints alongside new opportunities.

The lecture discusses the **early anthropogenic hypothesis**, which proposes that prehistoric agriculture influenced greenhouse-gas trajectories before industrialisation. Its timing and magnitude need to be evaluated against natural climate processes, population estimates and land-use reconstructions. The secure general point is that agriculture changed landscapes and emissions; the stronger claim that it explains a particular departure in the global climate record remains a hypothesis to test.

## Session 2 · Culture changes selection

### A cultural practice and a genetic response have different dates

Milk use is a well-studied example of gene–culture relationships. Lactase breaks down lactose. In many mammals its activity declines after weaning; **lactase persistence** allows substantial activity into adulthood. Different human populations have variants associated with persistence, so the history is not one mutation spreading everywhere.

Keeping dairy animals creates access to milk. People can also ferment or process milk, changing its lactose content and uses. Cultural milk use can therefore precede high frequencies of lactase persistence. Inherited variation may subsequently have different consequences for survival and reproduction in the altered environment.

:::figure niche

<div class="update"><strong>Since the lecture · Milk use alone does not explain the genetic chronology.</strong> Evershed and colleagues (2022) combined prehistoric European dairy residues with genetic evidence. Milk use was widespread before lactase persistence became common, and its intensity alone did not explain their selection models. They proposed famine and pathogen exposure as important contexts. This is a supported model-based interpretation for the studied history, not a universal explanation for every region or variant. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-05010-7">Read the study</a>.</div>

:::question milk

Selection does not deliberately produce the needed mutation in response to milk. It changes the frequencies of inherited variants when their consequences differ under particular conditions. Archaeological practice, variant presence and allele-frequency change are three distinct observations.

### An experiment can change an evolutionary account

The original lecture presents a proposed difference between modern-human and Neanderthal **aryl hydrocarbon receptors**, which respond to compounds including some associated with smoke. An experimental difference in a receptor might suggest a physiological mechanism, but the cellular context and the compounds tested matter.

<div class="update"><strong>Since the lecture · The smoke-receptor interpretation was reassessed.</strong> Aarts and colleagues (2021) found similar responses of reconstructed Neanderthal and modern-human receptors to TCDD in human cells, unlike earlier large differences measured in another cellular setting. The course therefore does not treat a modern-human smoke-tolerance advantage as established. The later experiment also does not demonstrate equivalence for every smoke compound or whole-organism response. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msaa287">Read the study</a>.</div>

This example teaches a general rule: genetic sequence, molecular behaviour, organismal physiology and historical selection are different levels of inference. Connecting them requires evidence at each step.

## Session 3 · Brains in a social world

### Brain organisation is more than a layered size increase

Hublin returns to the brain’s organisation, including cortical development, genetic experiments and vascular proxies. The course keeps those questions while replacing the historical **triune brain** picture: brains did not evolve by simply adding a rational human shell over an emotional mammalian layer and a reptilian core. Vertebrate brains share homologous structures that have changed in multiple lineages, with extensive interaction between systems.

Comparative cell-type and circuit evidence provides a more useful framework than that stack. The **neocortex** has distinctive organisation in mammals, but cognition, emotion and regulation do not belong to three independent evolutionary compartments. [Tosches and colleagues (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar4237) used single-cell transcriptomics in reptiles to investigate the evolutionary relationships of cortical cell types.

The gene **ARHGAP11B** illustrates experimental work on cortical development. Studies have investigated effects on neural progenitors and cortical expansion. [Heide and colleagues (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abb2401) demonstrated effects on the developing marmoset neocortex. A developmental effect in an experimental organism supports a mechanism; it is not a complete account of hominin cognition or a precise date for an evolutionary transition.

Fossil carotid openings offer an anatomical proxy for vascular supply. [Seymour and colleagues (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160305) used their dimensions to model blood-flow changes, with a [2017 correction](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.170846). This involves assumptions relating bone openings, vessels and flow. It does not directly measure fossil synapses, thought or intelligence. Numerical changes inferred from that model should not be presented as direct physiological observations.

### Cooperation and cognition can influence one another

Human children develop in relationships with several people. Cooperation can make a demanding childhood viable, and social learning can improve the acquisition of skills and resources. The relationship may therefore involve feedback: care supports development, while cognition and learned practices change the possibilities for cooperation.

**Theory of mind** concerns representing the mental states of others. Empathy, communication, coordination and food sharing are related but distinct capacities. Non-human animals also exhibit important social and cognitive behaviours. Comparisons require tasks that measure the relevant ability, rather than declaring every difference uniquely human.

Other mammals can practise cooperative breeding without having human-like cognition. Provisioning alone is therefore not sufficient to explain the whole human social pattern. Human group organisation, cultural transmission, ecological demands and developmental plasticity need their own evidence.

### Social-brain hypotheses remain hypotheses

The **social-brain hypothesis** links cognitive investment to social complexity. Comparative associations between brain measurements and group characteristics motivate tests, but body size, ecology, evolutionary relatedness and the definition of a social group affect the analysis.

<div class="update"><strong>Since the lecture · No fixed 150-person ceiling.</strong> Lindenfors and colleagues (2021) reanalysed comparative extrapolations used for “Dunbar’s number” and found highly uncertain, method-dependent estimates. This challenges a precise universal biological cap; it does not refute every relationship between cognition and social life. Human networks also contain different kinds of relationships and institutions. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2021.0158">Read the study</a>.</div>

Hublin closes with the proposal that language can help maintain relationships in ways analogous to social grooming. It is an interesting functional hypothesis: communication can coordinate activity and reinforce affiliation. It does not establish one origin of language or reduce every conversation to grooming.

### Bring the six lectures together

The course began with an energy shortfall. Food webs constrain acquisition; the timing of life changes demand. A growing brain has its own energetic schedule. Processing changes what food can supply; movement changes the cost of reaching it. Heat exchange connects physiology to climate. Cooperation and technology alter who can meet those needs and in which environments.

Energy accounting identifies combinations that must be supported. Comparative and archaeological evidence asks which combinations existed. Evolutionary explanations then ask how they arose, while remaining open to competing causes.

### A final investigation

Imagine a synthetic archaeological case with three observations: local isotope comparisons suggest substantial animal protein; a juvenile tooth records slow early formation; hearth evidence is intermittent. One explanation proposes that processing and provisioning supported an extended childhood. Another emphasises other resources and preserves uncertainty about habitual fire use.

In your optional field note, identify what each observation establishes, which proposed link is still an inference, and the additional observation that would best distinguish the explanations. You could ask for better local dietary baselines, evidence of mechanical processing, preserved food residues, independently dated fire contexts or more juvenile growth records. Several requests can be defensible when their purpose is explained.

There is no numerical score for reproducing one story. A strong explanation connects independent evidence, respects its limits, and states what could change the conclusion.
