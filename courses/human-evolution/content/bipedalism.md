Walking can look ordinary because we do it without much conscious attention. Mechanically, it requires balancing a body over one supporting limb, redirecting its centre of mass and coordinating joints and muscles. The energetic consequences depend on anatomy, speed and distance.

In this chapter, connect the bipedal body to those mechanics, then examine fossils that preserve different combinations of traits. Keep three questions separate: what an animal could do, what doing it cost, and why the capacity evolved.

## Session 1 · An upright body

### Apes have several ways to move

Living apes climb, suspend themselves, move on the ground and sometimes walk bipedally. Occasional upright movement is not equivalent to habitual terrestrial bipedalism. Neither is the behaviour of a living chimpanzee a direct observation of the last common ancestor of chimpanzees and humans.

The ancestor’s locomotion must be inferred from comparative anatomy and fossils. A transition need not have been a single leap from modern knuckle-walking to modern human walking. Different hominins retained arboreal capacities while acquiring traits useful on the ground.

### A connected set of anatomical changes

The vertebral column brings the upper body’s mass into relation with the pelvis and supporting limbs. The pelvis helps support the viscera and changes the orientation and action of muscles. During single-leg support, muscles around the hip limit the tendency of the unsupported side to drop.

The femur’s orientation brings the knee closer to the body’s line of progression. Hip and knee extension allow a different support posture from an ape walking with more flexed joints. An extended limb can reduce the muscle force needed to support body weight under some conditions, but a fossil joint alone does not provide an entire movement sequence.

The human foot combines weight bearing, stability, elastic behaviour and propulsion. The big toe aligns with the other toes, while arches and the interactions of bones, ligaments and tendons change how forces are transmitted. A foot is neither a rigid block nor a miniature hand; its behaviour changes through the stance phase.

Habitual terrestrial bipedalism also reduces the hands’ role in supporting the body during locomotion. Human hand proportions and coordination enable precise and forceful grips. That relationship does not prove that making a particular tool caused the first bipedal adaptation. Several traits and behaviours changed at different times.

### Walking and running use different mechanics

Walking can exchange gravitational potential and kinetic energy as the body moves over the supporting limb, broadly resembling an **inverted pendulum**. Redirecting the centre of mass between steps still requires work. Elastic tissues and muscles also contribute.

Running has a more spring-like pattern: tissues store and return some energy as the body lands and leaves the ground. The Achilles tendon, foot structures and other tissues can contribute. Elastic return reduces some active work, but muscles still support, stabilise and accelerate the body. Neither mechanism makes movement free.

## Session 2 · What a kilometre costs

### Power, time and distance

Power measures expenditure per unit time. **Cost of transport** measures energy per unit distance, often normalised by body mass. A fast activity may use more power while completing the journey sooner. Therefore a higher wattage need not imply a proportionally higher cost per kilometre.

For a given cost of transport C, body mass M and distance D, movement energy is C × M × D. If C is expressed in joules per kilogram per metre, multiplying by speed in metres per second gives power per kilogram. Units expose the connection.

Resting expenditure must be treated consistently. **Gross** cost includes expenditure that would occur at rest; **net** cost subtracts that baseline. A study using gross energy per distance and another using net locomotor power cannot be compared without accounting for their definitions.

Walking cost per distance typically has a minimum over a range of speeds. Very slow or fast walking can be less economical. Running cost per distance can be relatively less sensitive to speed over a useful range, while running power rises with speed. Terrain, gradients, load, leg length, gait and individual variation change these relationships.

### Endurance and the range of a forager

Humans combine capacities for distance travel with substantial sweating and heat dissipation. These capacities can contribute to **persistence hunting**, in which tracking and repeated pursuit fatigue prey. Hublin uses this example to connect running anatomy, food acquisition and thermoregulation.

Observing a successful hunt establishes that a behaviour is possible. Establishing its importance in evolution requires questions about frequency, return, season, climate, tracking skill and archaeological support. Running could also matter for scavenging, reaching resources or other tasks. Anatomical capacities can serve more than one function.

## Session 3 · Several histories of bipedalism

### What actually survives?

Anatomical inference begins with the preserved material. A femur, pelvis, foot or skull each constrains different things. Distortion and reconstruction can affect the measurements. A fragmentary skeleton should not acquire a complete modern gait simply because a species is described as bipedal.

The position of the **foramen magnum**, where the spinal cord passes through the skull, contributes to interpretations of head posture. It is one line of evidence, not a whole locomotor diagnosis. Limb anatomy provides additional constraints.

:::figure fossils

*Sahelanthropus*, from Chad, and *Orrorin*, from Kenya, contribute to debates about the earliest hominin locomotion. The available samples are limited, and attribution and reconstruction matter. [Daver and colleagues (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04901-z) interpreted limb remains attributed to *Sahelanthropus* as evidence of bipedalism alongside arboreal behaviour, adding postcranial evidence after the lecture.

*Ardipithecus ramidus* preserves a combination of pelvic features associated with terrestrial bipedality and a grasping foot. Its reconstructed movement need not resemble either modern chimpanzee locomotion or modern human walking. The lecture also emphasises disagreement about how securely particular fossils can be placed in our lineage. A useful functional trait can evolve in a collateral lineage as well as a direct ancestor.

### Australopiths and footprints

Lucy and other *Australopithecus afarensis* remains provide several lines of anatomical evidence for habitual bipedalism. Yet climbing-related traits and body proportions complicate a simple miniature-modern-human reconstruction. Different australopith species also need not have had identical locomotion.

The **Laetoli footprints**, about 3.66 million years old, preserve an episode of movement across a substrate. They can inform foot placement and gait, but interpretation depends on the surface, deformation and comparison. Assigning the makers to a particular species uses surrounding evidence; a footprint rarely contains an independent species label.

Leg length and body size affect the dynamics of walking. A shorter-limbed individual may use a different speed or step frequency at a comparable mechanical state. A cost reconstructed for Lucy is conditional on the model’s mass, geometry and muscle assumptions. It is not a direct metabolic measurement.

### Homo was also varied

Some early *Homo* remains retain combinations of traits also seen in australopiths. Later skeletons, including Nariokotome, document a different set of proportions, with longer lower limbs. Footprints near Ileret provide another line of evidence for early Pleistocene foot function, while the makers’ attribution and reconstructed behaviour remain inferential.

The fossil sequence does not describe a single ladder with every named species as an ancestor. Hominin evolution involved branching lineages, different combinations of traits and uncertain relationships. Habitual bipedality preceded the very large brains of later *Homo*; explaining the first cannot require a trait that arose much later.

### A capacity is part of an explanation

Economical locomotion can reduce the energetic cost of reaching resources. Running and ranging can widen the set of available tasks. Those mechanisms connect this chapter to diet, but anatomy and present-day measurements need archaeological and ecological evidence before they become a historical explanation.

In the next chapter, a further constraint appears: much of the energy spent moving becomes heat that must go somewhere.
